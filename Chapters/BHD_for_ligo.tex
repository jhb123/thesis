\section{BHD versus DC readout for A+}
Different gravitational wave signals carry their information in different frequency bands, and the detector may be optimised to to reflect this. For instance, BNS signals continue to higher frequencies than BBH signals (due to them being smaller) so it may be of interest to optimise the detector around these frequencies to improve the understanding of the properties of BNS.  The current generation of gravitational wave detectors are going to be limited by a combination of of radiation pressure noise and shot noise in their detection bands. These two noise sources are intrinsically linked by the Heisenberg uncertainty principle, and can both be understood as arising due to the number of photons in used to probe the position of the end test masses. This limit in the noise floor is known as the standard quantum limit. A squeezed light field can be used to surpass the noise floor set by an squeezed state. The exact way in which the quantum noise is changed is controlled by the squeezing angle, and to detect these squeezed states the homodyne angle must be correctly set. The effect that controlling the squeezing angle has on the SQL has been demonstrated for at LLO \cite{Yu2020}, however this used a diagnostic BHD set-up which was not intended for providing the low noise required to make gravitational wave detections.
\paragraph{}
In addition to being key in shaping the noise curve of the detector, BHD has some practical benefits over DC readout. These benefits revolve around being able to eliminate the DC light required for DC readout to work. The primary benefit is in the reduction in SRC length noise coupling to the gravitational wave signal, and another benefit is being able to reduce the scattering from the septum window between HAM5 and HAM6. While strictly speaking these are not properties of BHD readout, as both could be achieved by other means. For instance, the septum window could be removed and  suspensions with additional stages could be used. These solutions however are not as practical as they sound, and ar not more practical than implemented BHD. The septum window is key to being able to commission the interferometer. The number of times HAM5 and the other chambers connected are vented should be kept low, as each time this is done gas and water vapour enters the system and this will not be removed as this is a large engineering challenge. The design and install of three new suspensions is also a large design task, which ultimately gives less benefit to having a BHD readout scheme.
\paragraph{}
DC readout produces a field due to a constant differential offset in the arm lengths. The field due to the differential offset is the local oscillator in DC readout. The local oscillator beats with a field created by a gravitational wave in order to produce a measurable signal. This method of readout has some key advantages, such as there being no phase noise between the local oscillator and the signal carrying field, and the local oscillator and signal being perfectly aligned and mode matched. The BHD system will need to provide sufficiently low phase noise as to not degrade the GW signal. The control over the alignment and mode matching between the LO and the signal is also important, as this will cause light to be scattered back into the interferometer from the OMCs. The new layout for the BHD readout will have to provided these features.
\paragraph{}
As the BHD signal is obtained through subtracting the signals from two photodiodes, it is possible to obtain a very low electronic noise floor when designing the transimpedance amplifier by subtracting the currents produced by the photo-diodes rather than having two separate transimpedance amplifiers and subtracting the signals made by these later. This is not really an advantage, as low enough electronic noise can be obtained using other construction techniques that allow for small DC signals while still having signal shaping that gives a large enough SNR in the detection band. Having separate transimpedance amplifiers for each photodiode also has useful diagnostic advantages, such as being able to see the sum of the two photo-diode signals. Therefore, direct subtraction of the photo-diode signals should not be considered an advantage of BHD over DC readout.
\paragraph{}
One of the practical outcomes of implementing BHD is the potential to reduce SRC length noise coupling. Noise on the length of the SRC can couple to the gravitational wave signal via two routes. The dominant coupling will arise due to the power entering the SRC, which is proportional to the DC offset. The other coupling arises due the optical spring between the SRC and the arm cavity length, whose strength depends on the DC offset.
\paragraph{}
The dominant coupling due to the power entering the SRC can be intuitively understood by considering a Fabry-Perot cavity. For small changes in the cavity length, the light transmitted by the cavity way that length noise may couple to the signal light is due to the dependence on the transmitted light by the SRC on its length and the amount of light entering the cavity. There is a relationship between the length noise and the power transmitted that scales linearly with the DC offset. 
\paragraph{}
The second route that SRC length noise may couple to the the signal is by the optomechanical coupling due to an the optical springs between the SRC and the arm cavity. Due to this optical spring effect, a change in length of the SRC will cause the arm cavity length to change as well\cite{Izumi2016}. This effect is much smaller than the first effect, so the estimate for the reduction of SRC length coupling will only considering the first effect.
\paragraph{}
SRC length noise is currently too high for LIGO to meet the A+ requirements \href{https://alog.ligo-wa.caltech.edu/aLOG/index.php?callRep=55755}{Shelia Dwyer post}. To estimate the reduction in SRC length noise that BHD may provided, the amount the DC offset that is currently used should be compared to the amount that is proposed for BHD. LIGO currently uses at the AS port there is 23\,mW, with 1\,mW of residual light (light that cannot be removed)\textcolor{red}{(shelia dwyer answer in email FW: BHD review committee request \& document PREVIEW). Assume 0.85 A/W (qe = 99\%)}. The AS power for BHD is between 1 and 5\,mW. Therefore, a conservative estimate would for the factor that the SRC length noise coupling is reduced by is 4.
\section{Local Oscillator Path Length Stability Requirement and HRTS requirement}
The requirement for the path length stability of the local oscillator is determined by the sensitivity goal for A+. Deconvolving the strain sensitivity curve for A+ with the linear response function between differential strain, $h$, and power at the AS port gives the noise of the AS beam. This is then converted to an equivalent phase noise between the AS the LO beams. Finally, the requirement for the suspended optics is derived.
\paragraph{}
A gravitational wave produces a phase modulated signal in the AS beam. The LO beam will also be phase modulated by the motion of the mirrors in the LO path. When these beams are combined, it is the phase difference between the two fields that produces a signal, so the phase noise of the LO path must be sufficiently low that the signal that is generated by the interference of the LO and AS beams corresponds to the phase modulation of the AS beam. 
\subsection{Local Oscillator Path}
Two options for the local oscillator path were considered: a beam picked off from the main beam splitters AR coating (BSAR) or the beam transmitted through PR2 \cite{Zhang2019} (POP). The determining factor in choice of LO path was the effect of backscatter, and the POP choice is far superior to the BSAR choice at all frequencies and scatter phases. This is because any backscatter from the BSAR enters only one arm, and therefore is a differential effect. Any back scatter that enters the POP port is common-mode, meaning that the differential arm signal is far less sensitive to this source of backscatter.

To apply a differential arm signal with \textsc{Finesse}, \verb|fsig| commands were used to apply two signals in antiphase with each other to the ETMs,

fsig sig2 ETMYHR 1 180.
\end{verbatim}
The frequency of this modulation was swept, and the signal produced at the AS port was measured by demodulating the light at the modulation frequency. The commands
\begin{verbatim}
xaxis sig1 f log 5 5000 2999
put P_DC_OMC_probe f1 $x1
\end{verbatim}
do this. Since the input modulation is doubled by moving the mirrors differentially the response needs dividing by 2. To account for the ETMs retro-reflecting the light (\textcolor{red}{or is the weird Finesse convention}), the response needs dividing by 2 again. \subsection{Converting the Sensitivity Curve for A+ into the HRTS requirement}
\textcolor{red}{The sensitivity curve will be shown in an earlier chapter.}To convert the A+ sensitivity curve into a path length stability requirement for the local oscillator, a few steps are required. First, the sensitivity curve must be expressed in units of $\frac{\rm{W}}{\sqrt{\rm{Hz}}}$. Secondly, the intensity noise of the AS beam needs to be converted into a phase noise. In order for the phase noise of the local oscillator to not limit the performance of the gravitational wave detector, the phase noise of the AS beam must be greater than the phase noise of the LO beam. Finally, a factor of 10 safety margin is required. The HRTS requirement is then obtained by including the number of reflective optics in the LO path.
\paragraph{}
The A+ sensitivity\cite{a+curve} curve was multiplied by the function shown in \ref{fig:bhdforligochapterdetectorrepsonse} to convert it from units of $\frac{\rm{h}}{\sqrt{\rm{Hz}}}$ to $\frac{\rm{W}}{\sqrt{\rm{Hz}}}$. This is shown in figure \ref{fig:bhdforligochapterlightnoise}. At frequencies above 1\,kHz, the requirement is flat as this is the region dominated by shot noise. At lower frequencies, the requirement is relaxed as the sensitivity curve becomes dominated by other noise sources.

\paragraph{}
The quantum mechanics of this problem have been included in the A+ sensitivity curve \cite{a+curve}, therefore we can approach this with classical physics. When the LO and AS beams interfere at the beam splitter (BHSBS2), the power of the resulting beam is 
\begin{equation}
P = |E_s e^{i\omega t} + E_{\rm{LO}} e^{i\omega t + \phi}  |^2 = |E_s|^2 + |E_{\rm{LO}}|^2 + 2|E_sE^\dagger_{\rm{LO}}|\cos(\phi), 
\end{equation}
where $E_s$ and $E_{\rm{LO}}$ are the (complex) field amplitude of the signal and local oscillator beams, $\omega$ is the laser frequency, $t$ is time, and $\phi$ is the phase difference between the local oscillator and the signal beam, also known as the homodyne angle. Differentiating with respect to $\phi$ gives the relationship between a change in power and a change in phase between the two beams,
\begin{equation}
dP = -2|E_sE^\dagger_{\rm{LO}}|\sin(\phi) d\phi.
\end{equation}
and since the homodyne angle is 90\si{\degree}, this becomes
\begin{equation}
dP = -2|E_sE^\dagger_{\rm{LO}}|d\phi.
\end{equation}
To convert $\phi$ to distance, use
\begin{equation}
\frac{\lambda}{2\pi}d\phi = dx.
\end{equation}
The light noise curve shown in figure \ref{fig:bhdforligochapterlightnoise} should be multiplied by  $\frac{\lambda}{2\pi}\frac{1}{2|E_sE^\dagger_{\rm{sig}}|}$ to obtain the the noise floor of the interferometer in units of LO path length noise. For a 98\,mW LO beam and a 5\,mW AS beam, the noise floor of the interferometer in units of LO path length is shown in figure \ref{fig:bhdforligochapterhrtsrequirement}.
\paragraph{}
The HRTS requirement assumes, based on the current design, that there will be 2 mirrors at 45\si{\degree}  which are used to steer the AS and LO beams. As the optics reflected at 45 degrees, the path length change due to the optics motion is multiplied by$\frac{2}{\sqrt{2}} = \sqrt{2}$. The beams are combined on a beamsplitter, and the notion of this beam splitter needs to be counted twice. A sketch of this is shown in figure \ref{fig:bhdforligochapterbhddoublecounted}. A factor of 10 is then included as a safety margin since there are many uncorrelated noise sources in LIGO. With a 98\,mW LO beam and a 5\,mW AS beam, the requirement for the HRTS optics longitudinal motion at 100\,Hz is $5\times10^{-17} \rm{m}/\sqrt{\rm{Hz}}$.
A typical motion of the optical benches at 10\,Hz is $10\times10^{-1}\rm{m}/\sqrt{\rm{Hz}}$ \cite{HAM_table_motion}. To achieve this with passive isolation, a triple suspensions will be used.

\section{HAM6 layout}
Fundamentally, the layout in HAM6 must combine the AS beam and the LO beam, and sense the combined beams to form a balanced homodyne detector. There are several other functionalities required and constraints imposed on the layout of HAM6. The following section will describe these functionalities and constraints and how they guided the design of the HAM6 layout. This work was undertaken by a team including myself, Ken Strain, Russel Jones, Stephen Webster and Mark Barton. 
\subsection{The Constraints and Requirements of the Layout of HAM6}
The entry point of the AS and LO beams in principle can be anywhere in HAM6, however in reality they are fixed due to the location of HAM6 with respect to the interferometer, due to the location of the septum windows between HAM5 and HAM6, and due to the many other components of the interferometer that these beams need to avoid. The LO beam is steered with BHDM1 onto BHDBS2, and the AS beam is steered with OM0 onto BHDBS2. This is required to provide the degrees of freedom to co-align the AS and LO beams. There is some freedom in choice for the location of OM0 and BHDBS2, but there is no choice in the location of the AS and LO beams.
\paragraph{}
The design beam parameter at the SRM is $q_{\rm{SRM}} = -3.55 + 1.09i\,\rm{m} $, so the design of the HAM6 mode matching optics is based on this number. In reality, the beam parameter could be different to this, as discussed in chapter \ref{chapter:simulation_finesse}). At the SRM, there are two sets of modes we could design the AWC to match to: The arm mode, or the SRC mode. Ideally, these would be the same mode, however geometry errors in the SRC cause the arm and the SRC to be mismatched meaning the radius of curvature and beam size at the SRM will not correspond to the eigenmode of the SRM. This set of likely modes, shown in \ref{fig:sim_chapter_beam_parameter_at_SRM}, was taken into consideration when choosing a layout for the AWC in HAM6.
\paragraph{}
After BHDBS2, the layout the optics between OMC input couplers and BHDBS2 must provide the functionality for steering the combined AS and LO beam into the OMC, and provide mode matching for the AS beam to the OMC. In addition to this, sensors in HAM6 are required for targeting the AS and LO beams. Some of these functions will be achieved using beams which are picked off from the main beam path, and so additional mirrors will be required to steer these beams around the chamber. A set of smaller optics will be installed to provide diagnostic beams which can be imaged with CCDs. A shutter will be required to protect the hardware in HAM6 from being damaged by the large amounts of light that can potentially be transmitted through the SRM.
\paragraph{}
After the AS and LO beams are combined, double stage suspensions will guide the beam from BHDBS2 to the OMC. These suspensions provide active positioning control and active wave front control. In principle, the role of the AWC in HAM6 is to correct for geometry errors of the optics after the SRM such that the mode matching between the Arm mode and the OMC waist is maximised. This assumes that the changes in the interferometers mode, for instance due to a change in thermal state, will be corrected for by other subsystems e.g. the TCS. In practice, operators of the interferometer do what ever improves the SNR of the the gravitational wave signal. For instance, it has been found that TCS can be used to decrease laser intensity noise coupling to the the GW signal channel \textcolor{red}{CHECK}. The AWC in HAM6 was designed with this flexibility in mind. The losses due to components in HAM6 is summarised in table \ref{tab:HAM6_losses} (See \href{https://dcc.ligo.org/E1900385}{E1900385}).
\paragraph{}
To provide orthogonal alignment control in each beam path after BHDBS2, two of the mirrors are required to be separated by $90\si{\degree}$ of Gouy phase. Additionally, to provide orthogonal mode matching control, two of the mirrors should be separated by $45\si{\degree}$. The Gouy phase separation requirements give us a starting point for designing layouts, as these can be interpreted as needing the mode matching mirrors to be as far apart as possible from each other on the HAM table. As the Rayleigh range of the beams is on the order of a meter, and most gouy phase is accumulated within one Rayleigh range either side of a beam waist, a beam will have to travel quite far across the table before it acquires sufficient Gouy phase. While in principle only two curved mirrors would be required to mode match the beam from the interferometer to the OMC, in fact a single mirror may be able to do that, a lens in the OFI will be used to provide further Gouy phase separation between the two active mode matching mirrors after BHDBS2.
\paragraph{}
The goal for mode matching between the arm mode and the OMC mode is is $>98\%$.  The SAMS actuators will provide $\pm100$,\,mD of optical power tuning range, with a bandwidth of the order 100\,Hz. Astigmatism can be considered as a loss, and none-zero angles of incidence (AOI) of beams onto curved optics results in a the beam becoming astigmatic. This prevents designs using large AOI. This is described in detail in Chapter \ref{chapter:light_beams}.
\paragraph{}
The current OMC design (with trivial modifications) will be used to remove junk light from the signal beam, so their is no choice in target waist size. The input beam at the SRM is defined in \href{https://dcc.ligo.org/LIGO-T1200410}{T1200410}. The mode matching is designed assuming that the beam at the SRM is the `design mode'. Mode matching between the beam exiting the SRM and the OMC waist will be done with a static lens and 2 active mirrors. The static lens will be placed just after the OFI and its purpose is to provide sufficient Gouy phase separation of the 2 active mirrors. 
\paragraph{}
The light transmitted by the OMCs is used to measure the gravitational wave signal, since the higher order modes are suppressed by a factor of the finesse of the OMC. The OMCs are expected to be the largest contributor of back-scatter from HAM6 back to the interferometer. By having the OMCs after the AS and LO beams are combined, and by having the OFI protect the interferometer, the requirement on the motion of the OMCs is fairly lax. Nevertheless, a single stage suspended platform is required. This will provide isolation down to 4\,Hz.  
\paragraph{}
It was decided that the two beams after BHDBS2 would have the same path length to reduce the cost of the HAM6 optics. This may also improve the common-mode noise rejection ratio.

\section{Sensing and Control}
\subsection{Longitudinal Control}
\subsubsection{Homodyne Angle}
One method of controlling the homodyne angle is using the so called `RF 36` signal. LIGO uses 9\,MHz and 45\,MHz modulations on the light to detect various lengths within the interferometer that need to be controlled. The beat between these two will produce a 36,MHz signal. The homodyne angle determines the amplitude of this signal, and so by demodulating a photodiode measuring the light in reflection from the OMC at 36\,MHz the homodyne angle may be controlled. This is detailed in (Tengs Thesis ref). The actuation may be provided by OM0, BHDBS2 or by BHDM1. Alternatively, a dither signal at a few kHz on OM0 could provide can provide a signal corresponding to the homodyne angle. \textcolor{red}{Cant the homodyne angle be set to 90\si{\degree} by subtracting the photodiode signals and requiring the DC to be zero, as in a Mach zehnder?}
\subsubsection{OMC Length}
Keeping the OMC length locked to the combined LO-AS beam can be done by actuating on the length of PZT mirrors in the OMC. A few kHz signal may be created by the modulation on a HRTS in either the LO or AS path, one of the HDS after BHDBS2, or event a dither on the other PZT within the OMC. These modulations will be measurable in transmission of the OMC. Alternatively, a PDH signal may be created by using the 9\,MHz signal may be measured in reflection from the OMC.
\subsubsection{Squeezing Angle to Measurement Phase}
The squeezing and measurement phase may be controlled by measuring the 3,MHz signal transmitted through the OMC. This signal originates in the AOM in the squeezer, and the squeezing angle can be controoled with the PZT in the the squeezer layout in HAM7.
\subsection{Alignment Control}
\subsubsection{LO and AS alignment}
The alignment of the signal to the to the AS beam will be done with either OM0 or BHDM1, and BHDBS2. A 36\,MHz signal measured in reflection of the OMC can be used to determine the overlap of the two beams. By having a Gouy Phase telescope in reflection of the the OMC, a signal proportional to displacement and angle may be obtained. Alternatively, a dither at a few Hz can be applied to BHDM1 and BHDBS2, and this signal will appear in the light transmitted by the OMC.
\subsection{Alignment of the LO-AS beam to the OMC}
A dither applied to a HRTS in either the LO or AS path will produce a signal in transmission through the OMC. The mirrors OMxS and OMx2 will be used to align the LO beam into the OMC, and as these are separated by $\sim90\si{\degree}$ of Gouy phase, orthogonal control of the input beam alignment will be achievable. 
\subsubsection{Squeezer to AS beam}
The pick-off at OM0 will be used to align the squeezer beam to the AS beam. The squeezer light has 42\,MHz sidebands, and so a pair of wavefront sensors separated by $\sim90\si{\degree}$ of Gouy phase can be demodulated at this frequency to derive an alignment error signal. The squeezer beam will be aligned to the AS beam with optics between the filter cavity and the OFI. The signal and squeezer beam will be aligned onto these wavefront sensors using SR2 and a tip tilt in transmission of OM0.
\subsubsection{Differential Hard Mode}
The differential hard mode arises due to radiation pressure effects in the arm cavities. As the power in the cavity changes, the dynamics of the test mass changes. These dynamics result from the alignment of the cavity axis to the test masses due radiation pressure. This differential hard mode dynamic must be controlled. This signal will be detected with light that is transmitted through OM0, and actuated on with the test masses.
\section{WS visulations as a tool for understanding mode matching problems}
\textcolor{red}{Hermite Gaussian beams will be explained earlier in the intro chapters, so don't need generic expansion, just use the 2nd order ones. Have a read of \cite{Mueller2000}}.
\paragraph{}
The mode-matching between two laser beam modes, or a laser mode and a cavity mode, describes how similar those two modes are. In general, the two modes can be mismatched by being misaligned or by having a different beam shape. Mode matching typically refers to the overlap of the two modes if they were perfectly aligned.
\paragraph{}
Mode-matching is usually, to first approximation, thought of as a difference in beam waist size and beam size location. This can be mathematically described by the $\rm{HG}_{20} + \rm{HG}_{02}$ mode. The effect of changing the waist location of the mode is equivalent to an in-phase change in HG20 content, whereas an change in waist location is orthogonal to this. \textcolor{red}{read up on this}. Sensing the $\rm{HG}_{20} + \rm{HG}_{02}$ mode therefore gives a measure of the mode-matching between two lasers. As the $\rm{HG}_{20} + \rm{HG}_{02}$ acquires $3\phi$ compared to a plane wave, and $2\phi$ compared to the $\rm{HG}_{00}$ mode, spacing the actuators at $45\si{\degree}$ will give orthogonal control of the mode matching. This means that the beam size and beam waist can be independently controlled.
\paragraph{}
A curved mirror can only change the curvature of a Gaussian beam, meaning that at the mirrors surface the beams before and after reflection must have the same size. The process of a Gaussian beam described by $q_1$ being reflected from a mirror with power $S_{\rm{m}}$ to produce a new Gaussian beam, $q_2$, can be written as
\begin{equation}
\frac{1}{q_2} = S_{\rm{m}} + \frac{1}{q_1}   = (S_{\rm{m}}+S_1) - i\frac{\lambda}{\pi w_1^2}.
\end{equation}
\paragraph{}
To visualise how a mode is changed by an optical layout, beam parameters can be represented in a 'WS space'. This space parametrises a beam in terms of its size, $w$, and it defocus, $S$.. This has the advantage of showing the beam in terms of practical quantities. The downside to this representation is as the beam propogates through the layout, the path through the WS space is fairly complex. There are two other ways that a beam parameter could be graphically represented. As $q$ is a complex number, the beam parameter could be represented in terms of its real and imaginary components. This makes seeing the effect of propagation straight forward, but the effect of a focussing element is harder to visualise. Alternatively, plotting $1/q$ in terms of $\lambda/\pi w^2$ and $s$ produces plots where the effect of a focussing elements and propagation is easy to visualise, as focussing is just a change in defocus and propagation follows a circular path. However, this technique obscures the beam size, and as these graphs are being used to visualise the beam, rather than design the optical layout, the WS representation was chosen.
\paragraph{}
To demonstrate how orthogonal actuators can be represented in WS space, consider the two lenses with variable power separated by 45\si{\degree} of Gouy phase. As the two lenses are orthogonal, the waist size and waist location of the mode exiting this layout will orthogonally controllable. In a WS space, the orthogonality of two actuators can be seen by the angle between the two vectors that represent the actuation. If the two lenses are orthogonal, then the actuation vectors will be orthogonal. Figure \ref{fig:bhdforligochapterwssketch} is a sketch of the WS space representation for a simple optical layout consisting of two active lenses separated by 45\si{\degree} Gouy phase. If there is large amounts of astigmatism, it no longer makes sense to represent the beam with just one WS space diagram.
\paragraph{}
Two perfectly mode matched beams will have identical beam parameters. If the input mode and the target mode differ in beam size and defocus, then some light will be scattered into the $\rm{HG}_{20} + rm{HG}_{02}$ mode. The mode matching can be calculated at any point along the optical axis of the layout, as the mode matching must be preserved, but it makes sense to choose a physically significant plane such as the input plane or the plane containing the waist of the target beam. The amount of mode matching $\mathcal{M}$ between two astigmatic modes modes, $q_{1,2}$, is
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{M} =\frac{\sqrt{\left(q_{x1}-q_{x1}^*\right)\left(q_{y1}-q_{y1}^*\right)\left(q_{x2}-q_{x2}^*\right)\left(q_{y2}-q_{y2}^*\right)}}{\left|q_{x2} - q_{x1}^*\right|\left|q_{y2} - q_{y1}^*\right|}.
\end{equation}
It is useful to plot contours of the mode overlap in the WS space between a mode and a target mode. Note that these contours can only tell you about the overlap between a mode and the target mode, the mode matching between two arbitrary modes can not be deduced from these contours. An example of a situation where this is obvious is two different modes may be, say, $90\%$ mode matched to the target. As they are different from each other, they cannot be $100\%$ mode matched.

The mode matching requirement for LIGO is $>98\%$. To visualise the effectiveness of the actuation provided by the active elements of the HAM6 layout, the region of the WS from which the target mode matching space achieved was computed. To do this, first the ring of modes in WS space that is 98\% matched after reflection from the second active mirror, OMx2, was considered. These modes must be within $S_{\rm{OMx2}}\pm\Delta S$, where $S_{\rm{OMx2}}$ is the static power of OMx2 and $\Delta S$ is the actuation range of the SAMS. If OMx2 had no actuation, then the effect of OMx2 would be to move the ring vertically in the WS plane. However, as power of OMx2 can be increased or decreased, the shape of the ring of modes that can be mode matched to $98\%$ can be changed. Crudely, the shape of this space can be seen by applying $S_{\rm{OMx2}}\pm\Delta S$ to the $98\%$ ring, producing 2 new rings. If the ring is cut by a line which intersects the locations on the ring at the extrema of the beam size, then two regions of the $98\%$ ring can be created. For one of these regions, to maximise the amount of WS space covered by the actuator, the actuation applied should be $+\Delta S$, whereas for the other region, the actuation should be $-\Delta S$. This gives a set of modes which can be propagated to the first mirror, OMx1, where the same procedure can be applied. This process shown in Figure \ref{fig:bhdforligochapterlayout5fomx1omx2planes}.

\section{Conclusion about BHD for A+}
One of the major changes to LIGO for A+ will be the implementation of BHD. Combined with frequency dependent squeezing, BHD offers the possibility to optimise the detector for different gravitational wave sources. Along with this, BHD offers a way by which the coupling between the signal recycling cavity length noise and DARM can be reduced.
\paragraph{}
BHD presents some technical challenges over DC readout. The LO phase noise must be kept low enough as to not contaminate the signal at the AS port with LO path noise. The requirement for the phase noise is shown in figure \ref{fig:bhdforligochapterhrtsrequirement}. This requirement can be met by using triple suspensions to deliver to local oscillator and AS beams to the beam splitter they are combined on. 
\paragraph{}
The functionality required from the BHD layout was discussed, and a proposed layout that satisfies these requirements is shown in figure \ref{fig:bhdforligochapterlayout5f}. The optical properties of this layout were investigated, and by selection of the right combination of mode matching optics, near orthogonal control of the AS-LO beam alignment and mode matching to the OMC was achieved.
\paragraph{}
By plotting the $<98\%$ mode matching requirement in WS space, the robustness to changes in the input beam parameter was investigated. The AWC provides a huge amount of mode matching range that the layout can tolerate very large changes in parameter at the SRM. It was found that almost all possible beams will be able to mode matched to the OMC. This is shown in figure \ref{fig:bhdforligochapterlayout5fmodessrmomcmodematchingsuccess}.
