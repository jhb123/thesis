\section{Local Oscillator Path Length Stability Requirement and HRTS requirement}
The requirement for the path length stability of the local oscillator is determined by the sensitivity goal for A+. Deconvolving the strain sensitivity curve for A+ with the linear response function between differential strain, $h$, and power at the AS port gives the noise of the AS beam. This is then converted to an equivalent phase noise between the AS the LO beams. Finally, the requirement for the suspended optics is derived.
\paragraph{}
A gravitational wave produces a phase modulated signal in the AS beam. The LO beam will also be phase modulated by the motion of the mirrors in the LO path. When these beams are combined, it is the phase difference between the two fields that produces a signal, so the phase noise of the LO path must be sufficiently low that the signal that is generated by the interference of the LO and AS beams corresponds to the phase modulation of the AS beam. 
\subsection{Local Oscillator Path}
Two options for the local oscillator path were considered: a beam picked off from the main beam splitters AR coating (BSAR) or the beam transmitted through PR2 \cite{Zhang2019} (POP). The determining factor in choice of LO path was the effect of backscatter, and the POP choice is far superior to the BSAR choice at all frequencies and scatter phases. This is because any backscatter from the BSAR enters only one arm, and therefore is a differential effect. Any back scatter that enters the POP port is common-mode, meaning that the differential arm signal is far less sensitive to this source of backscatter.

\subsection{Simulation of the Linear Response Function Between a Differential Strain and the Power at the AS Port}
The linear response function of the A+ detectors was obtained using \textsc{Finesse}. Since the core optics of the interferometer will not be changing in a way relevant to this calculation, the LLO model\cite{l1_finesse_2014} was used to calculate the response between differential motion of the ETMs and the power transmitted through the OMC. To apply a differential arm signal with \textsc{Finesse}, \verb|fsig| commands were used to apply two signals in antiphase with each other to the ETMs,
\begin{verbatim}
fsig sig1 ETMXHR 1 0
fsig sig2 ETMYHR 1 180.
\end{verbatim}
The frequency of this modulation was swept, and the signal produced at the AS port was measured by demodulating the light at the modulation frequency. The commands
\begin{verbatim}
xaxis sig1 f log 5 5000 2999
put P_DC_OMC_probe f1 $x1
\end{verbatim}
do this. Since the input modulation is doubled by moving the mirrors differentially the response needs dividing by 2. To account for the ETMs retro-reflecting the light (\textcolor{red}{or is the weird Finesse convention}), the response needs dividing by 2 again. To convert this from phase units to strain units, a factor of $\frac{\lambda}{2\pi L_{\rm{arm}}}$ is needed. Therefore, the output of the the \textsc{Finesse} simulation needs to be multiplied by $\frac{1}{2}\frac{1}{2}\frac{2\pi L_{\rm{arm}}}{\lambda}$ to be in units of $\frac{\rm{W}}{\rm{h}}$. \subsection{Converting the Sensitivity Curve for A+ into the HRTS requirement}
\textcolor{red}{The sensitivity curve will be shown in an earlier chapter.}To convert the A+ sensitivity curve into a path length stability requirement for the local oscillator, a few steps are required. First, the sensitivity curve must be expressed in units of $\frac{\rm{W}}{\sqrt{\rm{Hz}}}$. Secondly, the intensity noise of the AS beam needs to be converted into a phase noise. In order for the phase noise of the local oscillator to not limit the performance of the gravitational wave detector, the phase noise of the AS beam must be greater than the phase noise of the LO beam. Finally, a factor of 10 safety margin is required. The HRTS requirement is then obtained by including the number of reflective optics in the LO path.
\paragraph{}
The A+ sensitivity\cite{a+curve} curve was multiplied by the function shown in \ref{fig:bhdforligochapterdetectorrepsonse} to convert it from units of $\frac{\rm{h}}{\sqrt{\rm{Hz}}}$ to $\frac{\rm{W}}{\sqrt{\rm{Hz}}}$. This is shown in figure \ref{fig:bhdforligochapterlightnoise}. At frequencies above 1\,kHz, the requirement is flat as this is the region dominated by shot noise. At lower frequencies, the requirement is relaxed as the sensitivity curve becomes dominated by other noise sources.

\paragraph{}
The quantum mechanics of this problem have been included in the A+ sensitivity curve \cite{a+curve}, therefore we can approach this with classical physics. When the LO and AS beams interfere at the beam splitter (BHSBS2), the power of the resulting beam is 
\begin{equation}
P = |E_s e^{i\omega t} + E_{\rm{LO}} e^{i\omega t + \phi}  |^2 = |E_s|^2 + |E_{\rm{LO}}|^2 + 2|E_sE^\dagger_{\rm{LO}}|\cos(\phi), 
\end{equation}
where $E_s$ and $E_{\rm{LO}}$ are the (complex) field amplitude of the signal and local oscillator beams, $\omega$ is the laser frequency, $t$ is time, and $\phi$ is the phase difference between the local oscillator and the signal beam, also known as the homodyne angle. Differentiating with respect to $\phi$ gives the relationship between a change in power and a change in phase between the two beams,
\begin{equation}
dP = -2|E_sE^\dagger_{\rm{LO}}|\sin(\phi) d\phi.
\end{equation}
and since the homodyne angle is 90\si{\degree}, this becomes
\begin{equation}
dP = -2|E_sE^\dagger_{\rm{LO}}|d\phi.
\end{equation}
To convert $\phi$ to distance, use
\begin{equation}
\frac{\lambda}{2\pi}d\phi = dx.
\end{equation}
The light noise curve shown in figure \ref{fig:bhdforligochapterlightnoise} should be multiplied by  $\frac{\lambda}{2\pi}\frac{1}{2|E_sE^\dagger_{\rm{sig}}|}$ to obtain the the noise floor of the interferometer in units of LO path length noise. For a 98\,mW LO beam and a 5\,mW AS beam, the noise floor of the interferometer in units of LO path length is shown in figure \ref{fig:bhdforligochapterhrtsrequirement}.
\paragraph{}
The HRTS requirement assumes, based on the current design, that there will be 2 mirrors at 45\si{\degree}  which are used to steer the AS and LO beams. As the optics reflected at 45 degrees, the path length change due to the optics motion is multiplied by$\frac{2}{\sqrt{2}} = \sqrt{2}$. The beams are combined on a beamsplitter, and the notion of this beam splitter needs to be counted twice. A sketch of this is shown in figure \ref{fig:bhdforligochapterbhddoublecounted}. A factor of 10 is then included as a safety margin since there are many uncorrelated noise sources in LIGO. With a 98\,mW LO beam and a 5\,mW AS beam, the requirement for the HRTS optics longitudinal motion at 100\,Hz is $5\times10^{-17} \rm{m}/\sqrt{\rm{Hz}}$.
A typical motion of the optical benches at 10\,Hz is $10\times10^{-1}\rm{m}/\sqrt{\rm{Hz}}$ \cite{HAM_table_motion}. To achieve this with passive isolation, a triple suspensions will be used.

\subsection{The Constraints and Requirements of the Layout of HAM6}
The entry point of the AS and LO beams in principle can be anywhere in HAM6, however in reality they are fixed due to the location of HAM6 with respect to the interferometer, due to the location of the septum windows between HAM5 and HAM6, and due to the many other components of the interferometer that these beams need to avoid. The LO beam is steered with BHDM1 onto BHDBS2, and the AS beam is steered with OM0 onto BHDBS2. This is required to provide the degrees of freedom to co-align the AS and LO beams. There is some freedom in choice for the location of OM0 and BHDBS2, but there is no choice in the location of the AS and LO beams.
\paragraph{}
The design beam parameter at the SRM is $q_{\rm{SRM}} = -3.55 + 1.09i\,\rm{m} $, so the design of the HAM6 mode matching optics is based on this number. In reality, the beam parameter could be different to this, as discussed in chapter \ref{chapter:simulation_finesse}). At the SRM, there are two sets of modes we could design the AWC to match to: The arm mode, or the SRC mode. Ideally, these would be the same mode, however geometry errors in the SRC cause the arm and the SRC to be mismatched meaning the radius of curvature and beam size at the SRM will not correspond to the eigenmode of the SRM. This set of likely modes, shown in \ref{fig:sim_chapter_beam_parameter_at_SRM}, was taken into consideration when choosing a layout for the AWC in HAM6.
\paragraph{}
After BHDBS2, the layout the optics between OMC input couplers and BHDBS2 must provide the functionality for steering the combined AS and LO beam into the OMC, and provide mode matching for the AS beam to the OMC. In addition to this, sensors in HAM6 are required for targeting the AS and LO beams. Some of these functions will be achieved using beams which are picked off from the main beam path, and so additional mirrors will be required to steer these beams around the chamber. A set of smaller optics will be installed to provide diagnostic beams which can be imaged with CCDs. A shutter will be required to protect the hardware in HAM6 from being damaged by the large amounts of light that can potential be transmitted through the SRM.
\paragraph{}
After the AS and LO beams are combined, double stage suspensions will guide the beam from BHDBS2 to the OMC. These suspensions provide active positioning control and active wave front control. In principle, the role of the AWC in HAM6 is to correct for geometry errors of the optics after the SRM such that the mode matching between the Arm mode and the OMC waist is maximised. This assumes that the changes in the interferometers mode, for instance due to a change in thermal state, will be corrected for by other subsystems e.g. the TCS. In practice, operators of the interferometer do what ever improves the SNR of the the gravitational wave signal. For instance, it has been found that TCS can be used to decrease laser intensity noise coupling to the the GW signal channel \textcolor{red}{CHECK}. The AWC in HAM6 was designed with this flexibility in mind. The losses due to components in HAM6 is summarised in table \ref{tab:HAM6_losses} (See \href{https://dcc.ligo.org/E1900385}{E1900385}).
\paragraph{}
The goal for mode matching between the arm mode and the OMC mode is is $>98\%$.  The SAMS actuators will provide $\pm100$,\,mD of optical power tuning range, with a bandwidth of the order 100\,Hz. Astigmatism can be considered as a loss, and none-zero angles of incidence (AOI) of beams onto curved optics results in a the beam becoming astigmatic. This prevents designs using large AOI. This is described in detail in Chapter \ref{sec:light_beams}.
\paragraph{}
The current OMC design (with trivial modifications) will be used to remove junk light from the signal beam, so their is no choice in target waist size. The input beam at the SRM is defined in \href{https://dcc.ligo.org/LIGO-T1200410}{T1200410}. The mode matching is designed assuming that the beam at the SRM is the `design mode'. Mode matching between the beam exiting the SRM and the OMC waist will be done with a static lens and 2 active mirrors. The static lens will be placed just after the OFI and its purpose is to provide sufficient Gouy phase separation of the 2 active mirrors. 
\paragraph{}
The light transmitted OMCs is used to measure the gravitational wave signal, since the higher order modes are suppressed by a factor of the finesse of the OMC. The OMCs are expected to be the largest contributor of back-scatter from HAM6 back to the interferometer. By having the OMCs after the AS and LO beams are combined, and by having the OFI protect the interferometer, the requirement on the motion of the OMCs is fairly lax. Nevertheless, a single stage suspended platform is required. This will provide isolation down to 4\,Hz.

\subsection{Mode Matching and Control}
\textcolor{red}{Hermite Gaussian beams will be explained earlier in the intro chapters, so don't need generic expansion, just use the 2nd order ones. Have a read of \cite{Mueller2000}}.
\paragraph{}
The mode-matching between two laser beam modes, or a laser mode and a cavity mode, describes how similar those two modes are. In general, the two modes can be mismatched by being misaligned or by having a different beam shape. Mode matching typically refers to the overlap of the two modes if they were perfectly aligned.
\paragraph{}
Mode-matching is usually, to first approximation, thought of as a difference in beam waist size and beam size location. This can be mathematically described by the $\rm{HG}_{20} + \rm{HG}_{02}$ mode. The effect of changing the waist location of the mode is equivalent to an in phase change in HG20 content, whereas an change in waist location is orthogonal to this. \textcolor{red}{read up on this}. Sensing the $\rm{HG}_{20} + \rm{HG}_{02}$ mode therefore gives a measure of the mode-matching between two lasers. As the $\rm{HG}_{20} + \rm{HG}_{02}$ acquires $3\phi$ compared to a plane wave, and $2\phi$ compared to the $\rm{HG}_{00}$ mode, spacing the actuators at $45\si{\degree}$ will give orthogonal control of the mode matching. 
\paragraph{}
To correct for mode-mismatch, the optical layout will be such that the AWC can orthogonally actuate on the beam parameter of the arm mode when its reached the OMC. 
\paragraph{}
The two mode will not perfectly interfere, and this results in some signal loss. The amount of mode matching $\mathcal{M}$ between two astigmatic modes modes, $q_{1,2}$, is
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{M} =\frac{\sqrt{\left(q_{x1}-q_{x1}^*\right)\left(q_{y1}-q_{y1}^*\right)\left(q_{x2}-q_{x2}^*\right)\left(q_{y2}-q_{y2}^*\right)}}{\left|q_{x2} - q_{x1}^*\right|\left|q_{y2} - q_{y1}^*\right|}.
\end{equation}
As well as mode matching, the layout in HAM6 should provide orthogonal control for alignment. As the alignment can be described by the  $\rm{HG}_{10} + \rm{HG}_{01}$, the alignment mode aquires an extra $\phi$ of Gouy phase compared to the $\rm{HG}_{00}$ mode. Orthogonal control can be achieved by separating the actuators for this by $90\si{\degree}$.
