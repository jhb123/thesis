A major change between aLIGO and LIGO A+ will be the replacement of DC readout with Balanced Homodyne Detection (BHD). BHD plays a key role in many experiments that utilise non-classical states of light (for a recent example of such an experiment relating to gravitational wave interferometry, see \cite{Yu2020}). The transition from aLIGO to LIGO A+ involves the redesign of many aspects of the interferometer, and elements of this effort aim to exploit quantum effects in order to increase the SNR of the detector.  Therefore, the implementation of BHD is an attractive prospect for those who want to harness the quantum nature of light to enhance the detector.
\paragraph{}
It was found that, aside from the potential quantum mechanical benefits, BHD offers the potential to increase the SNR by reducing Signal Recycling Cavity (SRC) length noise coupling to the differential arm motion (DARM) signal. Even if LIGO were to never operate in a novel, quantum-mechanically enhanced way, the reduction of SRC length noise is enough motivation to implement BHD. The longitudinal motion requirement for the optics that deliver the signal and local oscillator (LO) beams to the combining beam splitter was found to be $\leq5\times10^{-17} \rm{m}/\sqrt{\rm{Hz}}$ at 100\,Hz in order to avoid increasing the noise floor of the detector. Therefore, triple suspensions will be used to hang these optics. 
\paragraph{}

Section \ref{sec:BHD_for_ligo_BHD_vs_DC} contrasts BHD with DC readout within the specific setting of LIGO A+ and Section \ref{sec:BHD_for_ligo_LO_stability} derives the phase noise requirement for the  LO beam for A+. \section{What is BHD}
\label{sec:BHD_for_ligo_what_is_bhd}
BHD is a method of measuring information encoded by a light field, and it forms a key part of many quantum optics experiments. By combining a signal beam with a much stronger LO beam, phase modulations in the signal beam become amplitude modulations in the combined LO-signal beam (e.g.~\cite{Danilishin2012}). BHD can be configured to measure any quadrature of the signal beam which is vital for inferring the quantum state of a system and is required for more complex topologies of interferometer \cite{Knyazev2018,Eberle2010,Kimble2001,Zhang2018}. BHD has been implemented to detect 11.6\,dB of shot noise suppression to below \SI{1}{\hertz} due to the squeezing of vacuum fluctuations. \cite{Stefszky2012}.
\paragraph{}
All readout schemes for gravitational wave detectors rely on a LO field of some kind. The first generation of detectors used heterodyne readout (e.g.~\cite{Sigg2008}). Heterodyne readout involves a modulation-demodulation scheme whereby the signal is mixed with a RF LO to a frequency where technical noise is no longer a concern. It is then demodulated at the same modulation frequency to recover the original signal. This method comes with the draw back that the quantum shot noise is enhanced by the modulation-demodulation process \cite{Meers1991}.
\paragraph{}
The LO in a BHD readout scheme is a light field with the same optical frequency as the signal beam. The LO beam has a higher power than the signal beam and acts as a stable reference frequency. Ideally the LO would have no phase noise, however in reality there are fluctuations in the phase between the LO and the signal beam. The interference of the LO and signal beam causes this phase noise to become an amplitude modulation in the combined LO-signal beam\cite{Steinlechner2015}. This phase noise must be kept below the size of the phase fluctuations in the signal beam that are to be measured, which in A+ are determined by the shot noise of the light within the arm cavities. Unlike Heterodyne readout, BHD does not cause a fundamental increase in the noise floor of the measurement.
\paragraph{}
BHD is implemented by injecting the LO and signal beams into different input ports of a balanced beam splitter and placing photodiodes in the remaining output ports. The information encoded in an arbitrary quadrature of the signal beam can be selected by correctly setting the relative phase between the LO and signal beams. The relative phase between the LO and signal beam is also known as the \emph{homodyne angle}. Subtracting the two photo-currents results in a measurement which is proportional to the selected quadrature of the signal beam. Consider a LO field $\hat{a}$ and a signal field $\hat{b}$. It is arranged so that these fields interfere on a beam splitter are detected on two photodiodes, $c$ and $d$. The fields these diodes detect are $\hat{c}$ and $\hat{d}$. The photo-currents are then 
\begin{align}
\mathbf{\hat{N}}_c = \hat{c}^\dagger\hat{c} &= \left(\sqrt{R}\hat{a}^\dagger +\sqrt{T}\hat{b}^\dagger\right) \left(\sqrt{R}\hat{a} +\sqrt{T}\hat{b}\right) \\
&= R\hat{a}^\dagger\hat{a} + T\hat{b}^\dagger\hat{b} + \sqrt{RT}(\hat{a}^\dagger\hat{b} + \hat{b}^\dagger\hat{a})\\
\mathbf{\hat{N}}_d = \hat{d}^\dagger\hat{d} &= T\hat{a}^\dagger\hat{a} + R\hat{b}^\dagger\hat{b} - \sqrt{RT}(\hat{a}^\dagger\hat{b} + \hat{b}^\dagger\hat{a}).
\end{align}
For BHD, the beam splitter must be balanced so $R = T = 1/2$. This results in the difference in the photo-currents being
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{\hat{N}}_c - \mathbf{\hat{N}}_d = \hat{a}^\dagger\hat{b} + \hat{b}^\dagger\hat{a}.
\label{eq:bhd_photonnumber}
\end{equation}
The operators $\hat{a}$ and $\hat{b}$ can be `linearised', $ \hat{a}\rightarrow \alpha + \delta a$, \cite{ref for this} in order to separate the quantum behaviour and classical behaviour. Furthermore, the phase between the signal LO field can be expressed in terms of the homodyne angle with $\hat{b}\rightarrow \hat{b}\exp(i\phi)$. By neglecting terms of the order $\delta a \delta b$, this allows one to rewrite equation (\ref{eq:bhd_photonnumber}) as
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{\hat{N}}_c - \mathbf{\hat{N}}_d = 2\alpha\beta\cos\phi + 2\alpha\delta \mathbf{X}^b_{-\phi} + 2\beta\delta \mathbf{X}^a_\phi.
\end{equation}
The variance, $\sqrt{V(X)} = \Delta X$, of this measurement would then be
\begin{equation}
V( \mathbf{\hat{N}}_c - \mathbf{\hat{N}}_d) = 4\alpha^2 V(\delta \mathbf{X}^b_{-\phi})^2 + 4\beta^2 V(\delta \mathbf{X}^a_{\phi})^2.
\label{eq:BHD_var}
\end{equation}
If the LO is much stronger than the signal field, $\alpha \gg \beta$, then the measurement is
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{\hat{N}}_c - \mathbf{\hat{N}}_d = 2\alpha\beta\cos\phi + 2\alpha\delta \mathbf{X}^b_{-\phi},
\end{equation}
which is proportional to general quadrature of the signal.

\section{BHD versus DC readout for A+}
\label{sec:BHD_for_ligo_BHD_vs_DC}
There are some advantages for BHD over DC readout \cite{Fritschel2014}, although for A+ a quantifiable difference is hard to make. The main attraction for implementing BHD is the ability to reduce the dark fringe offset that is required to produce the LO for DC readout. This offset gives rise to most of the limitations associated with DC readout, such as the coupling between the SRC length noise and the DARM signal. BHD also provides the ability to select which quadrature of the signal beam is measured which is a requirement for novel interferometer configurations. 
\paragraph{}
The current detection scheme is known as DC readout \cite{Fricke2012}. A differential offset in the arm lengths produces the LO in DC readout. The LO beats with a signal created by a gravitational wave in order to produce intensity modulations at the AS port. The size of the LO can be controlled with the DC offset, and the homodyne angle can be controlled with a differential arm power. This method of readout has some key positive outcomes, such as there being no phase noise between the LO and the signal, and the LO and signal being perfectly aligned and mode matched. The problems with this readout technique are associated with requiring the DC offset as this light gives rise to noise couplings. 
\paragraph{}
For BHD to be worthwhile it must be at least as good as DC readout. Thus, BHD should be considered in relation to the perks of DC readout. Unlike DC readout, BHD may introduce phase noise between the LO and signal\cite{Steinlechner2015}. Therefore the relative stability between the LO and signal beams has to be considered. Control over the alignment and mode matching between the LO and the signal is also important. LO light that is scattered into higher order modes, by misalignment or mode mismatch, may backscatter from the OMC into the interferometer and could result in noise in the DARM signal. The implementation of BHD will only be successful if it provides a stable enough LO beam; sufficient mode-matching between the LO and signal beam, and active alignment of the LO and signal beams.
\paragraph{}
BHD has some practical benefits over DC readout\cite{Fritschel2014}\footnote{One `advantage' that is often talked about in relation to BHD is based on the electronic noise of the readout photodiodes. This is not a real advantage. aLIGO currently uses DC readout and is not limited by the electronic noise of the transimpedance amplifier. As the BHD signal is obtained through subtracting the signals from two photodiodes, it is possible to obtain a high SNR when designing the transimpedance amplifier by subtracting the currents produced by the photodiodes rather than having two separate transimpedance amplifiers and subtracting the signals made by these later. This is not really an advantage, as low enough electronic noise can be obtained using other construction techniques that allow for small DC signals while still having signal shaping that gives a large enough SNR in the detection band. Having separate transimpedance amplifiers for each photodiode also has useful diagnostic advantages, such as being able to see the sum of the two photodiode signals. Therefore, direct subtraction of the photodiode signals should not be considered an advantage of BHD over DC readout.} as well as some interesting prospects in relation to the quantum noise limit of the detector. The practical benefits arise due to being able to eliminate the DC light required for DC readout to work. The primary benefit is in the reduction in SRC length noise coupling to the gravitational wave signal. Another benefit is being able to reduce the scattering from the septum window between HAM5 and HAM6. Quantum noise shaping is, along with other interferometer enhancements (e.g~\cite{Kimble2001}), related to the complete free choice of homodyne angle that a BHD readout scheme permits.
\paragraph{}
One of the practical outcomes of implementing BHD is the potential to reduce SRC length noise coupling. Noise on the length of the SRC can couple to the gravitational wave signal via two routes. The dominant coupling, which is proportional to the DC offset, will arise due to the power entering the SRC. The other coupling arises due to the optical spring between the SRC and the arm cavity length.
\paragraph{}
The dominant coupling due to the power entering the SRC can be intuitively understood by considering a Fabry-Perot cavity. For small changes in cavity length, the light transmitted by a cavity that is on resonance or anti-resonance will be modulated by a term proportional to the square of the length change. This signal due to the length of the SRC is noise. The amplitude of this noise is linearly dependent on the amount of light entering the cavity which is a result of the DC offset. By decreasing the DC offset, this noise is decreased. As this noise is in the amplitude quadrature of the signal beam, unlike the gravitational wave signal which is in the phase quadrature, it will not be amplified by the LO if the homodyne angle is set to readout from the phase quadrature.
\paragraph{}
The second route SRC length noise may couple to the signal is via the optical springs between the SRC and arm cavities. Due to this optical spring effect, a change in length of the SRC will cause the arm cavity lengths to change as well \cite{Izumi2016}. The effect of optical springs on the SRC length noise coupling strength is much smaller than the first effect, so the estimate for the reduction of SRC length coupling will only considering the first effect.
\paragraph{}
SRC length noise is currently too high to meet the A+ requirements \cite{O3b_noise_budget}. %\href{https://alog.ligo-wa.caltech.edu/aLOG/index.php?callRep=55755}{Shelia Dwyer post}. 
To estimate the reduction in SRC length noise that BHD may provide, the amount of DC offset that is currently used should be compared to the amount that is proposed for BHD. aLGIO has 23\,mW of light due to the DC offset and 1\,mW of residual light (light that cannot be removed) at the AS port \cite{Dwyer_email_2020}. %\textcolor{red}{(shelia dwyer answer in email FW: BHD review committee request \& document PREVIEW).Assume 0.85 A/W (qe = 99\%)}. 
The AS power for A+ is between 1 and 5\,mW. Therefore, a conservative estimate for the factor that the SRC length noise coupling is reduced by is four.
\paragraph{}
Strictly speaking, these are not properties of BHD readout as scatter from the septum window and the SRC length noise could be reduced by other means. For instance, the septum window could be removed and suspensions with additional stages could be used. However, these solutions are not trivial to realise, and they are no more practical than implementing BHD. The septum window is a key part in being able to carry out commissioning tasks within HAM6. The number of times HAM5 and the other connected chambers are vented should be kept low as each time this is done gas and water vapour enters the system (this contributes to the noise floor of the interferometer). This residual gas and vapour will not be removed as this is a large engineering challenge. The design and install of three new suspensions is also a difficult task, which ultimately gives less benefit compared to a BHD readout scheme. Realistic upgrades that will reduce to the SRC length noise coupling are currently being developed \cite{SRCL_ideas_2020}.
\paragraph{}
Different gravitational wave sources carry their information in different frequency bands and the detector may be optimised to reflect this \cite{McClelland2011,squeezing_sql_2011a,McCuller2020,Kimble2001}. For instance, BNS signals continue to higher frequencies than BBH signals so it may be of interest to optimise the detector around these frequencies to improve our understanding of the properties of BNS systems. To optimise the sensitivity of the interferometer to different sources the detectors noise would need shaping, a task that can be achieved, along with other upgrades, BHD. 
\paragraph{}
The current generation of gravitational wave detectors are going to be limited by a combination of radiation pressure noise and shot noise in their detection bands. These two noise sources are intrinsically linked by the Heisenberg uncertainty principle and can both be understood as arising due to the number of photons used to probe the position of the end test masses. This limit in the noise floor is known as the standard quantum limit \cite{Kimble2001}. To circumvent this limit the homodyne angle must be free to choose; a functionality that is provided by BHD. In principle, DC readout gives the ability to set the homodyne angle \cite{Fritschel2014}, although in practice it is much less flexible than a BHD configuration.
\paragraph{}
A squeezed light field can be used to surpass the standard quantum limit, and to detect these squeezed states the homodyne angle must be correctly set. The exact way in which the quantum noise is changed is controlled by the squeezing angle. Currently the squeezing angle is set to be at the same angle as the readout quadrature, rather than the preferred option of setting the readout quadrature to the squeezing angle. The effect that controlling the squeezing angle has on the quantum noise floor has been demonstrated at LLO \cite{Yu2020}. However, \cite{Yu2020} used a diagnostic BHD set-up which was not intended for providing the low noise required to make gravitational wave detections. %\textcolor{red}{Need to read up on this, https://doi.org/10.1103/PhysRevD.65.022002}
\paragraph{}
More advanced upgrades, e.g.~see \cite{Kimble2001}, could alter quantum noise substantially, and they rely on the implementation of BHD. These changes to the interferometer are nontrivial, and would require significant redesign of many parts of the layout, so the implementation of BHD for A+ does not necessarily correspond to a suitable BHD system for one of these more advanced interferometer configurations. However, BHD for A+ would provide experience with implementing BHD on a large scale interferometer, which would help when considering how to implement these more novel interferometer configurations.
\paragraph{}
In summary, BHD has a couple of key advantages over DC readout. BHD allows for the reduction in SRC length noise coupling and, in conjunction with other upgrades, the ability to shape the quantum noise floor of the interferometer. In DC readout, the SRC length coupling comes from the DC offset required to provide a LO beam for the signal generated by a differential modulation in arm length to beat against. This DC offset is not required for BHD. Most interferometer configurations that beat the SQL assume that the homodyne angle can be freely set. The drawbacks of BHD when compared DC readout are all engineering challenges; nothing fundamentally favours DC readout over BHD. 
\section{Local Oscillator Path Length Stability Requirement for A+}
\label{sec:BHD_for_ligo_LO_stability}
This Section will go through the steps needed to derive the phase noise requirement for the beam path of the A+ LO. This uses a semi-classical approach, and the end result is the same as if a full quantum mechanical treatment was used. The quantum mechanics of this problem is included in the A+ sensitivity curve and the expected power in the signal beam. The phase noise of the LO must be kept low enough so that it does not result in intensity fluctuations greater than those due to the shot noise of the light in the signal beam. This requirement was also derived in \cite{Zhang2019},  \cite{Fritschel2017b} and \cite{HRTSrequirement}, however in this work the most current layout was considered. 
\paragraph{}
Phase noise arises due to phase fluctuations in the LO beam being indistinguishable from phase fluctuations in the signal beam. A gravitational wave produces a phase modulated signal in the signal beam, and the LO field phase is modulated by the motion of the mirrors in the LO path. When these beams are combined, it is the phase difference between the two fields that produces a signal, so the phase noise of the LO path must be low enough that the signal that is generated by the interference of the LO and signal beams is predominantly due to the phase modulation of the signal beam. 
\paragraph{}
The relationship between the differential phase in the arms and the power in the signal beam was used to find the power noise for the beam exiting the SRM in A+. The linear response function due to a differential phase change in the arms was computed with \textsc{Finesse}. This was used to deconvolve the strain sensitivity curve to produce a power noise curve for the light exiting the AS port of the interferometer. Since the interferometer is linear, noise is transformed linearly. This means that the quantum nature of the noise is irrelevant to the calculation.
\paragraph{}
This light noise curve was then used to set the maximum allowable intensity fluctuations due to phase noise between the LO and the signal beams. This phase noise can then be converted into a distance, and this produces a stability requirement for the steering optics used to combine the LO and signal beams.%From this, it is found that the optics need to be suspended as a triple stage pendulum. 
\paragraph{}
The following Sections will go through the aforementioned steps. First the LO path will be described. Then a linear response function between a differential strain and signal at the AS port will be obtained via \textsc{Finesse}. Next, The sensitivity curve for A+ \cite{a+curve} will be expressed in units of $\frac{\rm{W}}{\sqrt{\rm{Hz}}}$. This will be converted into a longitudinal motion requirement. Finally, there is a short discussion about the proposed optics that fulfil this requirement.
\subsection{Local Oscillator Path}
The beam path for the LO and signal beams up to the point of recombination will be described in this Section. The LO will be picked off from the power recycling cavity and guided by one steering mirror, BHDM1, to the BHD combining beam splitter, BHDBS2. The signal beam will exit the OFI and be steered by one mirror, OM0, onto BHDBS2. OM0 and BHDM1 will be used to align the beams so they overlap on BHDBS2. The angle of BHDBS2 will be used to make the LO and signal beams overlap in the far field.
\paragraph{}
The LO could have been derived from a separate laser, but to acquire laser light with low frequency and intensity noise the LO will be picked off from a point within the interferometer. Two options for the LO pick off were considered \cite{Zhang2019}: a beam picked off from the main beam splitters AR coating (BSAR), or the beam transmitted through PR2 (POP). The determining factor in choice of pick off point was the effect of backscatter. The POP port was chosen as it is far superior to the BSAR choice at all frequencies and scatter phases. This is because backscattered light from the BSAR enters only one arm, thus making it a differential affect. Any backscatter that enters the POP port is common-mode, and therefore DARM is far less sensitive to this source of backscatter.
\paragraph{}
The layout for the optics which combine the LO and signal beam is shown in Figure \ref{fig:bhdforligochaptersketchoflodelivery}. The beam transmitted by PR2 is used for control of the interferometer, so BHDBS1 is needed to pick off some of this light. The LO beam is steered towards the combining beam splitter BHDBS2 with BHDM1. Two lenses (\textcolor{red}{need names for these}) are used for mode matching the LO beam to the signal beam. The signal beam is transmitted through the SRM and OFI, and then it is steered towards BHDBS2 with OM0.

\subsection{The Linear Response Function Between Differential Strain and Power at the AS Port}

The linear response function of the A+ detectors was obtained using \textsc{Finesse}. Since the core optics of the interferometer will not be changing in a way relevant to this calculation, the LLO model \cite{l1_finesse_2014} was used to calculate the response between differential motion of the ETMs and the power transmitted through the OMC. The linear response function at the frequencies contained in the detection band are not influenced by the OMC. This means no major modifications to the \textsc{Finesse} model were needed, and so the model represents a state that the interferometer would be in for observing gravitational waves.
\paragraph{}
\textsc{Finesse} allows one to measure linear response functions by applying a `swept sine' signal to a component and demodulating the light at the frequency of the applied signal. To create a differential arm length signal with \textsc{Finesse}, \verb|fsig| commands were used to apply two signals in anti-phase with each other to the ETMs,
\begin{verbatim}

\end{verbatim}
The frequency of this modulation was swept, and the signal produced at the AS port was measured by demodulating the light at the modulation frequency. The commands

put P_DC_OMC_probe f1 $x1

do this.

\subsection{Converting the Sensitivity Curve for A+ into a Longitudinal Motion Requirement}
To convert the A+ sensitivity curve into a path length stability requirement for the LO, a few steps are required. First, the sensitivity curve must be expressed in units of $\frac{\rm{W}}{\sqrt{\rm{Hz}}}$. Second, the intensity noise of the signal beam needs to be converted into a phase noise. In order for the phase noise of the LO to not limit the performance of the gravitational wave detector, the phase signal in the signal beam must be greater than the phase noise of the LO beam. Finally, a factor of ten safety margin is required. The HRTS requirement is then obtained by including the number of reflective optics in the LO path and signal path.

Next, a change in power measured in the combined LO/signal beam due to a phase modulation, i.e.~path length modulation, in the LO or signal path needs calculating. When the LO and signal beams interfere at BHSBS2, the exiting beams have power $P_c$ and $P_d$. These are given by
\begin{equation}
P_c = \left|\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}E_s e^{i\omega t} + \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}E_{\rm{LO}} e^{i\omega t + \phi}  \right|^2 = \frac{|E_s|^2}{2} + \frac{|E_{\rm{LO}}|^2}{2} + |E_sE^\dagger_{\rm{LO}}|\cos(\phi)
\end{equation}
and
\begin{equation}
P_d = \left|\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}E_s e^{i\omega t} - \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}E_{\rm{LO}} e^{i\omega t + \phi}  \right|^2 = \frac{|E_s|^2}{2} + \frac{|E_{\rm{LO}}|^2}{2} - |E_sE^\dagger_{\rm{LO}}|\cos(\phi),
\end{equation}
where $E_s$ and $E_{\rm{LO}}$ are the (complex) field amplitude of the signal and LO beams; $\omega$ is the laser frequency; $t$ is time and $\phi$ is the homodyne angle. The difference in these signals gives BHD signal
\begin{equation}
P = 2|E_sE^\dagger_{\rm{LO}}|\cos(\phi).
\label{eq:classical_bhd}
\end{equation}
Differentiating with respect to $\phi$ gives the relationship between a change in power and a change in phase between the two beams,
\begin{equation}
dP = -2|E_sE^\dagger_{\rm{LO}}|\sin(\phi) d\phi,
\end{equation}
and since the homodyne angle is 90\si{\degree} this becomes
\begin{equation}
dP = -2|E_sE^\dagger_{\rm{LO}}|d\phi.
\end{equation}
To convert $\phi$ to distance, use
\begin{equation}
\frac{\lambda}{2\pi}d\phi = dx.
\end{equation}
The light noise curve shown in Figure \ref{fig:bhdforligochapterlightnoise} should be multiplied by $\frac{\lambda}{2\pi}\frac{1}{2|E_sE^\dagger_{\rm{sig}}|}$ to obtain the noise floor of the interferometer in units of LO path length noise. 
\paragraph{}
To determine the longitudinal motion requirement for the optics that combine the LO and signal beam the number of optics and, since there are many uncorrelated noise sources in LIGO, a factor of ten safety margin needs to be included. The requirement assumes, based on the current design, that there will be two mirrors at 45\si{\degree} which are used to steer the AS and LO beams. As the optics reflect at 45\si{\degree}, the path length change due to the optics motion is multiplied by $\sqrt{2}$. The beams are combined on a beam splitter, and the motion of this beam splitter needs to be counted twice. Figure \ref{fig:bhdforligochapterbhddoublecounted} illustrates this.
\paragraph{}
For a 98\,mW LO beam and a 5\,mW signal beam, the noise floor of the interferometer in units of LO path length is shown in Figure \ref{fig:bhdforligochapterhrtsrequirement}. The requirement for the longitudinal motion of optics in the LO or signal path at 100\,Hz is $5\times10^{-17} \rm{m}/\sqrt{\rm{Hz}}$.
\paragraph{}
To determine the amount of isolation required, the typical motion of the optical table needs to be compared to the longitudinal motion requirement for the optics. A typical motion of the optical benches at 10\,Hz is $10\times10^{-1}\rm{m}/\sqrt{\rm{Hz}}$ \cite{HAM_table_motion}. To achieve this with passive isolation, triple suspensions will be used. These suspensions are known as HAM Relay Triple Suspensions (HRTS)\cite{Huddart2019}.

\subsection{Summary of LO path length Stability}
Details of the LO path were discussed and analysed. The LO will be derived from a beam picked off from the POP port, and a sketch of how the signal and LO will be combined is shown in Figure \ref{fig:bhdforligochaptersketchoflodelivery}. Starting with the linear response function between a differential strain and a signal at the AS port and the strain sensitivity curve for A+, we were able to derive a relative path length stability requirement for the LO and signal beams. At 100\,Hz this requirement is $5\times10^{-17} \rm{m}/\sqrt{\rm{Hz}}$. By comparing this motion to a typical motion of the optical table, we were able to derive an isolation requirement, shown in Figure \ref{fig:bhdforligochapterhrtsrequirement}. This longitudinal phase noise requirement led to triple suspensions being chosen to steer the LO and signal beams onto the combining beam splitter. 
\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:BHD_for_ligo_conclusion_1}
One of the major changes to LIGO for A+ will be the implementation of BHD. Combined with frequency dependent squeezing, BHD offers the possibility to optimise the detector for different gravitational wave sources. Along with this, BHD offers a way by which the coupling between the SRC length noise and DARM can be reduced.
\paragraph{}
BHD presents some technical challenges over DC readout. The LO phase noise must be kept low enough as to not contaminate the signal at the AS port with LO path noise. The requirement for the phase noise is shown in Figure \ref{fig:bhdforligochapterhrtsrequirement}. This requirement can be met by using triple suspensions to deliver to LO and signal beams to the beam splitter they are combined on.
