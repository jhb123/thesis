A major change between the aLIGO and LIGO A+ detectors will be the replacement of DC readout (see Section~\ref{sec:BHD_for_ligo_alternatives}) with \gls{bhd}. In contrast to DC readout, the \gls{lo} and signal do not follow the same optical path in a \gls{bhd} scheme; the consequence of this is the phase of the \gls{lo} beam needs to be stable. The stability requirement of the \gls{lo}'s phase is discussed in Section~\ref{sec:BHD_for_ligo_LO_stability}. Figure~\ref{fig:bhdforligochaptersketchoflodelivery} is a sketch of the \gls{lo}'s beam path. The proposed \gls{bhd} layout is discussed in Chapter~\ref{chapter:BHD_for_ligo_2}.

\section{The Principles of and Motivations for Balanced Homodyne Detection}
\label{sec:BHD_for_ligo_what_is_bhd}
%By combining a signal beam with a much stronger \gls{lo} beam, phase modulations in the signal beam become amplitude modulations in the combined \gls{lo}-signal beam (e.g.~\cite{Danilishin2012}). \gls{bhd} can be configured to measure any quadrature of the signal beam which is vital for inferring the quantum state of a system, and is required for more complex topologies of interferometer~\cite{Knyazev2018,Eberle2010,Kimble2001,Zhang2018}. \gls{bhd} has been used to detect 11.6\,dB of shot noise suppression to below \SI{1}{\hertz} due to the squeezing of vacuum fluctuations.~\cite{Stefszky2012}.
%\par
\gls{bhd} is a method of measuring the information encoded in any quadrature of a signal beam (see e.g.~\cite[Section~2]{Danilishin2012}); thus, \gls{bhd} forms a key part of many quantum optics experiments (e.g.~\cite{Walls1983,Yu2020,Stefszky2012}). In a \gls{bhd} readout scheme, the signal beam is set up to interfere with a \gls{lo} beam with the same optical frequency on a beam splitter, and the interference is measured with photodiodes at the beam splitter's output ports. A sketch of a \gls{bhd} readout is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:bhdforligochapterbhdsketch}.
\par
The \gls{lo} beam has a larger power than the signal beam, and the power of the \gls{lo} is chosen to bring the quantum noise above the noise floor of the photodiode and its preamplifier. An arbitrary quadrature of the signal beam can be measured by correctly setting the relative phase between the \gls{lo} and signal beams. This phase is known as the \emph{homodyne angle}. If the two photocurrents are balanced, subtracting them results in a measurement which is proportional to the selected quadrature of the signal beam. Additionally, the subtraction results in the removal of the classical intensity noise of the \gls{lo} from the measurement. \gls{bhd} is mathematically described in Section~\ref{sec:BHD_for_ligo_QM_BHD}.
\begin{figure}
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=0.5\linewidth]{Figures/BHD_for_ligo_chapter_BHD_sketch}
	\caption{A coherent state $\ket{\alpha}$, enters port $a$ of a beam splitter. A signal $\ket{\Psi}$ enters port $b$. These two beams interfere, resulting in photons being detected at the $c$ and $d$ ports. The two photocurrents are subtracted, and the signal that is produced is proportional to an arbitrary quadrature of the state $\ket{\Psi}$; the difference in the signals at the $c$ and $d$ ports is given by Equation~(\ref{eq:bhd_signal}). The quadrature of the state $\ket{\Psi}$ that is observed depends on the homodyne angle, $\phi$. }
	\label{fig:bhdforligochapterbhdsketch}
\end{figure}
\par
The LIGO A+ detectors need to use frequency-dependent squeezing to reach their design sensitivities. To do this, the homodyne angle must be \ang{90}. There is a low amount of tolerance on the homodyne angle, $(90\pm1)\si{\degree}$, before the quantum noise is significantly degraded~\cite{eric_oelker_2021}, and the current readout method (see Section~\ref{sec:BHD_for_ligo_alternatives}) does not offer sufficient control over the homodyne angle. For aLIGO, the homodyne angle is around \ang{10} away from the phase quadrature~\cite{BHD_angle_2020}. Currently, the squeezing angle is set to be at the same angle as the readout quadrature~\cite{Tse2019}; the preference would be to set the readout quadrature to the squeezing angle, as then there would be more control over the quantum noise of the detector i.e.~you wouldn't be forced to use a squeezing angle that was bad for radiation pressure effects, for example. The effect that controlling the squeezing angle has on the quantum noise floor has been demonstrated at LLO~\cite{Yu2020}. However,~\cite{Yu2020} used a diagnostic \gls{bhd} setup which was too noisy to allow gravitational wave detections. 
\par
%\par
%Ideally the \gls{lo} would have no phase noise, however in reality there are fluctuations in the phase between the \gls{lo} and the signal beam. The interference of the \gls{lo} and signal beam causes this phase noise to become an amplitude modulation in the combined \gls{lo}-signal beam\cite{Steinlechner2015}. This phase noise must be kept below the size of the phase fluctuations in the signal beam that are to be measured, which in A+ are determined by the shot noise of the light within the arm cavities. Unlike Heterodyne readout, \gls{bhd} does not cause a fundamental increase in the noise floor of the measurement.
\subsection{Alternatives to Balanced Homodyne Detection}
\label{sec:BHD_for_ligo_alternatives}
The current detection scheme is known as DC readout~\cite{Fricke2012}; this is a form of homodyne detection. A differential offset in the arm lengths produces the \gls{lo} in DC readout; the size of the \gls{lo} can be set with the differential offset in the length of the arms, and the homodyne angle can be set with the differential arm power~\cite{Fritschel2014}. DC readout has some key positive outcomes, such as there being no phase noise between the \gls{lo} and the signal, and the \gls{lo} and signal being perfectly aligned and mode matched to each other. However, while theoretically the homodyne angle can be set, due to different amounts of loss in each arm, the homodyne angle is not a free parameter. The inability to tune the homodyne angle increased the detector's quantum noise \footnote{This increase was buried under the classical noise floor; however, this noise was in a frequency band where the LIGO detectors are designed to be quantum noise limited.}. 
\par
The first generation of detectors used heterodyne readout (e.g.~\cite{Sigg2008}). Heterodyne readout involves a modulation-demodulation scheme whereby the signal is beat with a \gls{rf} \gls{lo} to a frequency where technical noise is no longer a concern. It is then demodulated at the same modulation frequency to recover the original signal. This method comes with the draw back that the shot noise is enhanced by the modulation-demodulation process~\cite{Meers1991}. Heterodyne detection is described in more detail in the final paragraph of Section~\ref{sec:pd_chapter_grav_wave_ifo_requirments}.
\subsection{Quantum Mechanical Description of Balanced Homodyne Detection}
\label{sec:BHD_for_ligo_QM_BHD}
To understand how \gls{bhd} can be used to detect quantum states of light, it is useful to mathematically express light and interference in terms of quantum mechanics. From the two-photon formalism of light (see Section~\ref{sec:detector_chapter_two_photon_formalism}), the general quadrature operator, $\mathbf{\hat{X}}_\phi$ can be obtained,
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{\hat{X}}_\phi = \mathbf{\hat{X}}\cos\phi + \mathbf{\hat{Y}}\sin\phi,
\label{eq:BHD_for_ligo_general_quadrature}
\end{equation}
where $\phi$ is the homodyne angle. By setting the homodyne angle, one can sense an arbitrary combination of the phase and amplitude of a light field.
%\begin{equation}
%\alpha_{\rm{\gls{lo}}} = \left| \alpha_{\rm{LO}} \right|\exp^{i\phi}.
%\end{equation}
\par
Consider the interference of a \gls{lo} field, $\hat{a}$, and a signal field, $\hat{b}$, on a 50:50 beam splitter. The resulting fields,  $\hat{c}$ and $\hat{d}$, are detected on two photodiodes. This is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:bhdforligochapterbhdsketch}. The photocurrents are proportional to 
\begin{align}
\mathbf{\hat{N}}_c = \hat{c}^\dagger\hat{c} &= \left(\sqrt{R}\hat{a}^\dagger +\sqrt{T}\hat{b}^\dagger\right) \left(\sqrt{R}\hat{a} +\sqrt{T}\hat{b}\right), \nonumber \\
&= R\hat{a}^\dagger\hat{a} + T\hat{b}^\dagger\hat{b} + \sqrt{RT}(\hat{a}^\dagger\hat{b} + \hat{b}^\dagger\hat{a}),\\
\mathbf{\hat{N}}_d = \hat{d}^\dagger\hat{d} &= T\hat{a}^\dagger\hat{a} + R\hat{b}^\dagger\hat{b} - \sqrt{RT}(\hat{a}^\dagger\hat{b} + \hat{b}^\dagger\hat{a}).
\end{align}
As the beam splitter is 50:50, $R = T = 1/2$. This results in the difference in the photocurrents being proportional to
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{\hat{N}}_c - \mathbf{\hat{N}}_d = \hat{a}^\dagger\hat{b} + \hat{b}^\dagger\hat{a}.
\label{eq:bhd_photonnumber}
\end{equation}
The operators $\hat{a}$ and $\hat{b}$ can be `linearised', $ \hat{a}\rightarrow \alpha + \delta a$ to separate the quantum behaviour and classical behaviour~\cite{Steinlechner2015}. Furthermore, the phase between the signal field and the \gls{lo} field can be expressed in terms of the homodyne angle with $\hat{b}\rightarrow \hat{b}\exp(i\phi)$. By neglecting terms of the order $\delta a \delta b$, Equation~(\ref{eq:bhd_photonnumber}) can be written as
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{\hat{N}}_c - \mathbf{\hat{N}}_d = 2\alpha\beta\cos\phi + 2\alpha\delta \mathbf{X}^b_{-\phi} + 2\beta\delta \mathbf{X}^a_\phi.
\end{equation}
The variance, $\sqrt{V(X)} = \Delta X$, of this measurement would then be
\begin{equation}
V( \mathbf{\hat{N}}_c - \mathbf{\hat{N}}_d) = 4\alpha^2 V(\delta \mathbf{X}^b_{-\phi}) + 4\beta^2 V(\delta \mathbf{X}^a_{\phi}).
\label{eq:BHD_var}
\end{equation}
If the \gls{lo} is much stronger than the signal field, $\alpha \gg \beta$, then the measurement is
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{\hat{N}}_c - \mathbf{\hat{N}}_d = 2\alpha\beta\cos\phi + 2\alpha\delta \mathbf{X}^b_{-\phi}.
\label{eq:bhd_signal}
\end{equation}
This shows that the difference between the two photocurrents will result in the quadrature of the $b$ field that is set by the homodyne angle to be measured. The noise of this measurement will be strongly dependent on the power of the $a$ field if it is much larger than the $b$ field.
\subsection{Other Motivations for Implementing Balanced Homodyne Detection in LIGO A+}
\label{sec:BHD_for_ligo_BHD_alt_motivation}
Different gravitational wave sources carry their information in different frequency bands, and the detector may be optimised to reflect this~\cite{McClelland2011,squeezing_sql_2011a,McCuller2020,Kimble2001}. For instance, \gls{bns} signals continue to higher frequencies than \gls{bbh} signals, so it may be of interest to optimise the detector around higher frequencies to improve our understanding of the properties of \gls{bns} systems. To optimise the sensitivity of the interferometer to different sources, the detector's noise would need shaping. To do this, \gls{bhd} can be used alongside other upgrades (e.g~\cite{Kimble2001,Zhang2020}).

\par
More advanced upgrades, e.g.~converting the detectors to speedmeters (see Chapter~\ref{sec:detector_chapter_beating_IFO_quantum_limit} or~\cite{Kimble2001}), could alter the detector's quantum noise substantially; this would also rely on the implementation of \gls{bhd}. These changes to the interferometer are non-trivial and would require significant redesign of many parts of the layout, so the implementation of \gls{bhd} for A+ does not necessarily correspond to a suitable \gls{bhd} system for one of these more advanced interferometer configurations. However, \gls{bhd} for A+ would provide experience with implementing \gls{bhd} on a large-scale interferometer, which would help when considering how to realise these more novel interferometer configurations.

Additionally, there will be less scattering from the septum window between HAM5 and HAM6\footnote{Strictly speaking, the scattering from the septum window is not a property of DC readout as the window could be removed. However, this window is crucial for the maintenance of the interferometer.}. %\footnote{One `advantage' that is often talked about in relation to \gls{bhd} is based on the electronic noise of the readout photodiodes. Therefore, direct subtraction of the photodiode signals should not be considered an advantage of \gls{bhd} over DC readout.}

\section{The Local Oscillator Path}
\label{sec:BHD_for_ligo_LO_stability}
%In this section, a semi-classical approach is used to derive the path length stability requirement for the local oscillator. 
%If a full quantum mechanical treatment were used (e.g.~\cite{Steinlechner2015}), the result would be the same because the quantum mechanics of this problem are included in the A+ sensitivity curve and the expected power in the signal beam i.e.~since the LIGO interferometers are linear, their noise is transformed linearly, and the quantum nature of the noise is irrelevant to the calculation. The phase noise of the LO must be kept low enough so that it does not result in intensity fluctuations greater than those due to the shot noise of the light in the signal beam. This requirement was also derived in~\cite{Zhang2019,Fritschel2017b} and~\cite{HRTSrequirement}; however, in this section, the most current layout was considered. 
Phase noise arises due to phase fluctuations in the \gls{lo} beam being indistinguishable from phase fluctuations in the signal beam. A gravitational wave produces a phase modulated signal in the signal beam, and the \gls{lo} field phase is modulated by the motion of the mirrors in the \gls{lo} path. When these beams are combined, it is the phase difference between the two fields that produces a signal, so the phase noise of the \gls{lo} path must be low enough for the signal generated by the interference of the \gls{lo} and signal beams to be due to the phase modulation of the signal beam. 
%\par
%This section is structured as follows: first, the LO path is described. Then, a linear response function between a differential strain and signal at the \gls{as} port is obtained. Next, the sensitivity curve for A+ (see Figure~\ref{fig:detectorchapterdesignsensitivitycurves}) is expressed in units of $\frac{\rm{W}}{\sqrt{\rm{Hz}}}$ by deconvolving it with the linear response function. This light noise curve was then used to find the maximum allowable intensity fluctuations due to phase noise between the LO and the signal beams. Finally, this phase noise requirement is converted into a optical path length requirement.
%\subsection{Local Oscillator Path}
\par
The layout for the optics which combine the \gls{lo} and signal beam is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:bhdforligochaptersketchoflodelivery}. The \gls{lo} will be picked off from the power recycling cavity and guided by one steering mirror, BHDM1, to the \gls{bhd} combining beam splitter, BHDBS2. The signal beam will exit the OFI and be steered by one mirror, OM0, onto BHDBS2. OM0 and BHDM1 will be used to align the beams so they overlap on BHDBS2. The angle of BHDBS2 will be used to make the \gls{lo} and signal beams overlap in the far field. Two lenses are used for mode matching the \gls{lo} beam to the signal beam.
\par
To acquire a \gls{lo} with low frequency and intensity noise, the \gls{lo} will be picked off from a point within the interferometer. The two ways of obtaining a \gls{lo} beam considered were a beam picked off from the main beam splitter's AR coating (BSAR) or the beam transmitted through PR2 (POP)~\cite{Zhang2019}. The determining factor in choice of pick-off point was the effect of backscatter on the interferometer's strain sensitivity. The POP port was chosen as it is far superior to the BSAR choice at all frequencies and scatter phases. This is because backscattered light from the BSAR enters only one arm, thus making it a differential effect. Any backscatter that enters the POP port is common-mode, and therefore the differential arm signal is far less sensitive to this source of backscatter.

	\centering

\subsection{The Local Oscillator Path Length Stability Requirement}
The first step is to express the A+ sensitivity curve in units of $\si{\radian/\sqrt{Hz}}$. This requires the A+ sensitivity curve to be deconvolved with the linear response function of the interferometer. The signal generated by the differential motion of the ETMs is low-passed by the coupled \gls{src}-arm cavity, so the linear response function can be written as 
\begin{equation}
H(f) = \frac{H_0}{1 - if/f_c},
\label{eq:bhdforligochapter_linear_response_fn}
\end{equation}
where $H_0$ is a gain factor, $f$ is the frequency and $f_c$ is the corner frequency of the \gls{src}-arm cavity. For the LIGO detectors, $f_c = 380\,\si{\hertz}$~\cite{Izumi2016}. This linear response function does not include optical spring effects, but these are small and not important for this calculation.
\par
$H_0$ can be calculated by dividing the phase noise of the signal beam, $\Delta\phi_{\rm{sig}}$, by the product of the A+ sensitivity curve (see Figure~\ref{fig:detectorchapterdesignsensitivitycurves}) and $1/(1 - if/f_c) $ at a frequency where the detector will be shot noise limited. Care needs to be taken to avoid double counting the squeezing; the signal beam will be squeezed in the phase quadrature, but as the A+ sensitivity curve includes squeezing, the squeezing factor can be omitted from the phase noise associated with the signal beam in this calculation.
\par
From Equation~(\ref{eq:phase_photon_number_relation}), the relationship between the phase of the signal beam $\phi_{\rm{sig}}$ and its power $P_{\rm{sig}}$ can be written as 
\begin{equation}
	\Delta\phi_{\rm{sig}} = \sqrt{\frac{h\nu}{4P_{\rm{sig}}}}
\end{equation}
where $h$ is Planck's constant and $\nu$ is the frequency of the light. For A+, $P_{\rm{sig}} = 5\,\rm{mW}$. The linear response function is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:bhdforligochapterdetectorrepsonse}. To express the A+ sensitivity curve in units of $\si{\radian/\sqrt{Hz}}$, the sensitivity curve should be divided by the linear response function shown in Figure~\ref{fig:bhdforligochapterdetectorrepsonse}.
\begin{figure}
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=1\linewidth]{Figures/BHD_for_ligo_chapter_detector_repsonse}
	\caption{The linear response function between strain and light power, expressed in units of radians/gravitational wave strain, at the \gls{as} port. The corner frequency at $\approx380$\,Hz comes from the pole of the coupled \gls{src}-arm cavity.}
	\label{fig:bhdforligochapterdetectorrepsonse}
\end{figure}
\par
The next step is to use the equations for interference at a beam splitter to convert a change in phase between the \gls{lo} and signal beam into a change in power of the combined beams that exit the beam splitter. When the \gls{lo} and signal beams interfere at BHSBS2, the exiting beams have power $P_c$ and $P_d$. These are given by
\begin{equation}
P_c = \left|\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}E_{\rm{sig}} e^{i\omega t} + \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}E_{\rm{LO}} e^{i\omega t + \phi}  \right|^2 = \frac{|E_{\rm{sig}}|^2}{2} + \frac{|E_{\rm{LO}}|^2}{2} + |E_{\rm{sig}}E^\dagger_{\rm{LO}}|\cos(\phi)
\end{equation}
and
\begin{equation}
P_d = \left|\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}E_{\rm{sig}} e^{i\omega t} - \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}E_{\rm{LO}} e^{i\omega t + \phi}  \right|^2 = \frac{|E_{\rm{sig}}|^2}{2} + \frac{|E_{\rm{LO}}|^2}{2} - |E_{\rm{sig}}E^\dagger_{\rm{LO}}|\cos(\phi),
\end{equation}
where $E_{\rm{sig}}$ and $E_{\rm{LO}}$ are the complex field amplitudes of the signal and \gls{lo} beams expressed in units such that $E^*E$ gives the power of the beam; $\omega$ is the laser frequency; $t$ is time and $\phi$ is the homodyne angle. The power of the \gls{lo} beam will be $P_{\rm{LO}} = 100\,\si{\milli\watt}$. Note, the signal carrying light will be detected on four photodiodes (two at the output of each \gls{omc}), and this power is compatible with the discussion of a photodiode's requirements in Section~\ref{sec:pd_chapter_grav_wave_ifo_requirments}.
\par
The difference in $P_c$ and $P_d$ signals gives the \gls{bhd} signal,
\begin{equation}
P = 2|E_{\rm{sig}}E^\dagger_{\rm{LO}}|\cos(\phi).
\label{eq:classical_bhd}
\end{equation}
Differentiating with respect to $\phi$ gives the relationship between a change in power of the interfered beams and a change in phase between the \gls{lo} and signal beams,
\begin{equation}
dP = -2|E_{\rm{sig}}E^\dagger_{\rm{LO}}|\sin(\phi) d\phi.
\end{equation}
Since the homodyne angle is 90\si{\degree}, this becomes
\begin{equation}
dP = -2|E_{\rm{sig}}E^\dagger_{\rm{LO}}|d\phi.
\label{eq:bhdforligo_interference_at_a_beam_splitter}
\end{equation}
\par
Using Equation~(\ref{eq:bhdforligo_interference_at_a_beam_splitter}), the phase noise requirement for the local oscillator can be expressed in units of $\si{\radian/\sqrt{Hz}}$. This is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:bhdforligochapterphasenoise}. This requirement was also derived in~\cite{Zhang2019,Fritschel_bhd_requirements} and~\cite{HRTSrequirement}; however, these calculations all differ from each other by factors of $\sim1$. This can be explained by differences in the assumed power of the local oscillator.
\begin{figure}[h]
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=1\linewidth]{Figures/BHD_for_ligo_chapter_phase_noise}
	\caption{The sensitivity curve for the A+ detectors expressed in units of $\si{\radian/\sqrt{Hz}}$. At frequencies above 1\,kHz, the noise is flat ($3.4\times10^{-5}\,\si{\radian/\sqrt{\hertz}}$) as it is dominated by shot noise. At lower frequencies, the noise is increased relative to shot noise due to radiation pressure, coating thermal noise and other noise sources. At 5\,\si{\hertz}, the noise is $8\times10^{-5}\,\si{\radian/\sqrt{\hertz}}$.}
	\label{fig:bhdforligochapterphasenoise}
\end{figure}
\par
To express phase in terms of optical path length, the phase should to be multiplied by $\lambda_{\rm{EM}}/(2\pi)$. The relationship between the optical path length noise, $n_p$, and the motion requirement for the optics, $n_o$, assumes that there will be two mirrors at \ang{45} which are used to steer the \gls{as} and \gls{lo} beams. As the optics reflect at 45\si{\degree}, the path length change due to the optic's motion is multiplied by $\sqrt{2}$. 
\par
The beams are combined on a beam splitter, and the motion of this beam splitter needs to be counted twice. Figure~\ref{fig:bhdforligochapterbhddoublecounted} illustrates this. The relationship between optical path length, $n_p$, and the motion of each optic, $n_o$, is
\begin{equation}
n_p = 10\times\sqrt{(\sqrt{2}n_o)^2 + (\sqrt{2}n_o)^2 +2\times (\sqrt{2}n_o)^2}.
\end{equation}
Since there are many uncorrelated sources of noise in the LIGO detectors, a factor of 10 is used as a safety margin. 
\par
Multiplying the phase noise shown in Figure~\ref{fig:bhdforligochapterphasenoise} by $\lambda_{\rm{EM}}/(2\pi\times10\sqrt{8})$ gives the upper limit tolerable for the longitudinal motion of the optics. This is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:bhdforligochapterhrtsrequirement}. To determine the amount of isolation required, the typical motion of the optical table needs to be compared to the motion requirement for the optics; see references~\cite{HRTS_pre_concept,HAM_table_motion} for details on this. This led to triple suspensions, known as \gls{ham} relay triple suspensions, being used to passively isolate the optics in the local oscillator and signal beam paths.

	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=1\linewidth]{Figures/BHD_for_ligo_chapter_HRTS_requirement}
	\caption{The sensitivity curve for A+ sets the limit on the amount of path length noise that is acceptable between the \gls{as} and \gls{lo} beams. The blue line shows the sensitivity curve in units of path length noise between the \gls{as} and \gls{lo} beams, and the orange line shows the longitudinal motion requirement for the optics which combine the \gls{lo} and signal beam. At 5\,Hz, the longitudinal motion requirement for the optics is $4.9\times10^{-13}\,\si{\metre/\sqrt{\hertz}}$.} 
	\label{fig:bhdforligochapterhrtsrequirement}
\end{figure}
\begin{figure}[b]
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=0.3\linewidth]{Figures/BHD_for_ligo_chapter_bhd_double_counted}
	\caption{A sketch of the \gls{lo} and signal beam paths. The \gls{lo} beam path is shown in blue, and the signal beam is shown in red. \section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:BHD_for_ligo_conclusion_1}
One of the major changes to the LIGO detectors for A+ will be the implementation of \gls{bhd}. \gls{bhd} is needed for the LIGO detectors to meet their sensitivity target as frequency dependent squeezing requires the homodyne angle to be a free parameter. 
\par
A sketch of how the signal and \gls{lo} will be combined is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:bhdforligochaptersketchoflodelivery}. We derived the relative path length stability requirement for the \gls{lo} and signal beams, and the requirement is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:bhdforligochapterhrtsrequirement}. The local oscillator's phase noise requirement led to \gls{ham} relay triple suspensions being chosen to steer the \gls{lo} and signal beams onto the combining beam splitter. 
%\par
%BHD presents some technical challenges compared to DC readout. The \gls{lo} phase noise must be kept low enough as to not contaminate the signal at the AS port with LO path noise. The requirement for the phase noise is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:bhdforligochapterhrtsrequirement}. This requirement can be met by using triple suspensions to deliver to LO and signal beams to the beam splitter they are combined on.
