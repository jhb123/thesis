The general theory of relativity led to Einstein predicting the existence gravitational waves; however, it was once widely believed that even if gravitational waves did exist, they would be undetectable because they couple weakly to matter. Despite this, with the current generation of gravitational wave interferometers, there have been over seventy direct measurements of a strain induced by a gravitational wave.
\par
Gravitational waves carry information about black holes and neutron stars that cannot be obtained by any other means, thus decades of research have gone into making these interferometers sensitive enough to detect gravitational waves. Improving the sensitivity of these instruments will allow us to measure more accurately the properties of individual signals, have larger datasets to develop statistical models with, and allow us to probe deeper into the universe for more exotic progenitors of gravitational waves. The origins of gravitational waves and their detection by Michelson interferometers are discussed in Chapter~\ref{chapter:Gravitational_wave_interferometers}.

The power of the laser used in the LIGO detectors must be increased if the detectors are to reach their design sensitivity. The target power is 125\,W, but currently the maximum laser power that can be delivered to the LIGO detectors is 70\,W. This power is limited by the single-pass laser amplifier. To increase the maximum power of the LIGO detector's laser, two single-pass amplifiers will be used in series. In Chapter~\ref{chapter:TnT_lab_work}, the assembly and characterisation of this prototype is presented. 
\par
This laser was able to produce over 100\,W of amplitude stabilised light in the fundamental Gaussian mode. Two pieces of hardware for stabilising the light were explored in this chapter, an AOM or a current shunt on a pump diode, and it was found that the AOM was simpler to use over a wider range of powers. Measurements of the pointing noise indicated this noise could couple to the differential arm signal, so when this laser is commission in the PSL room, sources of pointing noise, such as turbulence within the water cooling system, must be minimised. It is planned that this design of laser will be used for LIGO's fourth observation run.
\par
To reach the A+ design sensitivity, 12\,dB of squeezing is planned; therefore, loss in the interferometer must be minimal. Currently, approximately 10\% of the loss in the LIGO Livingston detector is unaccounted for. There is uncertainty in the beam parameter of the arm mode at the signal recycling mirror, and this leads to an uncertainty in the loss due to the mode mismatch between the interferometer and the output mode cleaner. In Chapter~\ref{chapter:simulation_finesse}, the uncertainty in the beam parameter of the arm mode at the signal recycling mirror was expressed in terms of the uncertainty in the radius of curvature of the optics that form the signal recycling mirror. This was done by simulating a detector whose key properties were identical to the LIGO Livingston interferometer. This allowed us to see what beam parameters one may expect to find at the SRM. Based on measurements of the Gouy phase of the signal recycling cavity at LIGO Livingston, the width and defocus of the beam exiting the SRM were inferred to be 1.84\,mm and $-2.80$ Dioptre.
\par
The transition from advanced LIGO to A+ will include using balanced homodyne detection since the homodyne angle must be a free parameter to reach the squeezing target for A+. In Chapter~\ref{chapter:BHD_for_ligo_1}, the essentials of balanced homodyne are described, and the phase noise requirement for the local oscillator is derived. In Chapter~\ref{chapter:BHD_for_ligo_2}, the active wavefront control for mode matching the interferometer and the output mode cleaners is discussed. Calculations of the robustness of the balanced homodyne detector to a mode mismatch were performed, and visualisations of the amount of mode mismatch that is tolerable by the balanced homodyne detector were created. 
\par 
The third-generation of ground-based gravitational wave interferometers will be far more sensitive than the current detectors. Existing detectors are planned to be limited in sensitivity by the Brownian motion of the molecules within the coatings of their mirrors, so cryogenically cooled test masses may be used in future gravitational wave detectors. Test mass technologies that are compatible with cryogenics will work with either 1.5\si{\micro\metre} or 2\si{\micro\metre} light; therefore, hardware which is compatible with the current wavelength of the laser, 1\si{\micro\metre}, will need to be replaced.
\par
Photodiodes for sensing the light containing the gravitational wave signal are one of the key pieces of hardware which has yet to be demonstrated at 2\si{\micro\metre}. In Chapter~\ref{chapter:2um_photoiode_chapter}, off-the-shelf extended InGaAs photodiodes were characterised to see if they meet the unique requirements of a gravitational wave detector. Current off-the-shelf extended InGaAs photodiodes do not have the quantum efficiency, 99\%, needed for detecting gravitational waves, although they can be reverse biased to increase their quantum efficiency. However, when under reverse bias, off-the-shelf extended InGaAs photodiodes can generate enough dark noise to limit the sensitivity of a third-generation interferometer. To make extended InGaAs photodiodes suitable for gravitational wave detectors, research into how to reduce the level of defects in extended InGaAs by optimisation of its growth conditions would need to be made.
