An interferometer converts a change in optical path length into a change in intensity of light. One of the fundamental limits to how precisely the path light takes within an interferometer is the shot noise level of the light. In other words, as shot noise manifests as intensity fluctuations of the light, this is indistinguishable from fluctuations in the differential phase acquired by two interfering beams. In this chapter, the calibration between power measured on a photodiode and the displacement noise of a mach-zehnder interferometer is demonstrated. The results from this experiment serve as an analogy to a BHD readout schemes:  as long as the the local oscillators power is much greater than the signal beams power, the shot noise limit of an interferometer with a BHD readout depends only on the power in the signal beam. 
\section{`Theory'}
The power of the light on each photodiode, as the reflectivity, $R$ and transitivity, $T$, of the beamsplitter were measured to both be $0.5\pm0.01$, is
\begin{align}
%P_A = E^*_A E_A = \left(\frac{E^*_{LO}}{\sqrt{2}}e^{-i\pi} + \frac{E^*_{sig}}{\sqrt{2}}\right)\left(\frac{E_{\rm{LO}}}{\sqrt{2}}e^{i\pi} + \frac{E_{\rm{sig}}}{\sqrt{2}}\right)\\
P_A &= \frac{P_{\rm{LO}}}{2} + \frac{P_{\rm{sig}}}{2} + \sqrt{P_{\rm{sig}}P_{\rm{LO}}}\cos\left(\phi\right),\\
P_B &= \frac{P_{\rm{LO}}}{2} + \frac{P_{\rm{sig}}}{2} + \sqrt{P_{\rm{sig}}P_{\rm{LO}}}\cos\left(\phi + \pi\right),
\end{align}
where $P_{\rm{sig}}$ and $P_{\rm{sig}}$ are the powers of the local oscillator and signal beams and $\phi$ is the phase difference between the local oscillator and signal. Subtracting the two photodiode signals gives  
\begin{equation}
S = P_B-P_A = 2\sqrt{P_{\rm{sig}}P_{\rm{LO}}}\cos\left(\phi\right).
\label{eq:MZ_chapter_base_equation}
\end{equation}
This signal is analogous to the one obtained from a balanced homodyne readout attached to a Michelson interferometer. In another interferometer configuration whose transfer function is $H(f)$, $E_{\rm{sig}}$ would be replaced by $E_{\rm{sig}}/H(f)$.
\paragraph{}
The relationship between optical path length change, $\Delta x$ and a change in power, $\Delta P$, measured by subtracting the Mach-Zehnder's photodiode signals is 
\begin{equation}
\Delta z = \frac{\lambda}{2\pi}\frac{1}{2\sqrt{P_{\rm{sig}}P_{\rm{LO}}}}\Delta S.
\label{eq:MZ_chapter_transfer_function}
\end{equation}
The shot noise of a beam with power $P$ is $\Delta S = \sqrt{2\hbar\omega_{\rm{EM}}P}$. The level of shot noise comes from the total power on each photodiode. If the signal beam's power is much lower than the local oscillator's power, then the shot noise can be expressed as
\begin{equation}
\Delta z = \sqrt{\frac{2hc\lambda}{16\pi^2P_{\rm{sig}}}}
\label{eq:MZ_chapter_shot_noise_level}
\end{equation}
\section{Apparatus}
The in-air mach-zehnder is shown in Figure \ref{fig:mzchapterinairmz} and Figure \ref{fig:mzchapterinairsketch}. It was constructed from highly stable rigid mounts to reduce the amount of mount-vibration noise. The MZ was mounted on a thick aluminium base-plate which was isolated from the optical table by rubber dampeners. The MZ was acoustically shielded with a plastic box of a few mm thickness. As the beam size within the MZ was suffieicnetly small,  the light power in one arm was attenuated with an ND filter as surface roughness of the ND filter did not matter. One of the corner mirrors in the MZ was glued to a PZT to allow for the length of one of the paths to be controlled. The light that emerged from the MZ was measured with the photodiode circuit shown in Appendix \ref{photodiode circuit.}. The response of this photodiode circuit is flat at all frequencies of interest and is 390\,V/W at DC.
\paragraph{}
A half wave-plate and polarised beam splitter were used to control the power entering the MZ and the polarisation of the light entering the MZ. The input laser power was monitored with a photodiode. A microphone were used to monitor the environmental noise sources.
\paragraph{}
The photodiode signals were recorded with CDS (see Appendix \ref{}). To send signals long distances across the lab, differential send-recieves circuits, constructed with the THAT range of IC, were used to remove common-mode noise that may be picked up along cable. The overall factor which converts the recorded digital signal into a voltage is determined by the number of bits used in the ADC (16), the peak-to-peak voltage that the ADC can record, 20\,V, and the factor of 2 that comes from the differential send-receive circuit: the factor is therefore 20/(2*65536).
\paragraph{}
To keep the measured signal above the digitisation noise inherent to the analogue to digital converters used in CDS,  whitening filters are required. This is a circuit which boosts the AC components of a signal while keeping the DC level of the signal small enough as to not saturate the ADC. A dewhitening filter is a digital filter whose transfer function is the reciprocal of the whitening filter's. In addition to this, CDS features an anti-aliasing filter which will effect measurements that are made near the Nyquist frequency of the ADC. This response of the AA filter can be seen in figure \ref{fig:mzchaptercdsnoise}. CDS noise is shown in figure \ref{fig:mzchaptercdsnoise}. For this photodiode circuit, the level of shot noise due to 20\,mW of light is  $\sim3\times10^{-8}\,\rm{V_{rms}/\sqrt{Hz}}$ and so the whitening filter needs to boost the photodiode signal by a factor of 100.

\section{Shot noise measurement}
The MZ was locked and the noise measured by both photodiodes was recorded via CDS. The measured signal corresponds to the level of shot noise predicted by equation \ref{eq:MZ_chapter_shot_noise_level} at frequencies where classical noise sources no longer dominate the signal. To demonstrate that the signal depends on the differential path length of the MZ, a modulation at 6033\,Hz was applied to the PZT. This can be seen in the spectrum shown in figure \ref{fig:mzchapterfullnoisespectrum}. By analogy between PZT motion and noise level, for a 100\,Hz measurement bandwidth, differential path length changes smaller than $5\times10^{-14}\,\rm{m_{rms}}$ cannot be measured as the measurement will be limited by the power of the signal beam.

\section{In-Vacuum work}
To measure shot noise at frequencies around 100\,Hz, the frequency band of interest in a gravitational wave detector, the acoustic and seismic noise noise which shake the mirrors need to be reduced. This can be achieved by using suspended optics and performing the experiment in-vacuum. There was an opportunity to perform this experiment in-vacuum with left-over apparatus from a previous experiment. This proved challenging for as the interferometer was hard to lock due to large amounts of seismic motion coupling to the differential arm length. Ultimately, scattered light interfering with the differential arm signal meant that shot noise could not be measured at any frequency. However, this apparatus could be used to verify that an angular misalignment, $\theta$, will cause the signal, $S$, to drop from its maximum value, $S_0$, as
\begin{equation}
S_{\rm{sig}} = S_0 e^{-\left(\theta/\theta_c\right)^2},
\end{equation}
where $\theta_c = \sqrt{2}\lambda_{\rm{EM}}/\pi w_0 $ is the characteristic angle of the a beam of waist $w_0$.

\subsection{Servo calibration}
The differential arm length needed controlling below the suspension main resonance, ~ $\sim1$\,Hz as the optics moved on the order of one wavelength at these frequencies. Due to the design of the of the balanced homodyne readout, there are lots of suspension-related modes that couple to the longitudinal signal -- these are around 30\,Hz. This is made extra bad by the optical bench -- this moved around a large amount due to poor seismic isolation made the optical table move around more than the floor at these frequencies. This combination of factors meant that the differential arm length noise was at a similar level at $\sim30\,Hz$ as it was at $\sim1\,Hz$. The suspended mirror was actuated on with a magnet+coil to lock the MZ arm length, this meant that the actuators strength fell as $1/f^2$ above the suspensions resonance, meaning that the coil would need to drive a signal that was provide a large signal at $1$\,Hz, as well as one that was $~1000$ times greater at $\sim30$\,Hz. The magnet+coil strength was limited by geometry (size of the mirror, size of the magnet, size of the coil, amount of current that the coil could have etc) and so was not strong enough to do this. This meant that the unity gain point needed to be between $1$ and $10$ Hz. The servo was design to: achieve a unity gain point just above $\sim1$\,Hz so that the main suspension resonance could be prevented from unlocking the MZ, and filtering out the $\sim30$\,Hz modes from making using up the dynamic range of the servo.
