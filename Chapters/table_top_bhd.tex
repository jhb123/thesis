An interferometer converts a change in optical path length into a change in intensity of light. One of the fundamental limits to how precisely the path light takes within an interferometer is the shot noise level of the light. In other words, as shot noise manifests as intensity fluctuations of the light, this is indistinguishable from fluctuations in the differential phase acquired by two interfering beams...
\section{`Theory'}
The power of the light on each photodiode, as the reflectivity, $R$ and transitivity, $T$, of the beamsplitter were measured to both be $0.5\pm0.01$, is
\begin{align}
%P_A = E^*_A E_A = \left(\frac{E^*_{LO}}{\sqrt{2}}e^{-i\pi} + \frac{E^*_{sig}}{\sqrt{2}}\right)\left(\frac{E_{\rm{LO}}}{\sqrt{2}}e^{i\pi} + \frac{E_{\rm{sig}}}{\sqrt{2}}\right)\\
P_A &= \frac{P_{\rm{LO}}}{2} + \frac{P_{\rm{sig}}}{2} + \sqrt{P_{\rm{sig}}P_{\rm{LO}}}\cos\left(\phi\right),\\
P_B &= \frac{P_{\rm{LO}}}{2} + \frac{P_{\rm{sig}}}{2} + \sqrt{P_{\rm{sig}}P_{\rm{LO}}}\cos\left(\phi + \pi\right),
\end{align}
where $P_{\rm{sig}}$ and $P_{\rm{sig}}$ are the powers of the local oscillator and signal beams and $\phi$ is the phase difference between the local oscillator and signal. Subtracting the two photodiode signals gives  
\begin{equation}
S = P_B-P_A = 2\sqrt{P_{\rm{sig}}P_{\rm{LO}}}\cos\left(\phi\right).
\label{eq:MZ_chapter_base_equation}
\end{equation}
This signal is analogous to the one obtained from a balanced homodyne readout attached to a Michelson interferometer. In another interferometer configuration whose transfer function is $H(f)$, $E_{\rm{sig}}$ would be replaced by $E_{\rm{sig}}/H(f)$.
\paragraph{}
The relationship between optical path length change, $\Delta x$ and a change in power, $\Delta P$, measured by subtracting the Mach-Zehnder's photodiode signals is 
\begin{equation}
\Delta z = \frac{\lambda}{2\pi}\frac{1}{2\sqrt{P_{\rm{sig}}P_{\rm{LO}}}}\Delta S.
\label{eq:MZ_chapter_transfer_function}
\end{equation}
For shot noise, $\Delta S = \sqrt{2\hbar\omega_{\rm{EM}}}$. The level of shot noise comes from the total power on each photodiode.
\section{Apparatus}
The in-air mach-zehnder is shown in Figure \ref{fig:mzchapterinairmz} and Figure \ref{fig:mzchapterinairsketch}. It was constructed from highly stable rigid mounts to reduce the amount of mount-vibration noise. The MZ was mounted on a thick aluminium base-plate which was isolated from the optical table by rubber dampeners. The MZ was acoustically shielded with a plastic box of a few mm thickness. As the beam size within the MZ was suffieicnetly small,  the light power in one arm was attenuated with an ND filter as surface roughness of the ND filter did not matter. One of the corner mirrors in the MZ was glued to a PZT to allow for the length of one of the paths to be controlled. The light that emerged from the MZ was measured with the photodiode circuit shown in Appendix \ref{photodiode circuit.}. The response of this photodiode circuit is flat at all frequencies of interest and is 390\,V/W at DC.
\paragraph{}
A half wave-plate and polarised beam splitter were used to control the power entering the MZ and the polarisation of the light entering the MZ. The input laser power was monitored with a photodiode. A microphone were used to monitor the environmental noise sources.

\subsection{Calibration of In-Air Mach-Zehnder}
The shot noise level can can be found by summing $P_A$ and $P_B$. The power was  measured to be 11\,mW, so $\Delta S = 5.4\times10^{-11}\,\rm{W}/\sqrt{\rm{Hz}}$(\href{https://arran.physics.gla.ac.uk/wp/qnd-readout/2019/03/14/sum-and-difference-spectral-densities/}{labbook post with data}).

\paragraph{}
The Mach-Zehnder can be calibrated by measuring $P_{\rm{sig}}$ and $P_{\rm{LO}}$. The power in the LO path was measured to be 20\,mW (\href{https://arran.physics.gla.ac.uk/wp/qnd-readout/2019/03/08/changed-injection-signal-to-mz-hooked-up-mic-and-power-monitor-photodiode/}{LO and Sig power measurement labbook post}), and the power in the lower path before the beam passed through the ND filter was measured to be 17\,mW. After the ND filter (\href{https://arran.physics.gla.ac.uk/wp/qnd-readout/2019/02/13/mz-nd-filter-install-servo-and-sends-saturating/}{ND filter labbook post}), the power would be 0.1\,mW. This is shown in figure \ref{fig:mzchapterinaircalibration}.
\paragraph{}
Alternatively, the Mach-Zehnder can be calibrated by measuring $\Delta S/\Delta z$ by applying a ramp signal to the PZT. The ramp signal and the photodiode signals were recorded on an oscilloscope. The photodiode signals can were then plotted against the measured ramp signal, and data was fit with Equation \ref{eq:MZ_chapter_base_equation}. This is shown in figure \ref{fig:mzchapterinaircalibration}.
\paragraph{}
Although there is a 20\% descrepency between the two calibrations, both methods of calibrating the response of the Mach-Zehnder give consistent results within margin of error determined by the quantisation noise associated with the oscilloscope and the uncertainty of the signal beam's power.

\section{Shot noise measurement}
The MZ was locked and the noise measured by both photodiodes was recorded via CDS (see appendix \ref{}). To keep the measured signal above the digitisation noise inherent to the analogue to digital converters used in CDS,  Whitening filters are required. This is a circuit which boosts the AC components of a signal while keeping the DC level of the signal small enough to not saturate the ADC. This filter must be corrected for in post processing. In addition to this, CDS features an anti-aliasing filter which will effect measurements that are made near the Nyquist frequency of the ADC. To send signals long distances across the lab, differential send-recieves circuits are used to remove common-mode noise that may be picked up by the cable.
