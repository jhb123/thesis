\section{The Fundamental Gaussian Mode}
The simplest laser beam profile can be described by the fundamental Gaussian mode. This is a mode which has a circular intensity profile which follows a Gaussian distribution, and its surface of constant phase is spherical. As the phase fronts are spherical, Gaussian beams describe lasers in optical layouts which have spherically curved optics. The fundamental Gaussian mode is given by
\begin{equation}
E(\mathbf{r}) = E_0\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}} \frac{1}{w(z)} \exp\big(i\phi(z)\big)\exp\left( -ik\frac{x^2 + y^2}{2R_C(z)} - \frac{x^2 + y^2}{w^2(z)}\right)\exp\big(-ikz\big).
\label{eq:gaussian_mode}
\end{equation}
\par
Gaussian beams are characterised by the size of their waist, $w_0$. The width of a Gaussian beam at a position $z$ along the optical axis is represented by $w(z)$, and $w(0) = w_0$. Gaussian beams are also characterised by $\phi(z)$, which gives the Gouy phase of the beam at $z$ along the optical axis, and by $R_c(z)$, which gives radius of curvature of the beam at $z$.
\par
It is useful to express $w(z)$, $\phi(z)$ and $R_c(z)$ in terms of the Rayleigh range, $z_{\rm{R}}$. The Rayleigh range characterises how fast the beam will spread out. The size of the waist determines the Rayleigh range, a smaller beam waist corresponds to a shorter Rayleigh range. The Rayleigh range is given by
\begin{equation}
z_{\rm{R}} = \frac{\pi w_0^2}{\lambda}.
\end{equation} 
\par
The width of the beam as it propagates along the optical axis can be written as
\begin{equation}
	w(z) = w_0\sqrt{1 + \left(\frac{z}{z_{\rm{R}}}\right)^2}.
	\label{eq:beam_size}
\end{equation} 
In the far field ($z>z_{\rm{R}}$), Equation~(\ref{eq:beam_size}) becomes linear and the beam size can be approximated as 
\begin{equation}
w(z) \approx w_0\frac{z}{z_{\rm{R}}}.
\end{equation}
\par
As a Gaussian beam propagates along the optical axis, it acquires an additional phase shift compared to a plane wave. This is known as the Gouy phase shift, $\phi(z)$, and it given by
\begin{equation}
	\phi(z) = \tan^{-1}\frac{z}{z_{\rm{R}}}.
\end{equation}
\par
The radius of curvature, $R_c(z)$, is given by
\begin{equation}
R_c(z) = z + \frac{z_{\rm{R}}^2}{z}.
\end{equation}
The inverse of a beam's radius of curvature is known as its defocus, $S(z) = 1/R_c(z)$. The defocus of a beam is useful as it is easier to compute the effects of a beam interacting with a focussing element of an optical layout in terms of the defocus than it is with the radius of curvature.
\subsection{The Complex Beam Parameter}
The fundamental Gaussian mode can be expressed in terms of a complex beam parameter, $q(z)$. This parameter is useful for calculating how a Gaussian beam is transformed by an optical system. From this parameter, all the physical quantities related to a Gaussian beam can be computed. The complex beam parameter is defined as
\begin{equation}
q(z) = z + iz_{\rm{R}}.
\label{eq:eq_q}
\end{equation}
\par
The inverse of the complex beam parameter can be written in terms of the radius of curvature and width of the beam. This is given by
\begin{equation}
\frac{1}{q(z)} = \frac{1}{R(z)} - i\frac{\lambda}{\pi w^2(z)}.
\end{equation} 
Equation~(\ref{eq:gaussian_mode}) can be rewritten in terms of the complex beam parameter,
\begin{equation}
E(\mathbf{r}) = E_0\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}} \frac{q_0}{w_0q(z)} \exp\big(i\phi(z)\big)\exp\left( -ik\frac{x^2 + y^2}{2q(z)}\right)\exp\big(-ikz\big).
\end{equation}
The width of the beam, Gouy phase, and the defocus at a point $z$ along the optical axis can be calculated from $q(z)$ using
\begin{align}
w(z) & = \sqrt{\frac{\lambda}{-\pi\Im\left(\frac{1}{q(z)}\right)}},\label{eq:beam_radius}\\
\phi(z) &= \tan^{-1}\left(\frac{\Re\big(q(z)\big)}{\Im\big(q(z)\big)}\right),\label{eq:beam_gouy}\\
S(z) &= \Re\left( \frac{1}{q(z)} \right).\label{eq:beam_defocus}
\end{align}
\subsection{Astigmatism}
If a beam has an elliptical profile, then the beam is astigmatic. The simplest type of astigmatism can be described with two complex Gaussian beam parameters, one for the yz (tangential) plane and one for the xz (saggital) plane. A Gaussian beam with simple astigmatism will have a different waist size and location in the tangential and sagittal planes. This type of astigmatism can arise due to Gaussian beams passing through a cylindrical lens or by them having a non-zero angle of incidence to a mirror. 
\par
An astigmatic Gaussian beam can be split up into two orthogonal fields,
\begin{align}
E(\mathbf{r}) &= E(x,z)E(y,z) \nonumber\\ &= E_0\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}} \sqrt{\frac{q_{x0}}{w_{x0}q_{x}(z)}}\sqrt{\frac{q_{y0}}{w_{y0}q_{y}(z)}} \exp\big(i\phi_{\rm{Av}}(z)\big) \nonumber\\ &\times\exp\left( -ik\frac{x^2}{2q_{x}(z)} -ik\frac{y^2}{2q_{y}(z)}\right)\exp\big(-ikz\big).
\end{align}
where $\exp\big(i\phi_{\rm{Av}}(z)\big)$ is the average of the Gouy phase in the saggital and tangential planes. The equations for waist size, Gouy phase and defocus remain the same (Equation~(\ref{eq:beam_radius}), Equation~(\ref{eq:beam_gouy}) and Equation~(\ref{eq:beam_defocus})). A beam with astigmatism can be thought of as two independent beams, and their interaction with the optical layout can be calculated independently.
\section{Gaussian Beams Traversing Optical Layouts}
\label{sec:gaussian_beam_tracing}
As a Gaussian beam propagates through an optical layout, the beam parameter that describes it changes. The effect of simple optical components can be modelled with ABCD matrices, and an entire layout can be modelled by multiplying a series of ABCD matrices together. This formalism can also be used to compute cavity eigenmodes.
\par
When a beam interacts with an optical component, it is transformed from a mode described by $q_1$ to a new mode $q_2$ according to 
\begin{equation}
q_2 = \frac{A q_1 +B}{C q_1 + D}.
\end{equation}
The coefficients $A,B,C,D$ are defined by an ABCD matrix,
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{M} = \begin{pmatrix} A &B\\	C &D \end{pmatrix}.
\end{equation}
\par
The beam parameter of a laser as it passes through the first component to the $n^{\rm{th}}$ component can be calculated with the matrix obtained from multiplying the ABCD matrices of the components together,
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{M} = \mathbf{M_n}\mathbf{M_{n-1}}\ldots\mathbf{M_0}.
\end{equation}
Note, the left most matrix corresponds to the last optical component in the layout. The ABCD matrices for various components is summarised in table \ref{tab:optical_components_ABCD}.
\begin{table}
	\centering
	\begin{tabular}{|c | c |}
		\hline 
		Optical Component & ABCD Matrix \\
		\hline & \\
		space & $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & d \\ 0 &1\end{pmatrix}$ \\ & \\
		\hline & \\
		Mirror or lens (normal incidence) & $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ -S & 1\end{pmatrix}$\\ & \\
		Mirror (AOI = $\alpha$, Sagittal Plane) & $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ -\frac{S}{\cos\alpha} & 1\end{pmatrix}$\\ & \\
		Mirror (AOI = $\alpha$, Tangential Plane) & $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ -S\cos\alpha & 1\end{pmatrix}$\\ & \\
		\hline
	\end{tabular}

\label{tab:optical_components_ABCD}
\end{table}
\section{Higher Order Modes and Optical Imperfections}
The fundamental Gaussian mode describes the electric field of a beam in an ideal optical system. Imperfections in the optical layout, such as misalignment, mode mismatch and spatial distortions in mirror surfaces, can generate light with a different intensity profile to the fundamental Gaussian mode. For instance, if two interfering beams are misaligned slightly, the resulting beam will have a mode profile that looks like two spots instead of one. A small difference in waist size or location, i.e. a mode mismatch, between them would result in the beam having a ring-shaped mode profile. Similar effects happen when a laser and a cavity are misaligned. These imperfections in the layout and the resulting beam shapes can be modelled as the optical layout scattering light into higher order optical modes (e.g~\cite{Morrison1994}). 
\par
One complete, orthonormal basis set of functions that can be used to construct higher order modes with are the Hermite-Gaussian (HG) modes. These modes have $xy$ symmetry and are rectangularly shaped. HG modes are useful for modelling misalignments because this type of imperfection can be naturally broken into $xy$ coordinates.
\par
Mode mismatches are best described by the $r\theta$ coordinates, and so it is natural to express these in terms of another orthonormal basis set known as the Laguerre-Gauss (LG) modes. However, if the beam is astigmatic then the mode no longer has the symmetry required in order to be modelled with LG modes. Second order HG modes can also describe mode mismatch, even if the modes are astigmatic.
\par
The spatial distribution of a higher order mode's field amplitude can be described with
\begin{equation}
\Psi_{nm}(x,y,z) = \Psi_n(x,z)\Psi_m(y,z).
\end{equation}
The functions $\Psi_{n}$ have the form
\begin{multline}
\Psi_n(x,z) = \left(\frac{2}{\pi}\right)^{1/4} \left(\frac{\exp\big(i(2n+1)\phi(z)\big)}{2^nn!w(z)}\right)^{1/2}\\ H_n\left(\frac{\sqrt{2}x}{w(z)}\right)\exp\left(-i\frac{kx^2}{2R_c(z)} - i\frac{x^2}{w^2(z)}\right),
\end{multline}
where $H_n(x)$ a Hermite polynomial of the order n. By writing $\Psi_{nm}(x,y,z)$ out explicitly,
\begin{multline}
\Psi_{nm}(x,y,z) = \left(2^{(n+m-1)}n!m!\pi \right)^{1/2} \frac{1}{w(z)}\exp\big(i(n + m + 1)\phi(z)\big) \\ H_n\left(\frac{\sqrt{2}x}{w(z)}\right) H_m\left(\frac{\sqrt{2}x}{w(z)}\right) \exp\left(-ik\frac{x^2+y^2}{2R_c(z)} - i\frac{x^2 + y^2}{w^2(z)}\right),
\end{multline}
we can see that higher order modes have the same waist size and radius of curvature as the fundamental mode, but different intensity distributions and additional Gouy phase.
%\par
%The eigenmode of a cavity is determined by its length and the radius of curvature of its end mirrors. The Gaussian mode that is the eigenmode of the cavity will have the same radius of curvature at the end mirrors of the cavity as the mirrors themselves. As higher order modes share the same radius of curvature as the fundamental mode, these modes can also be resonant within the cavity. What separates these modes out in a cavity is the round trip Gouy phase that is acquired. Mode cleaner cavities are designed such that the round trip Gouy phase is such that only one mode can be resonant at once, and all the other modes destructively interfere. 
%\par
%The Gouy phase separation of higher order modes is important when designing alignment and mode matching control systems, as different types of errors have different phases. For example, angular and translational misalignment are both first order effects. This means they both transfer light from the $HG_{00}$ mode into the $HG_{10}$. However, the complex amplitude of these misalignment situations is separated by $\pi/2$ rad of phase. Having two sensors separated by $\pi/2$ of Gouy phase, one can derive alignment control signals.
\par
In general, a mode can be written as a superposition of HG modes 
\begin{equation}
E(x,y,z) = \sum_{n=0}^{n=\infty}\sum_{m=0}^{m=\infty}A_n \Psi_n(x)A_m \Psi_m(y),
\end{equation}
where $A_n$ is the amplitude of the $n^{\rm{th}}$ mode.
