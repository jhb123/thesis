The detection of gravitational waves is vital for developing our understanding of the Universe. Gravitational waves interact with matter extremely weakly, so kilometre-scale interferometers, such as the LIGO detectors, are the only instruments which have directly measured the strain in space-time that is induced by them. Increasing the sensitivity of these detectors is vital for improving our understanding of the sources of gravitational waves. 
\par
To reduce the quantum shot noise of the LIGO detectors, they require a low noise, high power laser. This thesis contains experimental characterisation of the prototype for the laser that will be used during LIGO's fourth observation run. This laser was measured to generate over 100\,W of amplitude stabilised light in the HG00 mode, thus it is an important step towards reaching the design sensitivity of the LIGO detectors.
\par
Balanced homodyne detection is a key part of the upgrade from advanced LIGO to LIGO A+. To lower the quantum noise of the detectors by harnessing the quantum nature of light, it is crucial that the balanced homodyne detector has minimal loss. Mode mismatch between the interferometer and the output mode cleaners is a source of loss; therefore, active optics for mode matching between the interferometer and the output mode cleaner will be used. In this thesis, the robustness of these optics to mode mismatches was analysed.
\par
Third generation gravitational wave detectors, such as the Einstein Telescope and LIGO cosmic explorer, will be far more sensitive and be able to probe far deeper into the Universe than the current generation of detectors. The increase in sensitivity may be achieved with cryogenically cooled test masses, and these test masses may compatible with a laser with a wavelength of 2\si{\micro\metre} instead of the 1\si{\micro\metre} wavelength that is currently used in interferometers. High quantum efficiency photodiodes are required if the detector's quantum noise is to be minimal, so Off-the-shelf extended InGaAs photodiodes where characterised in the context of the unique requirements of a gravitational wave detector. It was found that current off-the-shelf extended InGaAs will not be suitable for third generation detectors. Significant amounts of research into the optimal conditions for manufacturing extended InGaAs photodiodes would be needed before using extended InGaAs photodiodes in a third generation detector is viable.
