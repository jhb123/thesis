\section{Transimpedance Amplifier for Measuring the Dark Noise of Photodiodes}
\label{sec:appendix_dark_noise_tia}
A circuit which allowed for the reverse bias to be set and for the easy exchange of photodiodes was constructed. To measure small amounts of current noise, a transimpedance amplifier with a large gain is needed. As the dark current for these diodes is of the order of 1\si{\milli\ampere}, the gain of the transimpedance amplifier was chosen to be $1\times10^6$ so that signals of the order 1\,V would be produced, and so a 1\,\si{\mega\ohm} resistor was used as in the feedback path.
\paragraph{}
Operation amplifiers have current noise at their inputs, voltage noise at the their outputs and the feedback resistor will generate Johnson-Nyquist noise (e.g.~\cite[Figure 8.58]{Horowitz_and_Hill}). Designers of OP amps, generally, improve the OP amps output voltage noise at the expense of the input current noise or vice versa and so there is not one-size-fits-all op amp. In this application, as the current noise that is to be measured is small and the feedback resistor is large, a TL071, a JFET style op amp with low input current noise, was selected. As the photodiode has capacitance and the op amp has finite bandwidth, a 2.2\si{\pico\farad} capacitor in parallel to keep the circuit stable.
\paragraph{}

\subsection{Transfer function}
The transfer function of the transimpedance amplifiers shown in Figure \ref{fig:circuitappendixtransimpedanceamplifiertf} was measured under a variety of different physical constructions. This circuit's behaviour can be affected by stray capacitance. When constructed from strip board and with the photodiode anode and cathode cables very near each other, there was of the order 10\,pF of stray capacitance i.e. it was as if the circuit had an extra 10\,pF capacitor between the inverting input of the op amp and ground. By making this circuit on a prototyping board with less stray capacitance between the strips and by keeping the photodiode anode and cathode wires from forming a capacitor, the stray capacitance was reduced. The measurement is in agreement with the \textsc{LISO} simulation of this circuit. This was cross checked using the method described in Section \ref{sec:appendix_TIA_TF}.

\paragraph{}
To avoid oscillations due to unusually large output impedance of the AD797, in similar circuits to one shown in Figure \ref{fig:pdchapterqetestcircuit}, the feedback capacitor may need a resistor in series with it (see \cite[p.14]{AD797}); in this circuit, one was not required. Every time a version of this circuit was made, an oscilloscope was used to check for such oscillations as LISO does not model this behaviour.

\section{Simulations with \textsc{Liso}}
The behaviour of circuits was simulated using \textsc{Liso}. This software performs a nodal analysis to calculate the voltages at each point within a circuit. \textsc{Liso} can calculate the noise and transfer function of circuits that include op amps. \textsc{Liso} has a wide variety of op amps in its op amp library and this can be easily expanded by specifying the noise properties, open loop gain and gain-bandwidth frequency.
\section{Transfer Function Calculation With \textsc{Matlab}}
\label{sec:appendix_TIA_TF}
The open loop transfer function of an op amp is
\begin{equation}
A(s) = \frac{A_0}{\frac{A_0}{2\pi f_{\rm{GWB}}}s +1}.
\end{equation}
The transfer function between the input current to the inverting input and the voltage at the output pin for an op amp made into a transimpedance amplifier is
\begin{equation}
H(s) = Z_{\rm{FB}}\frac{A(s)}{1+A(s)B(s)},
\end{equation}
where $B(s)$ is the transfer function of the components between the output pin of the op amp and the inverting input of the op amp and $Z_{\rm{FB}}$ is the impedance seen by the current flowing from the output pin to the inverting input. Without a capacitor in parallel with the feedback resistor $B(s) = \frac{1}{R_{\rm{FB}}C_{\rm{PD}}s+1}$. This can be problematic as the transfer function of the circuit may have a phase of $180\si{\degree}$ due to the photodiode's capacitance and the gain bandwidth product of the op amp as it crosses the unity gain point which will cause the circuit to be unstable. To fix this problem, a capacitor should be added in parallel to the feedback resistor, and so 
\begin{equation}
B(s) = \frac{R_{\rm{FB}}C_{\rm{FB}}s + 1}{R_{\rm{FB}}(C_{\rm{FB}}+C_{\rm{PD}})s + 1}.
\end{equation}
The impedance seen by the current is 
\begin{equation}
Z_{\rm{FB}} = \frac{R_{\rm{FB}}}{R_{\rm{FB}}(C_{\rm{FB}}+C_{\rm{PD}})s + 1}.
\end{equation}
$H(s)$ can be calculated with the following \textsc{Matlab} code:
\begin{lstlisting}
function [mag,phase] = photodiodeTransimpedance(cf, rf,cpd,gbw,A,w)
% cf is the capacitor in the feedback loop used to keep the circuit
% stable, and its units are Farads. rf is the feedback resistor
% measured in Ohms. cpd is the photodiodes capacitance (Farads). gbw is the
% gain bandwidth of the OP amp, and is measured in Hz. A is the Open loop
% gian of the OP amp. w is the angular frequency range that
% the phase and magnitude will be calculated over. 
A_s = tf(A,[A/(2*pi*gbw),1]);
B_s = tf([cf*rf,1],[rf*(cf+cpd),1]);
closedLoopAnalytic  = -tf(rf,[rf*(cf+cpd),1])*A_s/(1+A_s*B_s);
[mag,phase] = bode(closedLoopAnalytic,w);
mag = squeeze(mag);
phase = squeeze(phase);
end
\end{lstlisting}
