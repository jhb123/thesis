\section{Introduction}
The LIGO detectors require high input power, nominally 125\,\si{\watt}, at 1064\,\si{\nano\metre} to decrease the shot noise which limits its sensitivity at frequencies above 100\,\si{\hertz}. If a laser is in a coherent state, the shot noise associated with detecting the laser is $\sigma = \sqrt{\frac{hc}{\lambda}P}$, so the signal to noise ratio at high frequencies scales with $\sqrt{P}$. Alternatively, to decrease the shot noise limit the wavelength could be decreased; however, the LIGO detectors operate at 1064\,\si{\nano\metre} due to the availability of mirror coatings with low absorption and low thermal noise. In fact, future interferometers may operate at longer wavelengths due to the potential reduction of mirror coating thermal noise that can be achieved at longer wavelengths \cite{Craig2019}.
\paragraph{}
In the field of gravitational wave detection, high power refers to lasers with powers of the order 100\,\si{\watt}. High power is a loosely defined term as lasers ranging from 100\,\si{\watt} to 100\,\si{\kilo\watt} are defined as high power in the literature \cite{Shcherbakov2013,Lee2019,Huang2019,Ma2016,Robin2014,Dixneuf2020,Winkelmann2011,Wellmann2019,Buikema2019,Bode2020,Thies2019}. As the power is increased, other properties of the laser tend to be degraded. It needs to operate uninterrupted on year-long timescales; this poses a major engineering challenge. 
\paragraph{}
The laser for LIGO must be continuous wave, linearly polarised, have high spatial purity, low intensity noise and low frequency noise. These requirements are detailed in \cite{PSL_requirements}. To overcome the technical challenges that come with the high power and low noise requirements for the LIGO laser, the master-oscillator-power-amplifier (MOPA) topology was selected. This type of laser is based on a high performance seed laser which is then amplified. As amplifiers are linear and coherent, the low noise properties of the seed laser characterises the noise of the high power beam.
\paragraph{}
LIGO's original laser used a high power oscillator (HPO) as the means for obtaining the target power. The HPO was a ring cavity containing laser crystals that was seeded by a 35\,\si{\watt} low noise laser. This design went through extensive testing and refinement at the AEI \cite{Kwee2012} and was installed as part of LIGO's pre-stabilised-laser (PSL). However, due to jitter noise from the water cooling of the crystals within the HPO coupling to the differential arm motion signal (DARM), as well as one HPO being destroyed by accident, the HPO was bypassed and the LIGO detectors operated using the 35\,\si{\watt} laser \cite{InstrumentWhitePaper2019}.
%100\,kW \cite{Shcherbakov2013} lasers are used in the welding. 365\,\si{\watt} \cite{Dixneuf2020}. 811\,\si{\watt} \cite{Robin2014}. 1.89\,kW \cite{Ma2016}. 200W \cite{Wellmann2019}. 220\,\si{\watt} \cite{Winkelmann2011}. 178\,\si{\watt} \cite{Buikema2019}. 2.19\,kW \cite{Huang2019}. 2.05\,kW \cite{Lee2019}.
\paragraph{}
To reach higher powers during LIGO's third observation run, instead of the HPO, a neoVAN-4S single-pass amplifier was used. This stepped up the laser power from 35\,\si{\watt} to 70\,\si{\watt} \cite{spencer_thesis}. The next stage of development was a complete redesign of the LIGO laser based on using two upgraded versions of the neoVAN-4S. The neoVAN-4S-HP amplifier showed promising results \cite{Thies2019,Bode2020}, so a prototype laser was constructed and tested at LIGO Livingston. This design will be installed as the new PSL for the start of LIGO's fourth observation run \cite{InstrumentWhitePaper2019}.
\paragraph{}
In this chapter the new laser layout is discussed. The PMC throughput power, beam quality, intensity noise, pointing noise and frequency noise of the prototype laser were measured. It was found that this layout provided a suitably low noise laser. This laser was able to produce 100\,\si{\watt} of intensity stabilised light. While the 125\,\si{\watt} nominal power level was not obtained, additional work in optimising the layout may improve the maximum output power of this laser.

\section{Concept for the New PSL}
The new design of the PSL is based on using an NPRO with two single-pass amplifiers in series, followed by a cavity to provide 125W of laser light with high spatial purity and low noise. A sketch of this concept is shown in figure \ref{fig:ligolaserchapterconcept}. A seed laser followed by one or more amplifiers is known as a master-oscillator-power-amplifier (MOPA) configuration. Currently, the PSL is seeded by a 35W laser which is followed by one amplifier \cite{spencer_thesis,InstrumentWhitePaper2019}. Results from the high performance version of this amplifier were promising \cite{Thies2019}, leading to a proposal to construct the PSL out of two of these in series. A similar set up has been constructed \cite{Bode2020}.

\subsection{The NPRO as the Master Oscillator}
The first element of the PSL is a non-planar ring oscillator laser \cite{Kane1985,Freitag1995,Heurs2004}. The NPRO provides a low noise laser making it a suitable seed laser in a MOPA laser. This is a single frequency laser constructed from one Nd:YAG crystal which acts as both the gain medium and the cavity, thus it produces a laser with high amounts of frequency stability. The NPRO used is a Mephisto laser, built by Coherent, and it can produce to 2W of 1064\,\si{\nano\metre} light.
\subsection{neoVAN-4S-HP solid-state Amplifier}
To amplify the NPRO beam, two neoVAN-4S-HP amplifiers will be used. The neoVAN-4S-HP is a single-pass, solid state amplifier  manufactured by Neolase. It is constructed from four $\rm{Nd:YVO_4}$ crystals which are pumped by fibre coupled laser diodes. It should be seeded by s-polarised light at 1064\,nm. It is cooled via a plate with water (or other coolants) flowing through it. This keeps jitter noise low as flowing water is not directly in contact with the gain medium. As these amplifiers will be seeded by an NPRO, the light produced by them will take on the noise characteristics of the NPRO. 
\subsection{The Pre Mode Cleaner Cavity}
The pre mode cleaner (PMC), see Figure \ref{fig:ligo_layout_chapter_layout}, is used as the hand-off point between the PSL and the rest of the interferometer. Light transmitted by this cavity is used as the input beam for the interferometer. The PMC is a bow tie cavity with an FSR of 9\,\si{\mega\hertz}, thus it passively filters RF intensity noise. Control side bands are provided after the PMC but before the IMC, meaning that the IMC cannot be used to provide this stabilisation. The PMC is locked to the fundamental mode via a PDH lock, so the beam that is delivered to the rest of the interferometer has a low amount of higher order mode content. This is important as the amplifiers upstream of the PMC produce slightly elliptical beams. The PMC can be considered as a reference beam parameter and a hand-off point between the PSL and the rest of the interferometer. The PMC has been redesigned \cite{new_pmc_T1700543} to prevent contamination due to glue on its mirrors and to make maintaining the PMC easier.

\section{The PMC Output Power Reached by the Prototype Layout}
The PSL should deliver 125\,\si{\watt} to the interferometer \cite{PSL_requirements}. There are several variables that will affect the amount of power that is transmitted through the PMC: the flow rate through the amplifiers heads; the input alignment to each amplifier; the mode matching to each amplifier; the mode matching to the PMC; the alignment to the PMC; the pump diodes current and the polarisation of the light that is to be amplified. These are not all independent of each other. For instance, changing the flow rates will change the thermal lensing in the amplifier. This will change the optimal mode matching and alignment to the amplifier. Changing the the pump diode current effects the thermal lensing as well, meaning increasing it doesn't always lead to an improvement in TM00 power.
\paragraph{}
The prototype layout was able to deliver a maximum of 111\,\si{\watt} through the PMC during the a 55 hour trail run. This is shown in Figure \ref{fig:ligolaserchapterpowerplot}. The power was measured with recently calibrated power meters. The power dropped to 107\,\si{\watt} through the PMC. Changes in the lab temperature and humidity caused slight alignment changes. This resulted in the power decreasing. As the PSL is in a controlled environment, this effect will be less severe when commissioned for O4. The light produced by the amplifiers was greater than 125\,\si{\watt}. Mode matching between the final amplifier and the PMC could be improved at the percent level. This power could be increased by increasing the flow rate and pump diode currents to the amplifier, however concerns of jitter noise meant that the flow rate was kept low. With higher flow rates, an input power of 137\si{\watt} with a higher mode content of 4.6\% was produced (\href{https://alog.ligo-la.caltech.edu/TNT/index.php?callRep=11613}{Labbook post}). To actuate on the lasers power, some of the incident light will need to be 'dumped'. This means that the full power will be reduced by $\sim10\,\si{\watt}$ when the intensity stabilisation is implemented. Longer term measurements should be taken, however the results shown in \cite{Bode2020} are promising. LIGO Hanford measured the input laser power dropping from 55\,\si{\watt} to 52\,\si{\watt} over the course of one year (cite Ninas new paper); this is likely due to mode mismatches as a result of changes in the set-ups thermal state/.

\subsection{The Effect of Cooling on the Amplifier's Power and Beam Quality}
\href{https://alog.ligo-la.caltech.edu/TNT/index.php?callRep=11630}{Water flow example.}
The first thing that should be decided is how the cooling will be done and what flow rates will be used as the temperature of the amplifier heads will affect alignment and mode matching that is optimal. The flow rates of the coolant to the amplifier affects the the amount of thermal lensing. A balance must be struck between the amount of cooling - more is better - and the amount of jitter that can be potentially introduced to the beam via coolant moving through pipes. Due to the jitter noise experience at the Hanford detector that was due to the water cooling in the PSL, the flow rates will be set as low as possible. The thermal lensing in the $\rm{Nd:YVO_4}$ is non-circular since they are cooled from below there is a significant temperature gradient across the cyrstals. For small amounts of thermal lensing, the beam will become slightly astigmatic. From a beam quality point of view, it is better to have more cooling as with more cooling there is less thermal lensing. 
\paragraph{}
The cooling scheme that will be used is shown in Figure \ref{fig:ligolaserchapterwatercoolingschematic}. Flow meters which monitor the flow rate of the coolant will be placed on the return side of the system rather than the supply side. By having the flow meters on the return side, they will measure a drop in flow if there is a leak, allowing for the water supply to be cut.

A different cooling layout was used to test what the appropriate flow rates for the amplifiers and diode boxes could be. The diode box and amplifier head were connected in parallel, and each amplifier was on a separate cooling loop. This allows for the flow rate through each amplifier independently, meaning that the effect of flow rate on the beam quality can be studied for one amplifier. \href{https://alog.ligo-la.caltech.edu/TNT/index.php?callRep=11624}{labbook post}
\paragraph{}
The flow rate to the first amplifier was kept high to get as a high a quality beam as possible entering the second amplifier. The HOM content for this beam was 3.8\%. The second amplifier and diode box flow rate was decreased. The temperature of the diode head was monitored. The higher order mode content and was measured with the DBB and the power was measured with a high-power power meter. 
\paragraph{}
The change in beam quality and output power as a function of amplifier temperature is are shown in Figure \ref{fig:ligo_laser_chapter_cooling_pwoer_hom}. As the flow rate was decreased, the temperature of the heads increased. At 22.3\si{\celsius} the higher order mode content was $\sim 4.5\%$ and the amount of power in the fundamental mode was $\sim127\,\rm{W}$. By making mode scans, the change in higher order mode content was measured. For each amplifier head temperature, sixty mode scans were averaged. This revealed that changes in the second order mode made up the majority of the HOM content change. This is due to the changing in thermal lens focal length and the amount of thermally driven astigmatism of the beam. It was decided that the flow rate for the amplifier heads would be set to $0.75\,\rm{L\,min^{-1}}$, as this corresponds to less than 5\% HOM and $\sim 125\si{\watt}$ of power in the TM00 mode. If a lower flow rate was to be used, the temperature of the amplifiers heads would increase such that the power in the TM00 would fall below $125\si{\watt}$. \subsection{Amplifier Alignment Procedure}
To obtain the high beam power low higher order mode content the alignment of the input beam to the amplifier needs to be optimised. Aligning amplifiers differs to aligning cavities as an amplifier does not have an eigenmode. The output power of the amplifier may increase at the expense of higher order mode content, so the beam profile and the beam power must be monitored when aligning the amplifier.
\paragraph{}
First, the amplifier should be switched off. This protects it against damage as well as preventing the possibility of unknown beams with several tens of Watts of power being in the lab. The beam path between the NPRO and the exit of the first amplifier were inspected to make sure the beam path was clear and terminated with dumps.
%\href{https://alog.ligo-la.caltech.edu/TNT/index.php?callRep=11516}{lab book post}.
\paragraph{}
Next, the NPRO was set to produce 1.3W of light. This power of the light was measured on the input side of the amplifier. Using M03 and M04, the beam was roughly aligned so that the spot would hit the centre of the amplifier's input aperture. The power meter was placed on the exit side of the amplifier, and the input alignment was adjusted until the light exiting the amplifier was the same as the input power. Once this is done, one knows that the beam will be roughly aligned and some amplification will occur when the pump diodes are turned on. Once this was done, the high power attenuator immediately after the amplifier was set to dump all the light. The initial mode matching was based on the manufactures specifications, and the input light was set to s-polarisation.
\paragraph{}
The pump diode currents were increased until the amplifier `switched on'. This was at 2\,A of current through each of the pump diodes. The amplifier starts amplifying the input light when there is sufficient pump light to cause enough population inversion that stimulated emission occurs.
\paragraph{}
At this threshold, the output power was maximised by optimising the alignment. Then, the mode-matching was adjusted, in tandem with correcting for mis-alignments caused by non-perfect centring of the beam on the mode matching lens, L02, and non-perfect co-alignment of the rail this lens is mounted on to the optical axis. 
\paragraph{}
A smaller, faster power meter was used to monitor the power transmitted through the high power attenuator. Care must be taken to ensure the amount of light on this meter does not exceed its damage threshold. Compared to the high-power meter, the low-power meter has less drift and responds to changes in power faster. This gives quicker feedback to the aligner, allowing for easier alignment. 
\paragraph{}
A Wincam was set up to monitor the beam profile. If the amplifier is significantly misaligned and mode mismatched, the beam profile will become distorted. Large misalignment will cause the beam profile to look lopsided, and smaller misalignments will cause the beam profile to have shoulders. 
\paragraph{}
Once the the amplifier has been optimised for a low pump diode current, the pump diode current should be incremented in small steps of roughly 1-2A. Each time this is done, the thermal lensing will increase. Due to the non-uniform way in which the heating occurs, the input alignment needs to be re-optimised each time as does the input mode-matching. The idea is that by going in small steps of diode current, the optimal alignment can be safely achieved. The amplifier should be operated at high diode currents, $\approx 9\si{\ampere}$, so it is not worth spending a huge amount of time perfecting the input alignment and mode-matching at these currents. Its sufficient to be within a 1\% of optimal. 
\paragraph{}
Using measurements of the beam power and mode scans from the DBB, one can determine when the alignment to the amplifier is optimal. %\subsection{Intensity Stabilisation Concept}
The PSL must provide intensity stabilised light to the interferometer. The intensity stabilisation of the laser is done in two stages. Before the the IMC is locked, the laser is stabilised by the inner loop to a level required to the lock the IMC. The intensity noise requirement for the PSL is found in \cite{PSL_requirements} and reproduced in Figure \ref{fig:ligolaserchapterissrequirement}. At low frequencies, the requirement is driven by the need for the intensity noise to be less than the radiation pressure noise induced by the differences in the arm powers. At 10\,Hz, the laser must be stabilised to a level of $\sim1\times10^{-9}$. Above 10\,Hz, the requirement on the intensity noise is relaxed as there is passive filtering of the light by the PRC. The light is passively stabilised by the PMC to provide the required RF intensity noise. 
\paragraph{}
There are two loops used for intensity stabilisation; the intensity stabilisation concept is shown in Figure \ref{fig:ligolaserchapterissconcept}. To obtain lock of the IMC, the inner loop is required. Once the IMC is locked, the outer loop is used to fulfil the intensity noise requirement for when the interferometer is in observing mode. During observation mode, the intensity noise after the IMC is a better signal to use than one from a signal in the PSL.
\paragraph{}
The light used to measure the intensity noise for the inner loop is a low power beam that is transmitted by the PMC. There is $\sim1\,\si{\milli\watt}$ of light in this beam, so the laser cannot be stabilised beyond $\sim \frac{2\times10^{-8}}{ \sqrt{\rm{Hz}}}$ with this beam. Once the IMC is locked, a pick-off from the beam entering the interferometer is used to measure the intensity noise of the laser. This beam has enough power to reach the $\frac{1\times10^{-9}}{ \sqrt{\rm{Hz}}}$ shot noise requirement. As the inner-loop is in-air and is based on rigidly mounted optics, it is unable to remove noise associated with vibrating mounts.

\subsubsection{Acousto-Optic Modulator}
An AOM uses acoustic waves to scatter light into higher order modes. The amount of light that is Bragg scattered depends on the intensity of the the acoustic wave. The AOM used is a Crystal Technology 3080-199.  To control the intensity of the acoustic wave an RF driver, gooch and housego r21080-2am-rfx, was used. This driver produces an 80\,MHz signal whose power can be modulated. By modulating the power of this signal, the power in the zeroth order beam can be controlled. The diffraction efficiency of this AOM was found to be 74\%. This means that the AOM can be used for coarse and fine power adjustment. In order to modulate the intensity of a laser beam, some of it must always be scattered into the first order beam. In order to operate linearly, the DC level of the light that is dumped should be about 10\si{\watt}.

\subsubsection{Current Shunt}
Modulating the current through a laser diode will modulate the intensity of the laser. This is a standard way of implementing intensity control at the 10\si{\watt} level \cite{Abbott2001,Barr2005,Rollins2004,Zawischa2002,Nocera2004}. Using a current shunt as an intensity actuator has been demonstrated in a similar system at the 100\si{\watt} level \cite{Thies2019}.
\paragraph{}
The pump diodes current for the laser amplifier heads control the amount of power produced by the amplifier. By modulating the current through them, the power of the laser can be controlled. The current shunt diverts current from the pump diode by pulling it through resistor. This is controlled by a power transistor. 
\paragraph{}
The current shunt technique allows for one to have fewer transmissive high power optics in the beam path, which potentially can reduce noise and thermal distortions of the beam. It requires a smaller DC offset compared to an AOM in order for it to work. 
\paragraph{}
The transfer function between the diode current input and the ISS photodiode in transmission of the PMC was measured for a variety of different pump diode operating currents. A sketch of this is shown in figure \ref{fig:ligolaserchaptercstfmeasurement}. The transfer function was found to have significant amounts of variation in gain and phase between 1\si{\hertz}-100\si{\kilo\hertz}. It was found that the behaviour of the actuator at low frequencies strongly depended on the DC pump diode current. However, above 10\si{\kilo\hertz} the transfer function was less dependant on the DC pump diode current. This is good, as the unity gain point of the intensity stabilisation servo will be between 10-100\si{\kilo\hertz}, so the servo is more robust to changes in pump diode current. Figure \ref{fig:ligolaserchaptercurrentshunttf} shows the transfer function, in relative intensity units, for the current shunt actuator with a variety of different current shunt offsets. The AOM is a much stronger actuator that the current shunt, by roughly 30 dB. This can be compensated for by changing the servo electronics.

\subsubsection{Reasoning for Using the AOM Rather than the Current Shunt}
While the current shunt offers some advantages over the AOM, it cannot reduce the power at the many tens of Watts level which is a functionality that LIGO would likely need. LIGO has never operated at full power due to point absorbers in the test masses. To reduce the power by many tens of Watts the pump diode DC current would have to be reduced. This would affect the noise of the laser and the open loop transfer function of the servo. These amplifiers work best in their saturated regimes and the RIN may be increased if the pump diode currents are decreased significantly. The transfer function of the current shunt heavily depends on the pump diode DC current at 10\,Hz, this is where the requirement of the intensity stabilisation is most stringent. Alternatively, high power attenuation optics would be required, which means putting transmissive optics back in the beam bath and thus undoing the plus points gained by using a current shunt. When the LIGO detectors are able to operate at full power, then the current shunt actuator would be worth revisiting, however at present LIGO operates with between 25-50\si{\watt} of input power from the PSL.

\section{Pointing Noise of the Beam Entering the PMC}
The pointing noise witnessed at LHO is one of the reasons why single-pass amplifiers were chosen as the replacement of the HPO. Pointing noise manifests as intensity noise since a change in beam direction can be modelled as the introduction of higher order modes. These new amplifiers are cooled in such a way that the jitter on the PSL beam is reduced. Pointing is passively stabilised by the PSL via the PMC. The pointing noise requirement is based on the transfer function between the HG10 mode between the input of the interferometer and the dark port \cite{PSL_pointing_requirement}.
\paragraph{}
The pointing noise was measured using the DBB. The pointing noise is shown in Figure \ref{fig:ig:ligolaserchapterpointingnoise}. To measure pointing noise, the laser was locked the the DBB cavity. The reflected light from the cavity is made up of the the fundamental mode of the cavity (some of this mode leaks out) and the pointing modes which are excited by the laser. The beat between these two fields can be used to determine the pointing of the beam; this is known as differential wavefront sensing \cite{Morrison1994}. The electronic noise floor is at $1\times10^{-7}/\sqrt{\rm{Hz}}$. The pointing is expressed in units of $p = \delta x/w_0 + i\delta\alpha/\Theta$, where $\Theta$ is half the divergence angle of the beam, $\alpha$ is the angle of the beam, $x$ is the beams position and $w_0$ is the beams waist.

\section{Free Running Frequency Noise of the Prototype Laser System}
LIGO has a multi-stage frequency stabilisation system. Ultimately, LIGO uses the arm cavity as a frequency reference since $\delta f/f = \delta l/l$, however the PSL must provide a suitably low frequency noise to the interferometer so that it can lock the first elements of the interferometer. The PSL uses an ultra low expansion fused silica reference cavity in a Pound-Drever-Hall (PDH) locking scheme to provide this frequency stabilisation \cite{PSL_final_design}. 
\paragraph{}
The PMC can be used to measure the frequency noise of the PSL beam. The beam's frequency will be determined by the length noise of the PMC and the frequency noise fo the NPRO. The signals used to control the PMC can be converted into a measurement of the systems frequency noise. The control signal which is sent to the PZT in the PMC can give the frequency noise of the system where there is sufficient open loop gain. Around the unity gain point, the servos response needs to be factored in. Alternatively, the error signal and the open loop gain can be used to measure the frequency noise of the system.
\paragraph{}
The NPRO is locked to the PMC by controlling the length of the PMC. The length of the PMC is controlled with two actuators: a PZT attached to one its mirrors and a heater which can be used to control the thermal expansion of the PMC. The PZT provides the fast control, whereas the heater controls the length of the cavity on time-scales of minutes. The Pond-Drever-Hall technique is used to sense the length of the cavity. RF frequency side-bands are applied to the laser via an EOM. The light that is reflected from the PMC is demodulated at the same frequency as the RF side bands. When the cavity is near resonance the demodulated signal is proportional to the difference between the laser frequency and the frequency corresponding to the cavity length. A sketch of the PMC length control servo is shown in figure \ref{fig:ligolaserchapterfrequencynoisemeasurementsketch}.

\subsection{Frequency Noise Measurement}
The frequency noise is shown in figure \ref{fig:ligolaserchapterfrequencynoise}. Below the unity gain point, as the PZT response is flat, the PZT signal can be converted into frequency noise. The mixer signal and the open loop transfer function $\epsilon(1+GH)$ gives the frequency noise. These two measurements are within a factor of 2 of each other. Above the unity gain point of the servo, a signal applied to the PZT will not be suprressed by the loop, and so a signal measured at the error point will be equivalent to the frequency noise. A peak was used to check the calibration of the loop. The discrepancy between the blue and red traces in Figure \ref{ligolaserchapterfrequencynoise} may be due to the optical values used for the PMC, as these were not measured. To measure these, a broadband EOM could be used to sweep the frequency of the laser. The noise of a typical NPRO is shown. The measured noise follows this closely, it is likely that with a more accurate calibration, the PZT and error signal measurements would be be closer to the expected level for NRPO noise. The 2\,dB difference in the measured and modelled loop gain shows that the modelling of the loop is imperfect. The peak at 1111\,Hz should overlap. The red trace below the unity gain point is better calibrated since the FSR doesn't depend on the cleanliness of mirrors. between 100\,Hz to 1000\,Hz, there is a bulge in the frequency noise. This comes from the HEPA filter generating acoustic noise combined with non perfect offsetting in the PDH loop. below 100\,Hz and above 1\,kHz, the frequency noise of the laser is determined by the NPRO. Near the UGP, there is gain peaking which will ehnhance the noise.

\section{Conclusion}
The PSL has undergone a major redesign due to the jitter noise introduced by the cooling for the HPO. The new layout consists of an NPRO and two solid state amplifiers followed by the PMC. A prototype of this new design was constructed and tested. The set-up procedure was established and a cooling scheme was trialled. 
\paragraph{}
The output power was measured over two days and was found to drop from a peak of 111\,\si{\watt} to 107\,\si{\watt}. This was due to the changes the lab temperature affecting the alignment. 100W of intensity stabilised light was transmitted through the PMC. Higher output power could be achieved if more pump diode current and cooling was used. Additionally, more power could be coupled to the PMC if more time is spent on optimising the mode matching between the second amplifier and the PMC. 
\paragraph{}
The frequency noise of the beam transmitted by the PMC was investigated using signals in the PMC control loop. The laser is dominated by NPRO noise at frequencies below $\sim100\si{\hertz}$ and above $\sim1\si{\kilo\hertz}$  As the PMC is not shielded from acoustic vibrations, the PMC introduce noise between these two frequencies. 
\paragraph{}
The jitter noise was measured and it was found to be close to the requirements. As the PSL is in a quieter environment than the lab this layout was tested in, this may not be a problem. To reduce the jitter noise further, the flow rates being reduced may help, however this reduce the output power. Active alignment could be implemented using the light that is reflected from the PMC
\paragraph{}
The free running intensity noise of the beam transmitted by the PMC was measured and the inner loop intensity stabilisation was tested. Two actuators were considered, a current shunt or an AOM. The AOM was chosen as this offers the most flexibility in terms of loop gains and operating power. The inner loop noise requirement was met with this set-up. 
\paragraph{}
Future iterations of the PSL may use fibre lasers and fibre amplifiers \cite{Buikema2019,Wellmann2019}. These have been demonstrated at the power levels required for LIGO. Coherent power addition is another technique that can be used to obtain high beam powers. When long term operation has been demonstrated these technologies may be a viable replacement for the current PSL design.
