\section{Introduction}
The LIGO detectors require high input power, nominally 125\,\si{\watt} at 1064\,\si{\nano\metre}~\cite{PSL_requirements}, to decrease the shot noise which limits sensitivity at frequencies above 100\,\si{\hertz}. The original laser for aLIGO cannot reach the required power without generating excess pointing noise. In this chapter, the new laser layout is discussed. The \gls{pmc} throughput power, beam quality, intensity noise, pointing noise and frequency noise of the prototype laser were measured. The prototype of new laser was able to produce 100\,\si{\watt} of intensity stabilised light. The pointing noise was measured to be above the requirement; however, this prototype was made in a lab which was noisier than the \gls{psl}, and the pointing noise may be improved by reducing turbulence in the water-cooling system. While the 125\,\si{\watt} nominal power level was not obtained, this layout can provide $\sim30\,\rm{W}$ more power than the laser which was used for LIGO's third observation run. With additional work in optimising the layout, the maximum output power of this laser may be increased.  
\par
If coherent light from a laser is detected, the amplitude spectral density, $\sigma$, of the shot noise associated with power $P$ is $\sigma = \sqrt{\frac{hc}{\lambda}P}$, so the \gls{snr}, $P/\sigma$, at high frequencies scales with $\sqrt{P}$. Alternatively, to decrease the shot noise limit the wavelength could be decreased; however, the LIGO detectors operate at 1064\,\si{\nano\metre} due to the availability of mirror coatings with low absorption and low thermal noise. Future interferometers may operate at longer wavelengths due to the potential reduction of mirror coating thermal noise that can be achieved at longer wavelengths~\cite{Craig2019}.
\par
In the field of ground-based gravitational wave detection, high power refers to lasers with powers of the order 100\,\si{\watt}. High power is a loosely defined term as lasers ranging from 100\,\si{\watt} to 100\,\si{\kilo\watt} are defined as high power in the literature~\cite{Shcherbakov2013,Lee2019,Huang2019,Ma2016,Robin2014,Dixneuf2020,Winkelmann2011,Wellmann2019,Buikema2019,Bode2020,Thies2019}. Pulsed lasers are often used to reach high powers, however gravitational wave detectors must use a single frequency, narrow linewidth, continuous wave laser, so a pulsed laser would be unsuitable. As the power is increased, other properties of the laser tend to be degraded. It needs to operate uninterrupted on year-long timescales; this poses a major engineering challenge. 
\par
The laser for LIGO must be linearly polarised, have high spatial purity, low intensity noise and low frequency noise. These requirements are detailed in~\cite{PSL_requirements}. To overcome the technical challenges that come with the high power and low noise requirements for the LIGO laser, the \gls{mopa} topology was selected. This type of laser is based on a high performance seed laser which is then amplified. As amplifiers are linear and coherent, the low noise properties of the seed laser characterises the noise of the high power beam.
\par
LIGO's original laser was able to generate $\sim150\,\si{\watt}$ of power in the HG00 mode~\cite{Kwee2012}. This laser used an injection-locked \gls{hpo} as the means for obtaining the target power. The \gls{hpo} was a ring cavity containing laser crystals, and it was seeded by a 35\,\si{\watt} low noise laser. This laser went through extensive testing and refinement at the Max Planck Institute for Gravitational Physics ~\cite{Kwee2012} and was installed as part of LIGO's \gls{psl}. 
\par
However, due to pointing noise from the water cooling of the crystals within the \gls{hpo} coupling to the differential arm motion signal, as well as one \gls{hpo} being destroyed by accident, the \gls{hpo} was bypassed and the LIGO detectors operated using the 35\,\si{\watt} laser~\cite{InstrumentWhitePaper2019}. To reach higher powers during LIGO's third observation run, instead of the \gls{hpo}, a neoVAN-4S single-pass amplifier made by neoLase~\cite{neolase} was used. This stepped up the laser power from 35\,\si{\watt} to 70\,\si{\watt}~\cite{spencer_thesis,Buikema2020}. 
\par
The next stage of development was a complete redesign of the LIGO laser. A prototype laser was constructed and tested at LIGO Livingston. This design will be installed as the new \gls{psl} for the start of LIGO's fourth observation run~\cite{InstrumentWhitePaper2019}.

\subsection{Non-Planar Ring Oscillators}
The first element of the \gls{psl} is an \gls{npro}~\cite{Kane1985,Freitag1995,Heurs2004}. The \gls{npro} provides a low noise laser making it a suitable seed for a \gls{mopa} system. This is a single frequency laser constructed from one \ce{Nd{:}YAG} crystal which acts as both the gain medium and the cavity, thus it produces a laser with a high amount of frequency stability. The \gls{npro} used was a Mephisto sold by Coherent~\cite{mephisto}, and it can produce 2W of 1064\,\si{\nano\metre} light.
\subsection{The neoVAN-4S-HP Solid-State Amplifier}
The neoVAN-4S-HP is a single-pass, solid-state amplifier  manufactured by Neolase. It is constructed from four \ce{Nd{:}YVO4} crystals which are pumped by fibre coupled, low noise laser diodes. It should be seeded by s-polarised light at 1064\,nm. These gain mediums are in contact with a metal plate which is cooled. This keeps pointing noise low as flowing water is not directly in contact with the gain medium. As these amplifiers have low noise pump-diodes and will be seeded by an \gls{npro}, the noise of the light produced by them should be determined by the \gls{npro}. Results from a setup using a single neoVAN-4S-HP showed these amplifiers can be continuously operated for many tens of days~\cite{Thies2019}. A similar setup using a 35\,W seed laser and two neoVAN-4S-HPs was able to generate 195\,W of light~\cite{Bode2020} for nearly 80 days with no significant drops in power. 
\subsection{The Pre Mode Cleaner Cavity}
The \gls{pmc}, see Figure~\ref{fig:ligo_layout_chapter_layout}, is used as the hand-off point between the \gls{psl} and the rest of the interferometer. Light transmitted by this cavity is used as the input beam for the interferometer. The \gls{pmc} is a 2\,m bow tie cavity with an \gls{fsr} of $\sim150\,\si{\mega\hertz}$ and a \gls{fwhm} of $\sim1\,\rm{MHz}$, thus it passively filters \gls{rf} intensity noise. \gls{rf} sidebands used for sensing and controlling lengths within the interferometer are provided after the \gls{pmc} but before the \gls{imc} (see Figure~\ref{fig:ligolayout}), meaning that the \gls{imc} cannot be used to provide the required \gls{rf} intensity stabilisation. The \gls{pmc} is locked to the fundamental mode so the beam that is delivered to the rest of the interferometer will have a low higher order mode content. This is important as the amplifiers produce light with spatial defects. The \gls{pmc} has been redesigned~\cite{new_pmc_T1700543} to prevent contamination due to glue on its mirrors and to make maintaining the \gls{pmc} easier. Failures of the old \glspl{pmc} can be found in \cite{failed_pmc,failed_pmc_2}. The \gls{pmc} design no longer includes a vacuum enclosure to isolate it from acoustic noise as this was found to be unnecessary.

An optical layout based on the concept described in Section~\ref{sec:ligo_laser_chapter_concept} was constructed. A sketch of the layout is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:ligo_layout_chapter_layout}. In addition to the main beam path, there are low power diagnostic beams after each amplifier that can be used to characterise the laser. Eventually, this laser will include the \gls{ttfss}; however, at this stage of testing, the \gls{ttfss} was not installed and so this has not been included in the layout description.
\par
The \gls{npro} beam passes through some optics to clean up the polarisation. Transmission through a steering mirror is used to monitor the power of the \gls{npro}. An \gls{eom} is used to produce 35.5\,MHz sidebands for the \gls{pdh} scheme used to lock the \gls{pmc}. A Faraday isolator ensures light reflected back from the amplifiers does not reach the \gls{npro} and hence damage it. The beam passes through a lens to mode match the beam to the first amplifier. Two mirrors are used to align the \gls{npro} beam to the amplifier. A half-wave plate ensures the polarisation into the first amplifier is set correctly. These optics are shown in Figure~\ref{fig:o191520}.
\par
After the first amplifier, the beam passes through a high power attenuation stage, which consists of a half wave-plate and two thin film polarisers, to allow for safe set up and low power operation. When setting up this layout, the amplifiers must be run at maximum power since they exhibit significant thermal lensing. The high power attenuators are not envisioned being used during observation runs. The light from the laser is then directed through a Faraday isolator and wave-plate before reaching the second amplifier. The mode matching lenses for the second amplifier are on either side of the Faraday isolator. Two mirrors immediately before the second amplifier are used for its alignment. The transmission through one of the steering mirrors before the second amplifier is used for monitoring the beam produced by the first amplifier. This section of the layout is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:o191520}.
\par
After the second amplifier, the beam goes through a high power attenuation stage before passing through an \gls{aom}. This \gls{aom} is for controlling the power of the laser. Downstream of the \gls{aom}, a pick-off beam is used to measure the properties of the laser produced by the second amplifier. Before entering the \gls{pmc}, the beam is transmitted through a thin film polariser so that the beam is set to be p-polarised. This part of the layout can be seen in Figure~\ref{fig:o191525}.
\par
The beam produced by the second amplifier is then coupled to the \gls{pmc}; this requires two mode matching lenses and two steering mirrors. There are three beams transmitted by the \gls{pmc}: a high power one, which would be sent to the interferometer, and two low power ones for stabilising the frequency and intensity of the laser. The low power beams contain of the order 100\,\si{\milli\watt} of light. The \gls{iss} inner-loop photodiodes are in a light-tight enclosure. One of the photodiodes is used as the in-loop sensor while the other is used as the out-of-loop sensor. The \gls{pmc}  is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:o191531}. The reflected light is used to lock the \gls{pmc}; this path can be seen in Figure~\ref{fig:o191525}.
\par
The ~\cite{Kwee2007,kwee_thesis} is integrated into the layout via several pick-off points and can be used to monitor the status of both amplifiers. The \gls{dbb} enables measurements of intensity noise, frequency noise, beam pointing noise and higher order mode content. The \gls{dbb} is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:o191542}. The \gls{dbb} consists of a cavity, motorised lenses, an automatic alignment system and several photodiodes. Despite having motorised lens mounts in the \gls{dbb}, static mode matching lenses are required in the diagnostic beam paths. To characterise the \gls{npro} beam, mirrors on magnetic mounts can be inserted into the layout.

\section{The Power Transmitted by the Pre Mode Cleaner}
%The PSL should deliver 125\,\si{\watt} to the interferometer~\cite{PSL_requirements}. There are several variables that will affect the amount of power that is transmitted through the PMC:  the pump-diode current; the input alignment to each amplifier and to the PMC; the mode matching to each amplifier and to the PMC; the flow rate of coolant through the amplifiers heads and the polarisation of the light entering the amplifiers. The pump-diode current and amount of cooling both affect the thermal lensing, thus they both affect the optimal mode matching and alignment. Increases in the pump-diode current will not necessarily increase the power in the fundamental mode as thermal lensing can distort the beam.
%\par
The \gls{psl} should deliver 125\,\si{\watt} to the interferometer~\cite{PSL_requirements}. The power transmitted through the \gls{pmc} is ultimately determined by two factors: the power in the beam generated by the amplifiers, and the distortion of this beam. The beam's distortion is affected by the amplifiers' input alignment, mode matching and thermal lensing. Increasing the pump-diode current increases the power that is produced by the amplifier as well as the thermal lensing. The flow rate of the coolant can be increased to mitigate thermal lensing; however, this can generate pointing noise.
\par
The prototype layout was able to deliver a maximum of 111\,\si{\watt} through the \gls{pmc} during a 55 hour trial run. This is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:ligolaserchapterpowerplot}. The power was measured with recently calibrated power meters. The power emerging from the \gls{pmc} dropped to 107\,\si{\watt} during the course of the trial run, while the power incident onto it remained $\sim125\,\si{\watt}$. Changes in the lab temperature and humidity caused slight alignment changes. This resulted in the power decreasing. As the laser will be in a controlled environment, this effect will be less severe when it is installed.

\par
To increase the output power, the mode matching to the \gls{pmc} could be improved and the amplified beam's distortion could be reduced. Mode matching between the final amplifier and the \gls{pmc} could be improved at the percent level. Increasing the flow rate would reduce the beam's distortion and may allow for higher pump-diode currents, however concerns of pointing noise meant that the flow rate was kept low. With higher flow rates, a beam with 137\,\si{\watt} of power and a higher mode content of 4.6\% was produced by the amplifiers. 
\par
To actuate on the power of the laser, some of the incident light will be `dumped' to provide headroom for the actuator to increase the beam's power. This means that the full power will be reduced by $\sim10\,\si{\watt}$ when the intensity stabilisation is implemented. 
\par
Long term measurements should be taken, however the results in~\cite{Bode2020} show these amplifiers can be run continuously for at least 80 days. Staff at \gls{lho} measured the input laser power dropping from 55\,\si{\watt} to 52\,\si{\watt} over the course of one year~\cite{Bode2020a}; this is likely due to the ageing of the laser diodes as well as mode mismatches resulting from changes in the thermal state of the setup.

\subsection{The Effect of Cooling on the Amplifier's Power and Beam Quality}
%\href{https://alog.ligo-la.caltech.edu/TNT/index.php?callRep=11630}{Water flow example.}
%The first thing that should be decided is how the cooling will be done and what flow rates will be used as the temperature of the amplifier heads will affect alignment and mode matching that is optimal. The flow rates of the coolant to the amplifier affects the amount of thermal lensing. A balance must be struck between the amount of cooling - more is better - and the amount of pointing that can be potentially introduced to the beam via coolant moving through pipes. Due to the pointing noise experience at the Hanford detector that was due to the water cooling in the PSL, the flow rates will be set as low as possible. The thermal lensing in the $\rm{Nd:YVO_4}$ is non-circular since they are cooled from below there is a significant temperature gradient across the cyrstals. For small amounts of thermal lensing, the beam will become slightly astigmatic. From a beam quality point of view, it is better to have more cooling as with more cooling there is less thermal lensing. 
\par
As the quality of the beam emerging from an amplifier is affected by the temperature of the amplifier's head, an experiment was performed to determine a suitable operating temperature. As the \ce{Nd{:}YVO4} crystals are cooled non-uniformly, the thermal lens is non-circular. For small amounts of thermal lensing, the beam will become slightly astigmatic. Due to the pointing noise witnessed at \gls{lho}, the flow rates were set as low as possible before the power in the fundamental mode dropped significantly.
\par

The diode box and amplifier head were connected in parallel, and each amplifier was on a separate cooling loop so that the amplifier head temperatures could be independently controlled. The flow rate to the first amplifier was kept high to produce a high quality input beam for the second amplifier. The \gls{hom} content for the beam entering the second amplifier was 3.8\%. The second amplifier and diode box flow rate was decreased and the temperature of the diode head was monitored. The higher order mode content was measured with the \gls{dbb} by averaging over sixty mode scans. The power was measured with a high-power power meter.

\subsection{Alignment Procedure for the Amplifiers}
To obtain high beam power and low higher order mode content, the alignment of the input beam to the amplifier needs to be optimised. The output power of the amplifier may increase at the expense of higher order mode content, so the beam profile and the beam power must be monitored when aligning the amplifier. Optimising the alignment to the amplifier is an iterative process, so with each increase in power a small change in the amplifier's alignment is required.
\par
Initially, the amplifier was switched off. This protects it against damage as well as preventing the possibility of stray light beams with several tens of watts of power being in the lab. The beam path between the \gls{npro} and the exit of the first amplifier were inspected to make sure the beam path was clear and terminated with dumps.
%\href{https://alog.ligo-la.caltech.edu/TNT/index.php?callRep=11516}{lab book post}.
\par
Next, the \gls{npro} was set to produce 1.3W of light. The power of the light was measured on the input side of the amplifier. The beam was roughly aligned so that the spot would hit the centre of the amplifier's input aperture using the two mirrors that immediately precede the amplifier. The power meter was placed on the exit side of the amplifier, and the input alignment was adjusted until the power emerging from the amplifier was the same as the input power. This indicates that the beam will be roughly aligned and some amplification will occur when the pump-diodes are turned on. The high power attenuator immediately after the amplifier was set to dump all the light. The initial mode matching was based on the manufacturers specifications, and the input light was set to s-polarisation.
\par
The pump-diode currents were increased until the amplifier `switched on'. This was at 2\,A of current through each of the pump-diodes. Amplification begins when there is sufficient pump light to cause enough population inversion that stimulated emission occurs. At this threshold, the output power was maximised by optimising the alignment. Then, the mode matching was adjusted in tandem with correcting for mis-alignments caused by non-perfect centring of the beam on the mode matching lens.
\par
A fast low-power power meter was used to monitor the beam transmitted through the high power attenuator. Compared to the high-power meter, the low-power meter drifted less and responded to changes in power faster. This gave quicker feedback and allowed for easier alignment. 
\par
A Wincam CCD profiler was set up to monitor the beam profile. If the amplifier is significantly misaligned and mode mismatched, the beam profile will become distorted. Large misalignment will cause the beam profile to look lopsided, and smaller misalignments will cause the beam profile to have shoulders. 
\par
Once the amplifier had been optimised for a low pump-diode current, the pump-diode current was incremented in small steps of roughly 1--2A. Each time this was done, the thermal lensing increased. Due to the non-uniform way in which the heating occurred, the input alignment was re-optimised each time as was the input mode matching. By going in small steps of diode current, the optimal alignment was safely achieved. The amplifier should be operated at high diode currents, $\sim 9\si{\ampere}$, so at low currents it was sufficient to be within 1\% of optimal. 
\par
To determine when the alignment was optimal, mode scans with the \gls{dbb} were performed. Due to thermal lensing, the beam will be slightly astigmatic and so optimising the mode matching from the amplifier to the \gls{dbb} was difficult. When optimising the pump-diode currents, the thermal lensing changes, so the mode matching to the \gls{dbb} needs to be corrected for this.

The free running-intensity noise of the laser exceeds the level needed for the interferometer to reach design sensitivity. Therefore, the laser must be stabilised. As actuators on the lasers intensity, a current shunt and an \gls{aom} were compared. Using the current shunt rather than the \gls{aom} enabled 10\,W more power to be obtained in the beam emerging from the \gls{pmc}, however the behaviour of the current shunt was highly dependent on the DC pump-diode current at 10\,Hz where the laser noise requirement is most strict. If the amplifiers were required to operate under different circumstances during the course of an observation run, the current shunt may prove to be unreliable. In contrast, the \gls{aom} has proved reliable during previous observation runs. While the \gls{aom} causes the beam to have less DC power, this may not be a problem for O4 as it is unlikely that the full 125\,W will be used due to point defects on the test masses~\cite{Buikema2020}.
\subsection{The Concept and Requirements for Intensity Stabilisation Servo}
The intensity noise requirement for LIGO's laser is found in~\cite{PSL_requirements} and reproduced in Figure~\ref{fig:ligolaserchapterissrequirement}. An imbalance between the powers in the arms gives rise to a laser-intensity-to-test-mass-motion coupling, so at low frequencies (0.1--10Hz), the requirement on intensity noise is set by the motion of the test masses due to seismic vibrations; in this frequency band, the intensity noise should be low enough that the motion of the test masses is dominated by seismic noise. At 10\,Hz, the laser must be stabilised to a level of $\sim1\times10^{-9}/\sqrt{\rm{Hz}}$. Above 10\,Hz, the requirement on the intensity noise is relaxed as there is passive filtering of the light by the PRC. The light is passively stabilised by the \gls{pmc} to reduce the intensity noise of the laser at \gls{rf}.  
%\par
%There are two loops used for intensity stabilisation; the intensity stabilisation concept is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:ligolaserchapterissconcept}. To obtain lock of the \gls{imc}, the inner-loop is required. Once the \gls{imc} is locked, the outer-loop is used to fulfil the intensity noise requirement for when the interferometer is in observing mode. During observation mode, the intensity noise after the \gls{imc} is a better signal to use than one from a signal in the PSL.
\par
The active intensity stabilisation of the laser is done in two stages as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:ligolaserchapterissconcept}. First, the laser is stabilised by the inner-loop to a level required to lock the \gls{imc}. The light used to measure the intensity noise for the inner-loop is a low power beam that is transmitted by the \gls{pmc}. There is $\sim1\,\si{\milli\watt}$ of light in this beam, thus the laser cannot be stabilised beyond $\sim 2\times10^{-8}/\sqrt{\rm{Hz}}$ due to shot noise. Once the \gls{imc} is locked, a pick-off from the beam entering the interferometer is used to measure the intensity noise of the laser. This beam has enough power, $\sim 350\,\rm{mW}$, to reach the level required, $1\times10^{-9}/\sqrt{\rm{Hz}}$, during observation mode. The noise of the beam entering the interferometer must be measured just after the \gls{imc} as components between the \gls{pmc} and \gls{imc} can contribute to the intensity noise, and this cannot be measured by the inner-loop diode.

\subsection{Free-Running Noise of the Prototype Laser}
To determine the open-loop gain needed for the servo to suppress the noise to the requirement, the free-running noise of the laser must be measured. The noise was measured at different points along the beam path to see the contribution that the \gls{npro}, each amplifier and the \gls{pmc} made to the total noise.
\par
The power noise of the \gls{npro} was measured using the \gls{dbb} \gls{rin} photodiode (Figure~\ref{fig:ligolaserchapterfrn}). The \gls{npro} had a typical noise level for this type of laser~\cite{Heurs2004}. This was recorded via the \gls{cds} (see Appendix~\ref{app:CDS}), so the measurements near 10\,\si{\kilo\hertz} are affected by the anti-aliasing filter. The noise between 100\si{\hertz} and 1\si{\kilo\hertz} had features associated with the motion of the table and mirror mounts.
\par
The power noise of the first and second amplifiers was measured using the \gls{dbb} \gls{rin} photodiode (Figure~\ref{fig:ligolaserchapterfrn}). The noise was measured with a spectrum analyser rather than \gls{cds} so that measurements up to 100\,kHz could be made. As the amplifiers were pumped with low noise diodes and were operating in their saturated regimes, the \gls{rin} is expected to decrease after each amplifier: when an amplifier is saturated, the amplifier will not behave linearly and instead will add a fixed input power regardless of its seed power. As expected, at frequencies above 10\,kHz the \gls{rin} after the second amplifier was less than the \gls{rin} after the first amplifier.  There was a peak at 20\si{\hertz}; this can be attributed to the air conditioning. The noise was covered in a forest of lines with 4\si{\hertz} spacing, starting from  4\si{\hertz} and continuing up to high frequencies. These lines were found to originate in the Beckhoff~\cite{beckhoff} slow-control computer which is used as an interface for the amplifiers. The laser control box needs better electrical isolation between its digital and analogue sections to remove the 4\,Hz lines.
\par
The noise of the beam emerging from the \gls{pmc} was measured (Figure~\ref{fig:ligolaserchapterfrn}). The \gls{pmc} introduces length to intensity noise coupling via the offset in the \gls{pdh} loop. The offset needed to remove this coupling drifted due to changing levels of \gls{rf} pick-up in the servo electronics for the \gls{pmc}. The coupling from length noise of the \gls{pmc} to intensity noise was decreased as much as possible.

\subsection{Power Control Actuators}
The actuator that was used to provide intensity stabilisation was an \gls{aom}. The use of a current shunt integrated into one of the pump-diodes in the second amplifier was investigated. The current shunt had the potential to reduce the number of transmissive optics in the beam path and increase the total power. Despite this, the current shunt may be an unsuitable choice for stabilising LIGO's laser. The current shunt transfer function depends on the DC current, at 10\,Hz it changed by $\sim10\,\si{\decibel}$ when the DC current was changed by $\sim1\,\si{\ampere}$ (see Figure~\ref{fig:ligolaserchaptercurrentshunttf}), and so this may make the intensity stabilisation servo unreliable if the DC pump-diode currents need to be changed over the course of an observation run. 
\subsubsection{Acousto-Optic Modulator}
An \gls{aom} uses acoustic waves to scatter light into higher order modes. The amount of light that is Bragg scattered depends on the intensity of the acoustic wave. The \gls{aom} used was a Crystal Technology 3080-199. An \gls{rf} driver, Gooch and Housego r21080-2am-rfx, was used to control the intensity of the acoustic wave. This driver produced an 80\,MHz signal whose power can be modulated. To linearised the \gls{aom}'s response, an analogue multiplier \gls{ic} (AD734) was included in the servo. By modulating the power of this signal, the power in the zeroth order beam can be controlled. The diffraction efficiency of this \gls{aom} was found to be 74\%. This means that the \gls{aom} can be used for coarse and fine power adjustment. To modulate the intensity of a laser beam, some of it must always be scattered into the first order beam. To operate linearly, the DC level of the light that is dumped was $\sim10\si{\watt}$.

\subsubsection{Current Shunt}
Modulating the current through a laser diode will modulate the intensity of the laser. This is a standard way of implementing power control at the 10\si{\watt} level~\cite{Abbott2001,Barr2005,Rollins2004,Zawischa2002,Nocera2004}. Using a current shunt as an intensity actuator has been demonstrated in a similar system at the 100\si{\watt} level~\cite{Thies2019}. The current shunt technique allows for one to have fewer transmissive high power optics in the beam path, which potentially can reduce noise and thermal distortions of the beam. 
\par
The amplifier's pump-diode current determines the power produced by the amplifier. By modulating the current through one of the pump-diodes, the power of the laser can be stabilised. The current shunt consists of a power MOSFET and a resistor. It diverts current from the pump-diode by pulling it through the resistor instead. The current that is pulled through the resistor depends on the voltage at the gate of the MOSFET (see~\cite[Chapters 2 and 3]{Horowitz_and_Hill}). The current shunt acts linearly with a smaller DC offset compared to an \gls{aom}, therefore higher beam power can be achieved.
\par
The transfer function between the current shunt input and the \gls{iss} photodiode in transmission of the \gls{pmc} was measured for a variety of different DC pump-diode currents. This is shown, in relative intensity units, in Figure~\ref{fig:ligolaserchaptercurrentshunttf}. A schematic of the measurement is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:ligolaserchaptercstfmeasurement}. The transfer function was found to have significant amounts of variation in gain and phase between 1\,\si{\hertz}--100\,\si{\kilo\hertz}. Above 10\,\si{\kilo\hertz} the transfer function did not vary much with DC pump-diode current. This is good, as the unity gain point of the intensity stabilisation servo will be between 10\,\si{\kilo\hertz}--100\,\si{\kilo\hertz}. The \gls{aom} is a much stronger actuator that the current shunt; this can be compensated for by changing the servo electronics. It was found that the behaviour of the actuator at 10\,Hz, where the servo requirement is hardest to meet, strongly depended on the DC pump-diode current.

\subsubsection{The Reason for Choosing the Acousto-optic Modulator Instead of the Current Shunt}
While the current shunt offers some advantages over the \gls{aom}, it cannot reduce the power at the many tens of watts level. This is a functionality that LIGO would likely need as the detectors have never operated at full power, at present the LIGO detectors operates with between 25--50\si{\watt}, due to point absorbers on the test masses. The pump-diode DC current would have to be lowered significantly to reduce the power by many tens of watts. This would affect the noise of the laser and the open-loop transfer function of the servo. These amplifiers work best in their saturated regime and the \gls{rin} may be increased if the pump-diode currents are decreased significantly. 
\par
There may be other circumstances in which the pump-diode currents need to be altered; towards the end of their life-time the diodes need to be frequently adjusted. Alternatively, high power attenuation optics would be required. Unlike the current shunt, the \gls{aom}'s response depends on the DC light level in a trivial way. The \gls{aom} causes a decrease in total power, but this downside is outweighed by the consistency and reliability under different operating conditions of the \gls{aom} compared to the current shunt. When the LIGO detectors are able to operate at full power, then the current shunt actuator would be worth revisiting

\subsection{Open-Loop Transfer Function of the Intensity Stabilisation Servo}
The free running noise of the laser transmitted by the \gls{pmc} is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:ligolaserchapterfrn} and the requirement is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:ligolaserchapterissrequirement}. Based on the noise and the requirement, the unity gain point of the servo must be above 10\,\si{\kilo\hertz}. At 10\,\si{\hertz}, where the requirement is hardest to meet, the noise requirement is $2\times10^{-8} \frac{1}{\sqrt{\rm{Hz}}}$ and the noise is $1\times10^{-5} - 1\times10^{-4} \frac{1}{\sqrt{\rm{Hz}}}$, so there needs to be at least 60\,dB of open-loop gain at this frequency. If the noise of the laser was not dominated by the 4\,Hz lines, the amplitude stabilisation could be achieved with a single pole low pass filter. However, this servo uses additional shaping to ensure that there is enough gain to suppress these peaks.
\par
A sketch of the servo is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:ligolaserchapteroltfmeasurement}. The servo features two circuits that add shaping; these are found built into the photodiode circuit~\cite{ISS_inner_loop_photodiode} and within the rack electronics~\cite{ISS_inner_loop_rack}. The servo model is described in detail in Section~\ref{sec:ligolaserchapterservomodel}. The inner-loop \gls{iss} features two photodiodes, one for in-loop measurements and one for out-of-loop measurements. The rack electronics allow for the servo to be controlled via \gls{cds}. This gives the user the ability to turn the servo on/off, to vary the overall gain and activate extra integrators.
\par

\section{Pointing Noise of the Beam Entering the Pre Mode Cleaner}
The pointing noise witnessed at \gls{lho} is one of the reasons why single-pass amplifiers were chosen as the replacement for the \gls{hpo}. Pointing noise on the beam entering the \gls{pmc} manifests as intensity noise of the beam exiting the \gls{pmc} since a change in beam direction can be modelled as the introduction of higher order modes. Pointing is passively stabilised by the \gls{psl} via the \gls{pmc}. These new amplifiers are cooled in such a way that the pointing noise on the \gls{psl} beam is reduced. The pointing noise requirement is based on the transfer function of the HG10 mode between the input of the interferometer and the dark port~\cite{PSL_pointing_requirement}.
\par
The pointing noise was measured using the \gls{dbb} and is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:ligolaserchapterpointingnoise}. To measure pointing noise, the laser was locked to the \gls{dbb} cavity. The reflected light from the cavity was made up of the fundamental mode of the cavity (some of the cavity mode leaks out) and the first order modes of the incident light associated with misalignment. The beat between these two fields can be used to determine the pointing noise of the beam; this is known as differential wavefront sensing~\cite{Morrison1994}.
\par
The pointing noise can be expressed as a normalised, complex quantity $p = \delta x/w_0 + j\delta\alpha/\Theta$, where $\Theta$ is half the divergence angle of the beam, $\alpha$ is the angle of the beam, $x$ is the beam's position and $w_0$ is the beam's waist. The beam exiting the cavity is sensed by quadrant photodiodes separated by \ang{90} of Gouy phase so that both quadratures of $p$ can be measured. The length of the cavity is modulated at 1\,MHz with a PZT, and the quadrant photodiodes are demodulated at this frequency to obtain a measurement of pointing.
\begin{figure}
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=1\linewidth]{Figures/ligo_laser_chapter_pointing_noise}
	\caption{The four relative pointing degrees of freedom for the beam into the \gls{pmc} was measured with the \gls{dbb}. The requirement~\cite{PSL_pointing_requirement} is shown in light blue. The pointing noise is above the requirement, however the TnT lab has a higher amount of environmental noise than the \gls{psl}. Measurements below 200\,Hz were be affected by scattering.}
	\label{fig:ligolaserchapterpointingnoise}
\end{figure}
\par
While the pointing noise measurement was affected by scattering below $\sim200\,\rm{Hz}$, the pointing was measured to exceed the requirement below $\sim1\,\si{\kilo\hertz}$. To reduce the pointing noise caused by turbulence in the cooling system, the water lines should be made to have gradual bends in them. As the LIGO \gls{psl} table is in a quieter environment than this setup, an improvement will be seen when this is constructed on the \gls{psl} table.
\section{Frequency Noise of the Prototype Laser}
The LIGO detectors employ a multi-stage frequency stabilisation system. Ultimately, the arm cavity is used as a frequency reference since $\delta f/f = \delta l/l$ and the LIGO arm cavities are $4$\,km and their length is stabilised by quadrupole suspensions, however the initial stabilisation of the laser uses an ultra low expansion fused silica reference cavity located on the laser table~\cite{PSL_final_design}. The free-running frequency noise must be measured to design the frequency stabilisation servo. The \gls{pmc} length noise, $\delta l_{\rm{PMC}}$, is converted to a frequency noise via $\delta f_{\rm{PMC}} = f_{\rm{EM}}\frac{\delta l_{\rm{PMC}}}{l_{\rm{PMC}}}$, where $f_{\rm{EM}}$ is the laser's frequency and $l_{\rm{PMC}}$ is the length of the \gls{pmc}.
\par
With the stabilisation loop running, the frequency noise of the beam exiting the \gls{pmc} will be determined by the frequency noise of the \gls{pmc} and the frequency noise of the \gls{npro}. The signals used to control the \gls{pmc} can be converted into a measurement of the systems frequency noise. The control signal which is sent to the PZT in the \gls{pmc} can give the frequency noise of the system where there is sufficient open-loop gain since at these frequencies, the closed loop gain is unity. Around the unity gain point of the servo, the response of the servo needs to be factored in. Alternatively, the error signal at the mixer and the open-loop gain can be used to measure the frequency noise of the system.
\par
The \gls{npro} is locked to the \gls{pmc} by controlling the length of the \gls{pmc}. The length of the \gls{pmc} is controlled with two actuators. A PZT is attached to one of the mirrors to provide fast control, and a heater which determines the thermal expansion of the \gls{pmc} provides slow control. The \gls{pdh} technique is used to find the difference between the cavities frequency and the frequency of the laser. \gls{rf} frequency side-bands are applied to the laser via an \gls{eom}, and the light that is reflected from the \gls{pmc} is demodulated at the same frequency as the \gls{rf} sidebands. When the cavity is near resonance the demodulated signal is proportional to the difference between the laser frequency and the frequency corresponding to the cavity length. A sketch of the \gls{pmc} length control servo is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:ligolaserchapterfrequencynoisemeasurementsketch}.

A PZT controls the length of the cavity at high frequencies. \par
To see how the error signal used to lock the cavity and the open-loop transfer function gives the frequency noise, consider Figure~\ref{fig:ligolaserchapterfrequencynoisemeasurementmathssketch}. The difference between the laser frequency, $a$, and the cavity frequency, $b$, is obtained via the \gls{pdh} technique. The PZT signal, $c$, is then obtained via the the transfer function of the electronics and the transfer function of the optics, i.e.~the frequency to voltage transfer function corresponding to the slope of the demodulated \gls{pdh} signal. Combining these transfer functions gives $G$. First, the error signal is defined as $c = a - b$.  Next, the control signal, $d$, is expressed in terms of the error signal, $c G = d$. Expressing the signal $b$ in terms of $d$ allows for signal $a$ to be written in terms of the error signal $c$,
\begin{equation}
a = c(1+GH).
\label{eq:ligolaserchaptermeasurement_of_frequency_noise}
\end{equation}
Equation (\ref{eq:ligolaserchaptermeasurement_of_frequency_noise}) shows that to measure the frequency noise of the laser, $a$, the error signal should be, approximately, multiplied by the open-loop transfer function, $GH$.

\subsection{Frequency Noise Measurement}
The measurement of the systems frequency noise is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:ligolaserchapterfrequencynoise}. Despite imperfect calibration, this measurement provides some useful information about the frequency noise of the laser and the length noise of the \gls{pmc}. As the \gls{pmc} was not vacuum sealed, between 100\,Hz to 1\,kHz, there was a bulge in the frequency noise. It is likely that this noise is not an issue, as LIGO never used the old \gls{pmc} under vacuum. This was due to the air conditioning generating acoustic noise and length noise within the \gls{pmc}, as well as non perfect offsetting in the \gls{pdh} loop. 
\par
Below the unity gain point, as the PZT response is flat, the PZT signal can be converted into frequency noise. The mixer signal and the open-loop transfer function $c(1+GH)$ gives the frequency noise. These two measurements are within a factor of 2 of each other. There is uncertainty in the calibration as nominal values for the optical properties of the \gls{pmc} were used. The mixer signal measurement is depends on the cavities linewidth, and as this depends on the cleanliness of mirrors, the \gls{pmc}'s linewidth could be different from its nominal value.  Reference~\cite{Uehara1995} describes measurements which could be used to accurately measure the optical properties of the \gls{pmc}; however these measurements would require additional hardware. 
\par
At frequencies much above the unity gain point of the servo, a signal applied to the PZT will not be suppressed by the loop. Near the unity gain point, a signal applied to the PZT will be attenuated. A peak at 1111\,Hz was used to check the calibration of the loop. The closed loop gain at 1111\,Hz was modelled to be -5.2\,dB, so the peak measured at the mixer should be reduced by a factor of $1-10^{-5.2/20} = 0.45$. The peak generated at the PZT was measured at the mixer to be reduced by a factor of 0.70; the difference between the prediction and measurement would be fixed with better characterisation of the \gls{pmc}.

\section{Conclusion}
The \gls{psl} has undergone a major redesign due to the pointing noise introduced by the cooling for the \gls{hpo}. The new layout consists of an \gls{npro} and two solid state amplifiers followed by the \gls{pmc}. A prototype of this new design was constructed and tested. This layout is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:ligo_layout_chapter_layout}. The setup procedure was established and a cooling scheme was trialled. 
\par
The frequency noise of the beam transmitted by the \gls{pmc} was investigated using signals in the \gls{pmc} control loop. The laser's frequency noise was dominated by fluctuations in the optical path length of the \gls{pmc} as the \gls{pmc} is not shielded from acoustic vibrations. This measurement is shown in Firgure~\ref{fig:ligolaserchapterfrequencynoise}.
\par
The pointing noise was found to exceed to the requirements (See~\ref{fig:ligolaserchapterpointingnoise}). As the pointing noise is close to the requirement, a significant reduction may be achieved when \gls{psl} is in a quieter environment. To reduce the pointing noise further, the flow rates being reduced will help, however this reduces the output power. Turbulence generated by bends in the water cooling pipes could be reduced to lower the pointing noise. Active alignment could be implemented using the light that is reflected from the \gls{pmc}. 
\par
The level of power achieved with this setup will be sufficient for O4 as the usable amount of power is limited by noise couplings which arise due to point absorbers in the test masses~\cite{Buikema2020}. The output power was measured over two days and was found to drop from a peak of 111\,\si{\watt} to 107\,\si{\watt}. This is shown if Figure~\ref{fig:ligolaserchapterpowerplot}. This was due to changes the lab temperature affecting the alignment. Higher output power could be achieved if more pump-diode current and cooling is used. Additionally, to increase the power emerging from the \gls{pmc}, the modematching into the \gls{pmc} could be improved.

100W of intensity stabilised light was transmitted through the \gls{pmc}. The free running intensity noise of the beam transmitted by the \gls{pmc} was measured and the inner-loop intensity stabilisation was tested. Two actuators were considered, an \gls{aom} and a current shunt; the \gls{aom} was chosen as this offers the most flexibility in terms of loop gain and operating power. The inner-loop noise requirement was met with this setup. The stabilised noise is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:ligolaserchapteraomstabilisationnoise}.

Future iterations of the \gls{psl} may use fibre lasers and fibre amplifiers~\cite{Buikema2019,Wellmann2019} as there is rapid development in this technology. These have been demonstrated at the power levels required for LIGO. Coherent power addition is another technique that can be used to obtain high beam powers (e.g.~\cite{Tuennermann2011,Wei2016}). When long term operation has been demonstrated, these technologies may be a viable replacement for the current laser design.
