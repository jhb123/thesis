\section{Quantum Efficiency, Power Handling and Dark Noise Requirements for Photodiodes in a Gravitational Wave detector}
\label{sec:pd_chapter_grav_wave_ifo_requirments}
%Quantum efficiency, power handling and dark noise requirements are three important properties for a photodiode that would be used as for readout of gravitational wave detectors. As discussed in Section~\ref{sec:detector_chapter_photodiodes} and~\ref{sec:detector_chapter_3G}, a new photodiode technology will be required in the readout train of the third generation of gravitational wave detector.
\par
The photodiodes used in a gravitational wave interferometer should have a quantum efficiency of at least $99\%$, as described in~\cite{A+HAM6_design_requirements}. Loss within the interferometer decreases its SNR, as it diminishes the level to which the signal light is squeezed (see Equation~\ref{eq:detector_chapter_loss}). High quantum efficiency is required to detect the target of $\sim$10\,dB of squeezed light that future gravitational wave detectors will use\cite{Hall2020,Reitze2019,Hild2011}. 
\par
In some applications, if a bigger signal is required, the gain can be increased by using a larger resistor in the feedback path of the transimpedance amplifier, or by using an avalanche photodiode. However, neither of these methods would work in a gravitational wave detector as both would boost the noise and the signal in proportion. 
\par
Current gravitational wave detectors run with light in the $\sim10\,\rm{mW}$ at the output (e.g.~\cite{Prijatelj2012}) to ensure that the junk light from other modes is a small portion of the total signal (see Section~\ref{sec:detector_chapter_OMC}). For the remainder of this chapter, as  $q\lambda_{\rm{EM}}/hc$ is of the order unity, the requirement for a photodiode is that it should be able to produce $\sim$10\,mA of photocurrent without saturating.
\par
Photodiodes for gravitational wave detectors should have low $1/f$ dark noise so that the interferometers are limited by quantum noise in their detection bands. Third generation ground based gravitational wave detectors are designed to be quantum noise limited down to about 10\,Hz~\cite{Punturo2010,Hild2011,Hall2020}, so the $1/f$ noise must be sufficiently far below the shot noise of the photocurrent above $10\,\si{\hertz}$. For a 10\,mA photocurrent, the shot noise is $6\times10^{-11}\,\si{\ampere_{\rm{rms}}/\sqrt{\hertz}}$. Below 10\,Hz, other rapidly rising sources of noise are predicted to limit the sensitivity of the Einstein Telescope and Cosmic Explorer.
\par
The $1/f$ noise could, in principle, be avoided by using heterodyne detection. However, a conventional heterodyne detection scheme would increase the shot noise level of the signal~\cite{Schnupp_shot_noise,Meers1991,Niebauer1991}. Although an optimal choice of modulation-demodulation waveform can recover the SNR by increasing the signal~\cite{Meers1991}, the benefits of using squeezed light would be lost because the shot noise would be enhanced compared to homodyne readout because vacuum noise at frequencies that do not contain signal would enter into the measurement. However, it has been proposed that the increase in noise associated with heterodyne readout could be avoided by using a broadband squeezer~\cite{Zhang2020a}.
\section{What is an Extended InGaAs Photodiode?}
\label{sec:pd_chapter_diode_intro}
The performance of an extended InGaAs photodiode depends on the number of defects within it. A basic explanation of how photodiodes work, how these defects arise, and how these defects affect the photodiode is given in this section. More details on the semiconductor physics of diodes and photodiodes can be found in many textbooks e.g.~\cite{Grundmann2010,Protopopov2014}.
%\par
%A photodiode is a square law detector that produces a current that is proportional to the power of the light it is detecting. As photodiodes are low-passed square law devices, they effectively demodulate the light field and produce a signal with a DC component and components proportional to the frequency and amplitude modulations of the field~\cite{Siegman1966}. Usually, photodiodes are of the order 1\,mm in diameter, have a relatively high quantum efficiency and a fast response. Photodiodes often follow a p-doped, intrinsic, n-doped (PIN) semiconductor structure. PIN photodiodes can detect up to tens of milliwatts of light power.
\par
The basic physics of a PIN photodiode can be qualitatively understood by considering a simpler pn junction. A pn junction consists of two types of semiconductor: p-doped and n-doped. These regions have ions which are in fixed positions and charge carriers, known as majority charge carriers, which are free to move. Holes are the majority charge carriers in the p-doped region, and the ions are negatively charged (acceptors). In the n-doped region, electrons are free to move, and the ions are positively charged (donors). In equilibrium, the number of holes is equal to the number of electrons. The free charge carriers will diffuse across the interface between the p-doped and n-doped region. This causes the p-doped and n-doped regions to become charged. This results in a region of the pn junction where there are no free electrons and holes. This is known as the depletion region.
\par
If a photon is absorbed by a pn junction, a free electron-hole pair is generated. A photon may be absorbed by the pn junction if its energy is large enough to move an electron from the valence band into the conduction band. If the photon is absorbed within the depletion region of the photodiode, the electron and hole are separated due to the electric field, and a current is generated in the circuit connected to the photodiode. However, if a photon is absorbed outside the depletion region, then the electron-hole pair will have a large chance of recombining and not contributing to the current in the circuit. The relationship between quantum efficiency, $\eta$, light power, $P_{\rm{EM}}$, and photocurrent, $I_p$ is given by 
\begin{equation}
I_p = \eta\frac{q\lambda_{\rm{EM}}}{hc}P_{\rm{EM}},
\end{equation}
where $\lambda_{\rm{EM}}$ is the wavelength of the light, $h$ is Planck's constant and $c$ is the speed of light.
\par
To widen the depletion region, an external voltage can be used. When a reverse bias is applied to the photodiode, there is a larger electric field difference at the interface between the p-doped and n-doped region; thus, the depletion region is widened i.e.~applying a reverse bias to a photodiode increases the volume of the photodiode in which absorbed photons will generate photocurrent.
\par
To further increase the width of the depletion region, a layer of intrinsic semiconductor can be used to separate the p-doped and n-doped regions. An intrinsic semiconductor has no doping, and the number of electrons equals the number of holes within it. As the intrinsic region physically separates p-doped and n-doped regions, a larger volume of the photodiode will be depleted of charge carriers due to the electric field within the photodiode. Additionally, the capacitance of the photodiode will be decreased.
\par
InGaAs is a widely used semiconductor for making photodiodes. InGaAs needs to be grown on a substrate. To combine two types of semiconductor, they must have similar lattice constants and share the same lattice structure; this is known as lattice matching. Typically, the indium content of InGaAs is around 53\%, this is signified with the chemical formula $\rm{In_{0.53}Ga_{0.47}As}$, so that the InGaAs can be lattice matched to an InP substrate\footnote{Ge is sometimes used as a substrate. This will be lattice matched for InGaAs with an In content around 10\%; this corresponds to the bandgap energy being similar to the energy of a 1\,\si{\micro\metre} photon.}. With this amount of indium, the InGaAs photodiode will suffer from few lattice mismatch defects, and the photodiodes created with can be of high quality. 
\par
Normally, InGaAs photodiodes are sensitive up to wavelengths around 1.6\,\si{\micro\metre}, but longer wavelength sensitivity can be achieved by increasing the indium content of the photodiode (e.g.~\cite{Martinelli1988,Alam2007,Chen2018}). As mentioned in Section~\ref{sec:detector_chapter_photodiodes}, these photodiodes are often called extended, and they can be sensitive for wavelengths up to 3\,\si{\micro\metre}. To absorb 2\,\si{\micro\metre} photons, the indium content needs to be at least 70\%. One of the key issues faced when creating an extended InGaAs photodiode is the strain induced by the lattice mismatch between the InGaAs and the InP substrate~\cite{Gendry1992} as this introduces defects.
\par
Defects caused by the strain between the InGaAs and InP layers of the photodiode generate excess noise, dark current and reduce its quantum efficiency\cite{Joshi1993}. The photodiode can be made thinner to reduce this strain. The critical thickness, i.e.~the thickness above which strain degrades the crystal, for extended InGaAs is of the order 0.1\,nm~\cite{Gendry1992} whereas the absorption length (of the order of $10^4\si{\centi\metre^{-1}}$~\cite{Alam2007}) requires the photodiode to be several microns thick for it to have the high quantum efficiency that is required for the detection of gravitational waves (see Section~\ref{sec:pd_chapter_grav_wave_ifo_requirments}). Therefore, it is highly likely that such a thick extended InGaAs photodiode will have defects. Additionally, because InGaAs is not a good conductor of heat, a thicker photodiode can show reduced power handling\cite{Li2004}. 
\par
The defects mentioned above manifest as abrupt changes in the crystal structure known as misfit dislocations (e.g.~\cite{Uchida1993,Jurczak2017}). The resulting undesirable states in the bandgap that charge carriers can occupy, known as trap states, give rise to a generation-recombination dark current~\cite{Zimmermann2003}; thus, defects are a source of $1/f$ noise in extended InGaAs (see~\cite{Hooge1981} for a description of $1/f$ noise in semiconductors). Defects cause there to be more free charge carriers in the InGaAs which leads to the depletion region becoming smaller, thus causing a decrease in quantum efficiency~\cite{DextquotesingleHondt}. For indium doping levels other than 53\%, buffer layers of InGaAs between the absorbing layer and the substrate can be used to mitigate the lattice mismatch~\cite{Zimmermann2003}. The number of defects depends on the growth conditions of the photodiode, and by improving them, the dark noise of a photodiode made from extended InGaAs can be reduced by orders of magnitude~\cite{Chen2018}. 
\par
An anti-reflection coating can be used to increase the quantum efficiency of a photodiode. For example, $\rm{In_{0.7}Ga_{0.3}As}$ has a bandgap which is equal to the energy of a 2\,\si{\micro\metre} photon, and it has a refractive index, $n$, of approximately $3.7$ at this wavelength. Assuming a single quarter-wave layer was to be applied, the film would need a refractive index of $n_f = \sqrt{3.7}$ and be $\sim\,250\si{\nano\meter}$ thick. The idea of using this anti reflective coating as a passivation layer -- a passive material designed for protecting the semiconductor from the environment -- is presented in~\cite{L_pez_2007}. Some manufacturers create a range of photodiodes with different spectral responses by changing the anti-reflective coating rather than by changing the photodiode's indium content\cite{Gasparian2018}.
\par
The dark noise and dark current of a photodiode have several contributors. In low bias regimes, the dark current is driven by the diffusion of minority carriers~\cite{DextquotesingleHondt,Forrest1981}. However, the thermally driven generation-recombination of electron/hole pairs drives the $1/f$ dark noise at low frequencies and at low bias~\cite{Hooge1981}. Therefore, the dark current does not predict the dark noise; however, as both increase with higher levels of defects, a photodiode with a high dark current will tend to have a high dark noise. When the bias exceeds a certain threshold, electrons can tunnel across the junction, leading to a substantial increase in dark current; this bias is typically of the order $1\,\si{\volt}$ in extended InGaAs photodiodes.
\par
The noise of a photocurrent can be modelled as
\begin{equation}
i_{\rm{rms}} = \sqrt{ 2qi + \frac{4k_BT}{R_{\rm{PD}}} + \left(\frac{i_c}{f^{\beta}}\right)^2 }.
\end{equation}
The first term under the square-root, $2qi$, is the term associated with the shot noise of a current $i$. The second term, $ \frac{4k_BT}{R_{\rm{PD}}}$, is the term related to the Johnson-Nyquist noise due to the shunt resistance of the photodiode, $R_{\rm{PD}}$; this term can become significant when the shunt resistance is low, as it is in some extended InGaAs photodiodes, and microwatt levels of light are being detected. The final term is an empirical relationship known as Hooge's law~\cite{Hooge1981}; $i_c$ is the characteristic noise level and $\beta$ is usually $0.5\pm0.1$.

\section{The Effect of Reverse Bias on the Quantum Efficiency of an Extended InGaAs Photodiode}
\label{sec:pd_chapter_reverse_bias_qe}
The relationship between the reverse bias and quantum efficiency of an extended InGaAs photodiode, the FD10D, was investigated. The two types of measurement used are described in Section~\ref{sec:pd_chapter_qe_method}. Figure~\ref{fig:pdchapterfd10dqeasfnofbias} shows a clear motivation for increasing the reverse bias of an extended InGaAs photodiode. 
\subsection{Method}
\label{sec:pd_chapter_qe_method}
\par
To measure the effect that changing the reverse bias voltage has on the quantum efficiency of a photodiode, two approaches were used. It is straightforward to determine the change in quantum efficiency by measuring the photocurrent as a function of reverse bias. Alternatively, one can modulate the bias and measure the modulation in the photocurrent. If the quantum efficiency, $\eta$, depends on the reverse bias of the photodiode, modulating the reverse bias, $V_B$, at a frequency $f$ will result in $\eta$ modulating at $f$ and so the measurement will be of $\Delta\eta/\Delta V_B$. The input modulation $\Delta V_{\rm{in}} = \Delta V_B$ and the output modulation is $\Delta V_{\rm{out}}\cos\theta = \Delta \eta V_{\rm{TIA}}(V_B)$, where $V_{\rm{TIA}}(V_B)$ is the DC signal from the photodiode and $\theta$ is the phase between the input and output modulation. The quantum efficiency should be independent of frequency if the photodiode is operating in a time-independent, linear fashion and so the two types of measurement should yield the same outcome.
\par
To prove that the increase in signal was a result of the photodiode's quantum efficiency increasing and there were no non-linear mechanisms by which photocurrent was generated, the shot noise level of the photocurrent was measured. This measurement required a sufficiently quiet photodiode circuit and an amplitude stabilised laser.
\par
To measure photocurrent with sufficient SNR, a transimpedance amplifier with electronic noise 25 times below the shot noise level of the expected signal (10\,mA) photocurrent was used. This circuit is shown in Appendix~\ref{sec:circuits_appendix_variable_bias_ad797}. The circuit allowed for the reverse bias of the photodiode to be set externally via a low-passed bench-top power supply. The low-pass filter had two poles at 16\,Hz.
\par
The optical layout is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:pdchaptershotnoisetestsetup}. A laser with a power of 50\,mW and a wavelength of 1.55\,\si{\micro\metre} (Pure Photonics PPCL300) was used to illuminate the FD10D, the device under test. The light from the laser was split by a 50:50 beam splitter so that a suitable amount could be used to amplitude stabilise the laser. The in-loop photodiode used to sense the laser's power was a high-performance InGaAs photodiode (C3061GH). The laser was amplitude stabilised by actuating on the current of the laser's pump-diode. The amplitude stabilisation servo had sufficient gain so that the in-loop noise was below the level of shot noise of a 10\,mA current, so this could be used to measure shot noise on the out-of-loop photodiode. With this apparatus, shot noise limited measurements could be made above 1\,kHz. A half waveplate and polarising beam splitter were used to adjust the power level of the light incident on the FD10D, and a lens was used to set the size of the beam.
\begin{figure}
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=1\linewidth]{Figures/PD_chapter_shot_noise_test_set_up}
	\caption{Optical layout used for measuring the effect that increasing the reverse bias of a photodiode had on its quantum efficiency and saturation limit. 	\label{fig:pdchaptershotnoisetestsetup}

\par
As the FD10D has a diameter of 1\,mm, the laser beam could not have a width larger than $330\,\si{\micro\metre}$ for there to be $<1\%$ loss due to clipping. For the reasons stated in Section~\ref{sec:pd_chapter_grav_wave_ifo_requirments}, the FD10D was tested at a power level of $\sim10\,\rm{mW}$. The methods in this section were used to measure relative increases in quantum efficiency; measurements of absolute quantum efficiency require a calibrated power meter. A method of measuring the absolute quantum efficiency of a photodiode with squeezed light is presented in~\cite{Vahlbruch_2016}.
\par
The photodiode was biased such that the dark noise was sufficiently low (see Section~\ref{sec:photodiode_chapter_dark_noise} for details), and the noise of the photocurrent was compared to the level expected for shot noise. The expected level of noise, $n$, in units of $\rm{A_{rms}/\sqrt{Hz}}$, is given by
\begin{equation}
n = \sqrt{2qI_{\rm{OOL}} + 2qI_{\rm{IL}} + n_{\rm{IL\,dark}}^2 +   n_{\rm{OOL\,dark}}^2 + n_{\rm{loop}}^2 + n^2_{\rm{analyser}}}.
\label{eq:pd_chapter_noise_budget}
\end{equation}
The first two terms under the square root represent the shot noise of the current produced by the in-loop and out-of-loop photodiodes, $n_{\rm{OOL/IL\,dark}}$ is the dark noise of the out-of-loop photodiode and in-loop photodiode, $n_{\rm{loop}}$ is the electronic noise of the loop and $n_{\rm{analyser}}$ is the noise of the analyser.
\par
The light from the laser was amplitude modulated at 1111\,Hz by adding a signal to the laser's pump-diode error signal (see Figure~\ref{fig:pdchaptershotnoisetestsetup}). This modulation was used to calibrate the measurements of noise as the relative intensity of this peak was known and could be checked against the signal measured by the high-performing in-loop photodiode. By tracking the height of an intensity modulation in the light for a series of powers up to 16\,mW, the saturation limit of the photodiode was investigated. A similar type of measurement is reported in~\cite{L_pez_2010}.
\subsection{Results}
\label{sec:pd_chapter_qe_results}
The effect of increasing the reverse bias on the quantum efficiency of the FD10D is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:pdchapterfd10dqeasfnofbias}. \section{Dark Current and Dark Noise in Extended\,InGaAs Photodiodes}
\label{sec:photodiode_chapter_dark_noise}
Following the observation that both dark noise and quantum efficiency both increase with reverse bias, there is a compromise to make between quantum efficiency and dark noise. A maximum reverse bias voltage that balanced quantum efficiency and noise was found for each photodiode listed in Table~\ref{tab:pd_chapter_photodiodes_tested}. See Section~\ref{sec:pd_chapter_qe_results} for details on the dependence of quantum efficiency on reverse bias. The dark noise of the photodiode should be below the shot noise level of the detected light. For the reasons discussed in Section~\ref{sec:pd_chapter_grav_wave_ifo_requirments}, the shot noise of a 10\,mA photocurrent, $6\times10^{-11}\,\si{\ampere_{\rm{rms}}/\sqrt{\hertz}}$, was used as a benchmark for the photodiodes' dark noise at a frequency of 10\,Hz. 
\par
Low dark currents were expected, so a transimpedance amplifier with higher gain than in Section~\ref{sec:pd_chapter_reverse_bias_qe} was used. This circuit, shown in Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix_dark_noise_tia}, could measure current noise of at least $0.2\,\si{\pico\ampere_{\rm{rms}}/\sqrt{\hertz}}$ between 10\,Hz--1000\,Hz.
\par
The photodiodes were placed in a light tight box while the measurements were being made. Measurements of the dark noise of photodiodes at room temperature are reported in Section~\ref{sec:pd_chapter_room_temp_experiment}. As the dark noise of a photodiode depends on its temperature, an experiment was performed to explore this (see Section~\ref{sec:pd_chapter_cooling_experiment}).

Since excess broadband noise was observed, there was concern of bias voltage noise coupling to the photocurrent via the photodiode's impedance. Thus, a noise projection measurement was made. For a description of noise projection measurements, see~\cite{Smith2005}. The transfer function between the voltage at the anode of the photodiode and the output of the transimpedance amplifier was measured while 1\,mA of photocurrent was being generated by the photodiode. A calibration modulation was applied to the bias voltage, and the noise at the anode of the photodiode and the noise at the output of the transimpedance amplifier were simultaneously measured. Using this method, 42.5\,dB of bias voltage noise was subtracted from the measurement; however, this did not remove the excess broadband noise. This is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:pdchapternoiseprojection}.

The equation of a diode contains a variable called the \emph{ideality factor}. If the ideality factor differs significantly from unity, additional behaviour that was not described in Section~\ref{sec:pd_chapter_diode_intro} may be occurring within the photodiode. Thus, the ideality factor of the IG24x500S4i was investigated. The equation of a photodiode is 
\begin{equation}
I = I_p + I_s \left(\exp\left(-\frac{qV_b}{nk_BT}\right) - 1 \right),
\label{eq:pd_chapter_ideality_factor}
\end{equation}
where $I_p$ is the photocurrent, $I_s$ is the saturation current of the photodiode, $q$ is the charge of the electron, $V_b$ is the bias of the photodiode, $n$ is the ideality factor, $k_B$ is Boltzmann's constant and $T$ is the temperature of the photodiode. The current flowing through the photodiode as a function of forward bias was measured and is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:pdchapterforwardbiasiv}. The SciPy python function \verb|scipy.optimize.curve_fit| was used to do an unweighted fit of the data to Equation~\ref{eq:pd_chapter_ideality_factor}. The photodiode was found to have $n\simeq1$. The $I_p$ term in Equation~\ref{eq:pd_chapter_ideality_factor} was checked by shining light onto the photodiode. This is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:pdchapterforwardbiasmodelwithlight}.

\section{Noise Properties of an InSb Detector}
\label{sec:pd_chapter_insb}
InSb photodetectors are sensitive to light with wavelengths between $1\,\si{\micro\metre}-5\,\si{\micro\metre}$, and they have been used by the infra-red astronomy community (e.g.~\cite{Hodapp2003}). InSb detectors have near unity quantum efficiency above 2.5\,\si{\micro\metre}, and below 2.5\,\si{\micro\metre}, their quantum efficiency can exceed unity due to multi-electron processes~\cite{Beattie1962}. Because InSb detectors are sensitive to light with wavelengths up to $5\si{\micro\metre}$, they may need shielding from heat sources if they were used in a gravitational wave interferometer. While a quantum efficiency greater than unity does not necessarily correspond to worse noise performance, multi-electron processes can convert some of the detected photon energy to heat i.e.~introduce a loss mechanism.
\par
The noise of the photocurrent produced by a Hamamatsu P5968 detector was measured. The transimpedance amplifier used to measure the photocurrent is shown in Section~\ref{sec:appendix_dark_noise_tia}. InSb detectors must be operated at low temperatures, so liquid nitrogen was used to cool the photodiode to 77\,\si{\kelvin}. The detector was characterised by covering its aperture with a low emissivity material (aluminium foil) which was at room temperature. The P5968's window lets light between $1.5\,\si{\micro\metre}-5.3\,\si{\micro\metre}$ pass. The viewing angle of the detector was \ang{60}; based on the solid angle covered by the material as seen by the detector, the expected level of thermal radiation that would be detected could be calculated. The expected photocurrent due to the aluminium foil was 5.5\,\si{\micro\ampere}. The DC current from the device was measured to be 5.3\,\si{\micro\ampere}, so the photocurrent can be explained by the thermal radiation of the aluminium foil. The results from this test are shown in Figure~\ref{fig:pdchapterp5968noise}. This corresponds to the device having a quantum efficiency of $\sim90\%$.

\section{Discussion}
None of the photodiodes tested in this chapter would meet the requirements described in Section~\ref{sec:pd_chapter_grav_wave_ifo_requirments} because the quantum efficiency is specified in all of their datasheets to be below 99\%. Communication with four commercial manufacturers of extended InGaAs photodiodes suggest that there is no off-the-shelf photodiode capable of meeting the requirements set out in Section~\ref{sec:pd_chapter_grav_wave_ifo_requirments}~\cite{Gasparian2018,Hornby2018,Miron2018,Kalinina2018}; the results in Section~\ref{sec:pd_chapter_reverse_bias_qe} and Section~\ref{sec:photodiode_chapter_dark_noise} support this and give insights into the best possible operating conditions for an off-the-shelf extended InGaAs photodiode. As discussed in Section~\ref{sec:pd_chapter_diode_intro}, high quality extended InGaAs photodiodes are difficult to fabricate because of the lattice mismatch between the InGaAs and the InP substrate.
\par
As only two copies of most photodiodes were available for testing, batch-level statistics cannot be obtained from these results. For instance, the FD10D reported on in this chapter has lower dark noise than the G12183-010k; however, this does not tell one if FD10Ds are generally better than G12183-010ks. Due to the nature of semiconductors with high amounts of strain, inter and intra wafer variability is expected. For example, there was a factor of $\sim100$ between the dark noise of the two G12182-010ks that were tested (see Figure~\ref{fig:pdchapterg12182-010knoisecompare}). Whether this is due to their manufacturing or how they were handled prior to purchase is unclear, however this result shows that it may be worth buying several extended InGaAs photodiodes and selecting the best ones from them. Additionally, the results only cover seven different models of extended InGaAs photodiode, and it is possible that another photodiode manufacturer will have better performing photodiodes than the ones tested in this chapter.
\par
Section~\ref{sec:pd_chapter_reverse_bias_qe} shows the effect that biasing an extended InGaAs photodiode can have on its quantum efficiency. The quantum efficiency of the FD10D was increased by $15\%$ when its bias was set to its maximum value. This increase in quantum efficiency occurs because the depletion region of the FD10D was made larger by applying a reverse bias, and because the electron-hole pairs that are excited by the incoming photons spend less time within the depletion region when the bias is larger, thus they have a smaller chance of recombining with another hole/electron. This motivates using as large a bias as possible as the quantum efficiency can be greatly increased.
\par
The high dark noise associated with the bias found in the IG24x500S4i and IG26x500S4i photodiodes (see Section~\ref{sec:pdchapterlasercomponentssaturation}) meant that they had to be operated with low biases if they where to produce shot noise limited photocurrent at frequencies above 10\,Hz. This resulted in them having a low saturation level, see Figure~\ref{fig:pdchapterlasercomponentsratio}. To avoid this saturation limit, one could split the beam and use an array of photodiodes to detect the light. However, due to each photodiode in the array contributing the same amount of dark noise, each photodiode would need to have less dark noise compared to if it was detected on one photodiode\footnote{Splitting the light onto an array of $k$ photodiodes makes the SNR a factor of $\sqrt{k}$ worse. As electronic noise adds in quadrature, the SNR would be $S/\sqrt{k}n$, where $n$ is the noise of an individual photodiode.}. Instead, better performing photodiodes could be used; for example, the FD10D showed no signs of saturation, see Figure~\ref{fig:pdchapterfd10dacdc}, when operating with a bias that fulfilled the noise criteria. 
\par
Applying a reverse bias to a photodiode reduces its capacitance, however the few hundred picofarads of capacitance that photodiodes have at low bias voltage is a manageable amount to design a transimpedance amplifier around for a DC readout or BHD (see Chapter~\ref{chapter:in_air_MZ}) scheme. For instance, the photodiode circuit used in Section~\ref{sec:pd_chapter_reverse_bias_qe} (see Appendix~\ref{sec:circuits_appendix_variable_bias_ad797}) has a large enough bandwidth for a photodiode used in gravitational wave interferometer and was quiet enough to be limited by the shot noise of a 10\,mA photocurrent over a bandwidth relevant in ground-based gravitational wave detection.
\par
While the dark current does not depend strongly on the reverse bias when the bias is below the tunnelling threshold, the dark noise of every photodiode increased as its reverse bias increased. Although photodiodes with higher dark current tended to have higher dark noise, the dark noise could not be predicted from the dark current. For instance, the FD10D had a larger dark current but lower noise than the IG24x500S4i. The dark current tended to be greater in larger photodiodes; this is due to larger photodiodes being more likely to have a greater number of defects as well them having more edge-related current. However, the IG24x500S4i and IG26x500S4i photodiodes had higher dark noise. 
\par
As the dark current and dark noise of a photodiode increases exponentially with temperature, the dark noise of a photodiode can be significantly reduced by cooling it. Extended InGaAs photodiodes can be purchased with thermoelectric coolers. Once the quantum efficiency and dark noise problems surrounding extended InGaAs photodiodes have been solved, the integration of such a photodiode into a vacuum system could be investigated.
\section{Conclusion}
Extended InGaAs photodiodes are widely available detectors that can be used to sense $2\,\si{\micro\metre}$ light; however, off-the-shelf detectors do not fulfil the requirements of a photodiode that can be used in a gravitational wave detector. In particular, this is due to them not having a large enough quantum efficiency, and this leads to a relative increase in the shot noise of the detected light.
\par
It is technically challenging to create extended InGaAs photodiodes with high quantum efficiency due to the lattice mismatch between the InP substrate and the InGaAs. While the depletion region of a photodiode can be made larger by running the photodiode with a reverse bias, the dark noise of an extended InGaAs photodiode increases when reverse biased. Measurements of the dark noise as a function of reverse bias (see Figure~\ref{fig:pdchapterdarkcurrentmegaplot}) and temperature (see Figure~\ref{fig:pdchapterdarkcurrenttermperaturealldiodes}) were made to find the operating conditions which balances quantum efficiency and dark noise.
\par
To overcome the manufacturing challenges associated with creating extended $\rm{InGaAs}$ photodiodes, a wafer would need to be commissioned. Increases in quantum efficiency can be obtained by coating the photodiodes in an anti-reflective film; however, the primary challenge of creating high quantum efficiency photodiodes is making them thick enough while keeping defect level low. Reference~\cite{Moseley1986} shows results from a photodiode with 95\% quantum efficiency and a cut-off wavelength of 2.4\,\si{\micro\meter}, however due to the exponential dependence of absorption on thickness, it is still a significant challenge to reach $99\%$ quantum efficiency. Future work may include investigating the growth conditions for extended InGaAs photodiodes.
\par
Alternative photodiode materials could be used to detect $2\,\si{\micro\meter}$ light, and a range of cryogenic photodetectors have been used in infrared telescopes. We investigated InSb detectors (see Section~\ref{sec:pd_chapter_insb}) as they have near unity quantum efficiency above $2.5\,\si{\micro\meter}$, however its characteristics at 2\,\si{\micro\meter} are still unclear. To investigate these, the device's window would need removing and a 2\,\si{\micro\meter} laser with several milliwatts of power would be needed because the device was not sufficiently sensitive to the laser that was available. HgCdTe is another material used to detect infrared light, and HgCdTe detectors under development by colleagues~\cite{UmanaMembreno2020} could be used to make a photodiode with at least $99\%$ quantum efficiency at 2\,\si{\micro\meter} as HgCdTe does not suffer from strain related issues; however, the optimal growth conditions for these devices has not been established, and the linearity and dark noise of such HgCdTe photodiodes is unknown.
