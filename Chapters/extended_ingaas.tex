\section{Requirements for the Readout Photodiodes in a Gravitational Wave detector}
\label{sec:pd_chapter_grav_wave_ifo_requirments}
Quantum efficiency, power handling and dark noise requirements are three important properties for a photodiode that would be used as for readout of gravitational wave detectors. As discussed in Section \ref{sec:detector_chapter_photodiodes} and \ref{sec:detector_chapter_3G}, a new photodiode technology will be required in the readout train of the third generation of gravitational wave detector.
\paragraph{}
The photodiodes used in the readout of a gravitational wave interferometer should have a quantum efficiency $>99\%$ \cite{A+HAM6_design_requirements}. Loss within the interferometer decreases its SNR as it diminishes the level to which the signal light is squeezed (see Equation \ref{eq:detector_chapter_loss}). High quantum efficiency is required to detect the baseline level of $\sim$10\,dB of squeezed light that future gravitational wave detectors will use\cite{Hall2020,Reitze2019,Hild2011}. Usually, if a bigger signal is required, the gain can be increased by using a larger resistor in the feedback path of the transimpedance amplifier or by using an avalanche photodiode, however neither of these methods would work in a gravitational wave detector as both would boost the noise and the signal in proportion.
\paragraph{}
Current gravitational wave detectors operate in the 1-10\,mW range at the output \cite{Prijatelj2012} to ensure that the junk light from other modes is a small portion of the total signal. As  $q\lambda_{\rm{EM}}/hc$ is of the order unity, the photocurrent will be assumed to be $\sim$10\,mA and so any new photodiode should be able to produce $\sim$10\,mA of photocurrent without saturating. If heterodyne readout was to be used, then the DC light power could be reduced.
\paragraph{}
Photodiodes for gravitational wave detectors should have low $1/f$ dark noise so that the interferometers are not limited in their detection bands by electronic noise. Third generation ground based gravitational wave detectors will be quantum noise limited down to about 10\,Hz \cite{Harry2010,Hild2011}, so the $1/f$ noise must be below the shot noise of the photocurrent above this frequency. For a 10\,mA photocurrent, the shot noise is $6\times10^{-11}\,\si{\ampere_{\rm{rms}}/\sqrt{\hertz}}$. Below 10\,Hz, other rapidly rising sources of noise will limit the sensitivity of the Einstein Telescope \cite{Punturo2010,Hild2011} and Cosmic Explorer \cite{Hall2020}
\paragraph{}
The $1/f$ noise could be avoided by using heterodyne detection, however a conventional heterodyne detection scheme would increase the shot noise level of the signal \cite{Schnupp_shot_noise,Meers1991,Niebauer1991}. Heterodyne detection can decrease the SNR of the interferometer if the mixing of the modulation and demodulation waveforms was not time independent \cite{Meers1991}. Although an optimal choice of modulation-demodulation waveform can recover the SNR by increasing the signal, the benefits of using squeezed light would be lost because the shot noise would be enhanced compared to homodyne readout by vacuum noise at frequencies that does not contain signal entering into the measurement. However, it has been proposed that the increase in noise associated with heterodyne readout could be avoided by the use of a broadband squeezer \cite{Zhang2020a}.
\section{What is an Extended InGaAs Photodiode}
\label{sec:pd_chapter_diode_intro}
In this section, a qualitative explanation of what an extended InGaAs photodiode is and how photodiodes work will be given. More details on the semiconductor physics of diodes and photodiodes can be found in many textbooks e.g~\cite{Grundmann2010,Protopopov2014}. The relationship between defects in InGaAs photodiode and properties such as dark current, dark noise and quantum efficiency of photodiodes will be discussed. 
%\paragraph{}
%A photodiode is a square law detector that produces a current that is proportional to the power of the light it is detecting. As photodiodes are low-passed square law devices, they effectively demodulate the light field and produce a signal with a DC component and components proportional to the frequency and amplitude modulations of the field \cite{Siegman1966}. Usually, photodiodes are of the order 1\,mm in diameter, have a relatively high quantum efficiency and a fast response. Photodiodes often follow a p-doped, intrinsic, n-doped (PIN) semiconductor structure. PIN photodiodes can detect up to tens of milliwatts of light power.
\paragraph{}
The basic physics of a PIN photodiode can be qualitatively understood by considering a simpler pn junction. A pn junction  consists of two types of semiconductor: p-doped and n-doped. In the p-doped region, holes (the majority charge carriers) are free to move and there are negatively charged ions (acceptors) that are fixed in position. In the n-doped region, electrons (the majority charge carriers) are free to move and there are positively charged ions (donors) fixed in position. In equilibrium, the number of holes is equal to the number of electrons. The free electrons/holes will diffuse across the interface between the p-doped and n-doped region. This causes the p-doped and n-doped regions to become charged. This results in a region of the pn junction where there are no free electrons and holes. The region of the device containing no free carriers is called the depletion region.
\paragraph{}
When a photon is absorbed by a pn junction, a free electron-hole will be generated. If this photon is absorbed within the depletion region of the photodiode, the electron and hole will be separated due to the electric field and will generate a current in the circuit connected to the photodiode. However, if a photon is absorbed outside the depletion region, then the electron-hole pair will recombine and will not contribute to the current in the circuit. The quantum efficiency of a photodiode is the proportion of photons that are converted into photocurrent. The relationship between quantum efficiency, $\eta$, light power,$P_{\rm{EM}}$, and photocurrent, $I_p$ is
\begin{equation}
I_p = \eta\frac{q\lambda_{\rm{EM}}}{hc}P_{\rm{EM}},
\end{equation}
where $\lambda_{\rm{EM}}$ is the wavelength of the light, $h$ is Planck's constant and $c$ is the speed of light.
\paragraph{}
To widen the depletion region, an external voltage can be used. When a \emph{reverse bias} -- a positive voltage across the cathode and anode -- is applied to the photodiode, there is a larger electric field difference at the interface between the p-doped and n-doped region; thus the depletion region is widened i.e.~applying a reverse bias to a photodiode increases the volume of the photodiode in which absorbed photons will generate photocurrent.
\paragraph{}
To further increase the width of the depletion region, a layer of intrinsic semiconductor can be used to separate the p-doped and n-doped region. An intrinsic semiconductor has no doping and the number of electrons equals the number of holes within it. As the intrinsic region physically separates p-doped and n-doped regions, a larger amount of the diode will be depleted of electrons and holes due to the electric field within the diode. Addtionally, the capacitance of the diode will be decreased.
\paragraph{}
InGaAs is a widely used semiconductor for making photodiodes. Typically, the indium content is around 53\%, sometimes this is made clear by writing $\rm{In_{0.53}Ga_{0.47}As}$, so that the InGaAs can be lattice matched to the InP substrate\footnote{Ge can also be used} on which the InGaAs is grown. With this amount of indium, the InGaAs photodiode will suffer from few lattice mismatch defects and so the photodiodes created with it will be of high quality. Normally they are sensitive up to wavelengths around 1.6\,\si{\micro\metre}.
\paragraph{}
Longer wavelength sensitivity can be achieved by increasing the  indium content of the photodiode~\cite{Martinelli1988}. These photodiodes are often called `extended' and can be sensitive for wavelengths up to 3\,\si{\micro\metre}. One of the main issues faced when creating an extended InGaAs photodiode is the strain induced by lattice mismatch between the InGaAs and the InP substrate \cite{Gendry1992} as this introduces defects.
\paragraph{}
Defects caused by the strain between the InGaAs and InP layers of the photodiode generate excess noise, dark current and reduce its quantum efficiency\cite{Joshi1993}. The photodiode can be made thinner to reduce this strain, although this reduces the quantum efficiency. The critical thickness, that is the thickness above which strain degrades the crystal, for extended InGaAs is of the order 0.1\,nm \cite{Gendry1992} whereas the absorption length (of the order of $10^4\si{\centi\metre^{-1}}$ \cite{Alam2007}) requires the photodiode to be several microns thick for it to have the high quantum efficiency that is required for the detection of gravitational waves (see Section \ref{sec:pd_chapter_grav_wave_ifo_requirments}). Additionally, because InGaAs is not a good conductor of heat, a thicker amount of InGaAs can lead to a lower power threshold before the photodiode is thermally destroyed\cite{Li2004}. 
\paragraph{}
These defects manifest as abrupt changes in the crystal structure known as misfit dislocations (e.g.~\cite{Uchida1993} \cite{Jurczak2017}). Defects produce trap states and these give rise to a generation-recombination dark current\cite{Zimmermann2003}; thus defects are the source of 1/f noise \cite{Hooge1981}. To obtain high quantum efficiency the defect level must be low \cite{DextquotesingleHondt}. Defects increase the doping level of the absorbing layer of the photodiode which causes the depletion region to become smaller, thus causing a decrease in quantum efficiency. For indium doping levels other than 53\%, buffer layers of InGaAs between the absorbing layer and the substrate can be used to mitigate the lattice mismatch \cite{Zimmermann2003}.
\paragraph{}
InGaAs that is optimised for 2\,\si{\micro\metre} has a refractive index, $n$, of approximately $3.6$ \cite{Alam2007}. Assuming a single quarter-wave layer was to be applied, the film would need a refractive index that was $n_f = \sqrt{3.6}$ and be 600\,\si{\nano\meter} thick. The idea of using this anti reflective coating as a passivation layer is presented in \cite{L_pez_2007}. Some manufacturers create a range of photodiodes with different spectral responses by changing the anti-reflective coating rather than changing the photodiode's indium content\cite{Gasparian2018}.
\paragraph{}
The dark noise and dark current of a photodiode has several contributors. In low bias regimes, the dark current is driven by the diffusion of minority carriers \cite{DextquotesingleHondt,Forrest1981}. However, the thermally driven generation-recombination of electron/hole pairs drives the dark noise at low biases \cite{Hooge1981}. Therefore, the dark current does not predict the dark noise, however as both of them increase with higher levels of defects, a photodiode with a high dark current will have a high dark noise. When the bias is large enough, electrons can tunnel across the junction leading to a great increase in dark current.
\paragraph{}
The noise of the current made by the photodiode can be modelled as
\begin{equation}
i_{\rm{rms}} = \sqrt{ 2qi + \frac{4k_BT}{R_{\rm{PD}}} + \left(\frac{i_c}{f^{\beta}}\right)^2 }.
\end{equation}
The first term under the square-root, $2qi$, is the shot noise associated with the DC current $i$. The second term, $ \frac{4k_BT}{R_{\rm{PD}}}$, is the Johnson-Nyquist noise due to the shunt resistance of the photodiode, $R_{\rm{PD}}$; this term can become significant when the shunt resistance is low, as it is in some extended InGaAs photodiodes, and microwatt levels of light is being detected. The final term is Hooge's law of noise in semiconductors \cite{Hooge1981}; $i_c$ is the characteristic noise level and $\beta$ is usually $0.5\pm0.1$.
\section{Selected Off-the-shelf Extended InGaAs Photodiodes}
The dark current and dark noise was characterised in a selection of commercially available photodiodes. The quantum efficiency as a function of bias was measured for one of the photodiodes, the FD10D, and the saturation limit of the Laser Components photodiodes was investigated. The photodiodes that were tested and their properties according to their datasheets\footnote{Datasheets often quote the noise equivalent power (NEP) of a photodiode. The NEP is the noise spectral density of the dark current generated by the detector divided by the responsitvity of the photodiode. For the Hamamatsu g12183 series photodiodes, the responsitivity is $\sim1\,\si{\ampere/\watt}$ and the NEP is $\sim10^{-12}\si{\watt/\sqrt{\hertz}}$, so the noise, as claimed by the datasheet, is $\sim10^{-12}\si{\ampere/\sqrt{\hertz}}$. This would seem sufficiently low to be shot noise limited down to $\sim1\si{\micro\watt}$. However, this number does not capture the behaviour of the photodiode at illumination levels of several milliwatts where a bias is required to ensure the photodiode does not saturate. As the noise of these photodiodes is not flat (see Appendix \ref{chapter:pd_appendix_pd_noise}), NEP should be specified as a function of frequency. The noise and responsitivity are both functions of reverse bias and so the NEP is a function of bias. Another quantity related to the NEP is the specific detectivity; this incorporates the area and bandwidth of the photodiode with the NEP.} are listed in Table \ref{tab:pd_chapter_photodiodes_tested}. According to each of the photodiodes datasheets, none of them have a quantum efficiency of $>99$\%; these photodiodes have quantum efficiencies around 80\%. However, the quantum efficiency of a photodiode can be improved by increasing the bias voltage (see Section \ref{sec:pd_chapter_reverse_bias_qe}). 
\paragraph{}
Increasing the bias increases the dark current and dark noise of the photodiode and so for each photodiode a maximum reverse bias voltage was found. The benchmark used was the photodiode's dark noise at 10\,Hz; the maximum useful bias was determined by the voltage where the dark noise was equal to the shot noise of a 10\,mA current. The bias voltage also affects the saturation limit of the photodiode. If a photodiode was found to have a low saturation threshold ($\sim1\,\si{\milli\ampere}$) then it would not be fit for use in a gravitational wave detector.

\section{The Effect of Reverse Bias on the Quantum Efficiency of an Extended InGaAs Photodiode}
\label{sec:pd_chapter_reverse_bias_qe}
The relationship between the reverse bias and quantum efficiency of an extended InGaAs photodiode, the Thorlabs FD10D, was investigated. The FD10D was exposed to a variety of beams so that saturation effects would be visible. Two styles of measurements were used: one where the reverse bias and the response of the photodiode were measured at DC and a lock-in style measurement where the quantity $\frac{\Delta \eta}{\Delta V_B}$ was measured. The quantum efficiency should be independent of frequency if the photodiode is operating in a time-independent, linear fashion and so the two styles of measurements should yield the same outcome. To prove that the increase in signal was a result of the photodiode's quantum efficiency increasing, and not some other mechanism e.g.~an avalanche effect, the photodiode was biased such that the dark noise was sufficiently low (see Section \ref{sec:photodiode_chapter_dark_noise} for details.) and the noise of the photocurrent was compared to the level expected for shot noise.
\subsection{Method}
To measure the photocurrent produced by the photodiode, a transimpedance amplifier which could produce shot noise limited signals for 10\,mA photocurrents was used. This circuit is shown in Appendix \ref{sec:circuits_appendix_variable_bias_ad797}. The circuit allowed for the reverse bias of the photodiode to be set externally via a low-passed bench-top power supply. 
\paragraph{}
A 1550\,nm wavelength laser (Pure Photonics PPCL300) was used to illuminate the FD10D. The light from the laser was split by a 50:50 beam splitter so that the laser could be amplitude stabilised. The in-loop photodiode was a high performance InGaAs photodiode (C3061GH). The FD10D was the out-of-loop photodiode. The laser was amplitude stabilised by actuating on the laser photodiode's current. Shot noise limited measurements could be made above 1\,kHz. A half waveplate and polarising beam splitter were used to control the power level of the light incident on the FD10D and a lens was used to set the size of the beam.
\begin{figure}
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=1\linewidth]{Figures/PD_chapter_shot_noise_test_set_up}
	\caption{Optical layout used for measuring the effect that increasing the reverse bias of a photodiode has on its quantum efficiency and saturation limit. The out-of-loop photodiode, $\rm{PD_{OOL}}$, was the photodiode under test. A 1550\,nm laser was used to test the optical properties of the photodiode. A combination of half wave plate (HWP) and polarised beam splitter (PBS) allowed for the power of the beam to be set and a lens was used to set the size of the beam on the photodiode under test. 	\label{fig:pdchaptershotnoisetestsetup}
\end{figure}
\paragraph{}
The FD10D was exposed to a variety of beams to investigate the possibility that current density related phenomena could affect the results. This was done by changing the size and power of the beam. As the FD10D has a diameter of 1\,mm, the laser beam should not have a width larger than $330\,\si{\micro\metre}$ for there to be $<1\%$ losses due to clipping. As gravitational wave detectors operate with 1-10\,mW of power, the FD10D was tested with powers in this range. The methods in this section can only be used to measure relative increases in quantum efficiency; measurements of absolute quantum efficiency require \textcolor{red}{find ref \ref{}} additional apparatus.
\paragraph{}
To measure the effect that changing the reverse bias voltage has on the quantum efficiency of a photodiode, two approaches were used. It is straightforward to determined the change in quantum efficiency by measuring the change in photocurrent at DC. Alternatively, one can perform a lock-in style measurement. If the quantum efficiency depends on the reverse bias of the photodiode, modulating the reverse bias, $V_B$, at a frequency $f$ will result in $\eta$ modulating at $f$ and so the measurement will be of $\Delta\eta/\Delta V_B$. The input modulation $\Delta V_{\rm{in}} = \Delta V_B$ and the output modulation is $\Delta V_{\rm{out}}\cos\theta = \Delta \eta V_{\rm{TIA}}(V_B)$, where $V_{\rm{TIA}}(V_B)$ is the DC signal from the photodiode and $\theta$ is the phase between the input and output modulation.
\paragraph{}
As 0.6\,V reverse bias was the highest bias that gave an acceptable level of dark noise (see Figure \ref{fig:pdchapterfd10ddarknoise}), the noise of the photocurrent was measured at a region where the noise of the laser was limited by shot noise. The expected level of noise, $n$, in units of $\rm{A_{rms}/\sqrt{Hz}}$, is
\begin{equation}
n = \sqrt{2qI_{\rm{OOL}} + 2qI_{\rm{IL}} + n_{\rm{IL\,dark}}^2 +   n_{\rm{OOL\,dark}}^2 + n_{\rm{loop}}^2 + n^2_{\rm{analyser}}}
\label{eq:pd_chapter_noise_budget}
\end{equation}
where $q$ is the charge of the electron, $I_{\rm{OOL/IL}}$ is the current through the out-of-loop and in-loop photodiodes, $n_{\rm{OOL/IL\,dark}}$ is the dark noise of the out-of-loop photodiodes and in-loop photodiode, $n_{\rm{loop}}$ is the electronic noise of the loop and $n_{\rm{analyser}}$ is the noise of the analyser.
\paragraph{}
The light incident onto the FD10D was amplitude modulated at 1111\,Hz. This modulation was used to calibrate the measurements of noise as the relative intensity of this peak was known and could be checked against the signal measured by the high performing in-loop photodiode. By tracking the height of an intensity modulation in the light for a series of powers up to 16\,mW, the saturation limit of the photodiode could be investigated.
\subsection{Results}
The effect of increasing the reverse bias on the quantum efficiency of the FD10D is shown in Figure \ref{fig:pdchapterfd10dqeasfnofbias}. For all beam intensities that the photodiode was exposed to, the increase in quantum efficiency was $\sim15\%$ as the reverse bias went from 0\,V to 1.8\,V, the stated maximum reverse bias in the FD10D's datasheet. There may be a saturation related effect at the 1\% level, beams with a lower intensity experienced a greater increase in $\eta$ than beams with a higher intensity, however the quantum efficiency may not be uniform over the photodiode -- the spatial dependence of $\eta$ can be crater-like with $\eta$ being greatest at the edges of the photodiode. The difference between the DC and AC response could only be described by a non-linear process. These effects are small and Figure \ref{fig:pdchapterfd10dqeasfnofbias} gives a clear incentive to increase the bias voltage to improve the quantum efficiency of the FD10D.

\paragraph{}
Figure \ref{fig:pdchapterfd10dnoisetest} shows the photocurrent measured has the noise level predicted by Equation \ref{eq:pd_chapter_noise_budget}. This means that the increase in photocurrent at a reverse bias voltage of 0.6V is, without doubt, an increase in quantum efficiency. At 0.6\,V reverse bias, for DC photocurrents ranging from 7.5\,mA to 15.5\,mA, the calibration peak and the noise level of the photocurrent were measured to ensure the photodiode was not saturating. Over this range there was a 2\% change in the ratio between the DC and AC measurement, this is shown in Figure \ref{fig:pdchapterfd10dacdc}. The ratio changed in the opposite manner to how one would expect if the photodiode was saturating; however, The SNR of this measurement is limited to the 1\% level due to the difference between the calibration peak and the level of shot noise and so this change is not significant.
\paragraph{}
Taking into account the light reflected by the FD10D's window, at 0.6\,V reverse bias, the quantum efficiency was calibrated using two other power meters. The internal quantum efficiency was measured to be 70\%. There is a significant amount of light reflected by the FD10D's window; if the window is not removed, at 0.6\,V reverse bias the quantum efficiency would be 60\%.

\section{Dark Current and Dark Noise in Extended\,InGaAs Photodiodes}
\label{sec:photodiode_chapter_dark_noise}
The dark noise of the extended InGaAs photodiodes used in previous quantum optics experiments \cite{Mansell2018a,Yap2019} resulted in the photodiodes being run without a bias, thus the dark noise of the photodiodes was a limiting factor in these experiments. The dark current and dark noise of extended InGaAs photodiodes depends on their reverse bias. The larger the reverse bias, the higher the dark current and dark noise.
\paragraph{}
There is a balance to strike between the quantum efficiency and dark noise. It is desirable to have as large a reverse bias as possible as this increases the quantum efficiency and saturation limit of the photodiode (see Figure\ref{fig:pdchapterfd10dqeasfnofbias}). The dark noise of the photodiode should be below the shot noise level of the light that will be detected. For the reasons discussed in Section \ref{pd_chapter_grav_wave_ifo_requirments}, the shot noise of a 10\,mA photocurrent was used as a bench mark to compare the dark noise of the photodiodes at 10\,Hz to. 
\paragraph{}
To measure the dark noise produced by the extended InGaAs photodiodes, a transimpedance amplifier with high gain was used. This circuit, shown in Section \ref{sec:appendix_dark_noise_tia}, allowed for measurements of current noise several hundred times smaller than the shot noise of a 10\,mA photocurrent ($6\times10^{-11}\,\si{\ampere_{\rm{rms}}/\sqrt{\hertz}}$). The photodiodes were placed in a light tight box while the measurements were being made.

\section{Excess Noise in the Photocurrent made by the IG24x500S4i}
The noise of the photocurrent produced by the IG24x500S4i exceeded shot noise by a factor of $\sim2$. This is shown in Figure \ref{fig:pdchaptershotnoiseproblemig24}. The set-up for this experiment is explained in Section \ref{sec:pd_chapter_reverse_bias_qe}. The photodiode was not saturating during this measurement as the 1111\,Hz peak had the right relative intensity. This excess noise was found at 1.3\,mA and 0.25\,mA of photocurrent.
\paragraph{}
To rule out bias noise causing the excess noise, a noise projection measurement was made. The transfer function between the voltage at the anode of the photodiode and the output of the transimpedance amplifier was measured while 1\,mA of photocurrent was being generated by the photodiode. A calibration modulation was applied to the bias voltage, and the noise at the anode of the photodiode and the noise at the output of the transimpedance amplifier were simultaneously measured. Using this method, 42.5\,dB of bias voltage noise was removed from the measurement; however, this did not remove the excess broadband noise. This is shown in Figure \ref{fig:pdchapternoiseprojection}.

\paragraph{}
The ideality factor of the IG24 was investigated. The ideality factor differing significantly from unity indicates that additional behaviour that was not described in Section \ref{sec:pd_chapter_diode_intro} occurring within the photodiode. The equation of a photodiode is 
\begin{equation}
I = I_p + I_s \left(\exp\left(-\frac{qV_b}{nk_BT}\right) - 1 \right),
\label{eq:pd_chapter_ideality_factor}
\end{equation}
where $I_p$ is the photocurrent,$I_s$ is the saturation current of the photodiode, $q$ is the charge of the electron, $V_b$ is the bias of the photodiode, $n$ is the ideality factor, $k_B$ is Boltzmann's constant and $T$ is the temperature of the photodiode. The current flowing through the photodiode as a function of forward bias was measured and is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:pdchapterforwardbiasiv}. The SciPy python function \verb|scipy.optimize.curve_fit| was used to do an unweighted fit of the data to Equation \ref{eq:pd_chapter_ideality_factor}. The photodiode was found to have $n\simeq1$. An additional check of Equation \ref{eq:pd_chapter_ideality_factor} was made by shining light was shone onto the photodiode. This is shown in Figure\ref{fig:pdchapterforwardbiasmodelwithlight}.

\section{Discussion}
None of the photodiodes tested in this Chapter would meet the requirements described in Section \ref{sec:pd_chapter_grav_wave_ifo_requirments} as the quantum efficiency is specified in all of their datasheets to be below 99\%. Communication with several commercial manufacturers of extended InGaAs photodiodes suggest that there is no off-the-shelf photodiode capable of meeting the requirements set out in Section \ref{sec:pd_chapter_grav_wave_ifo_requirments}; the results in Section \ref{sec:pd_chapter_reverse_bias_qe} and \ref{sec:photodiode_chapter_dark_noise} support this and give insights into the best possible operating conditions for an off-the-shelf extended InGaAs photodiodes. As discussed in Section \ref{sec:pd_chapter_diode_intro}, high quality extended InGaAs photodiodes are difficult to fabricate because of the lattice mismatch between the InGaAs and the InP substrate.
\paragraph{}
The results cover seven different models of extended InGaAs photodiode and so it is possible that another photodiode manufacturer (e.g~Teledyne Judson) have better performing photodiodes than the ones tested in this chapter. Additionally, as only two copies of most photodiodes were available for testing, batch-level statistics cannot be obtained from the results. For instance, the FD10D reported on in this chapter has lower dark noise than the G12183-010k; however, this does not tell one if FD10Ds are generally better than G12183-010ks. 
\paragraph{}
Section \ref{sec:pd_chapter_reverse_bias_qe} shows the effect that biasing an extended InGaAs photodiode can have on its quantum efficiency. The quantum efficiency of the FD10D was increased by $15\%$ when its bias was set to its maximum value. This increase in quantum efficiency occurs because the electron-hole pairs that are excited by the incoming photons spend less time within the depletion region, thus they have a smaller chance of recombining with another hole/electron. This motivates using as large a bias as possible as the quantum efficiency can be greatly increased.
\paragraph{}
The high amount of dark noise associated with the bias found in the Laser Components range of photodiodes (see Section\ref{sec:pdchapterlasercomponentssaturation}) meant that they had to be operated with low biases if they were to be able to produce shot noise limited photocurrents above 10\,Hz. This resulted in them having a low saturation level, see Figure \ref{fig:pdchapterlasercomponentsratio}. This is another reason why biasing a photodiode may be necessary. To avoid this saturation limit, one could split the beam and use an array of photodiodes to detect the light. However, better performing photodiodes could be used instead; the FD10D showed no signs of saturation, see Figure \ref{fig:pdchapterfd10dacdc}, when operating with a bias that fulfilled the noise criteria. 
\paragraph{}
Applying a reverse bias to a photodiode reduces its capacitance, however a few hundred picofarads of capacitance that photodiodes have at low bias voltages is a manageable amount to design a transimpedance amplifier around  for a DC readout scheme such as that used in the LIGO detectors. For instance, the photodiode circuit used in Section\ref{sec:pd_chapter_reverse_bias_qe} (see Appendix \ref{sec:circuits_appendix_variable_bias_ad797}) has a large enough bandwidth for a gravitational wave interferometer readout photodiode and was quiet enough to be limited by the shot noise of a 10\,mA photocurrent.
\paragraph{}
While the dark current does not depend strongly on the reverse bias when the bias is below the tunnelling threshold, the dark noise of every photodiode increased as its reverse bias increased. Although photodiodes with higher dark current tended to have higher dark noise, the dark noise could not be predicted from the dark current. For instance, the FD10D had a larger dark current but lower noise than the IG24x500S4i. The dark current tended to be greater in larger photodiodes, this is due to larger photodiodes being more likely to contain more defects. However, the Laser Components photodiodes break this rule as they were all of low quality. The Hamamatsu photodiodes with shorter cut-off wavelengths (less indium), had lower dark current and dark noise compared to the Hamamatsu photodiodes with longer cut-off wavelengths. 
\paragraph{}
The dark noise can vary considerably amongst a batch of photodiodes, for example there was nearly a factor of 100 between the dark noise of the two Hamamatsu G12182-010ks that were tested (see Figure \ref{fig:pdchapterg12182-010knoisecompare}). Whether this is due to their manufacturing or how they were handled prior to purchase is unclear, however this result shows that it may be worth buying a batch of extended InGaAs photodiodes and selecting the best ones from it.
\paragraph{}
The dark noise of a photodiode can be significantly reduced by cooling it. The dark current and dark noise of a photodiode increases exponentially with temperature. Photodiodes can be purchased with thermoelectric coolers, however integration of such a photodiode into a vacuum system was not investigated and could pose an engineering challenge. 
\section{Conclusion}
Extended InGaAs photodiodes are widely available detectors that can be used to sense $2\,\si{\micro\metre}$ light; however, off-the-shelf detectors do not fulfil the requirements of a photodiode that can be used in the readout of a gravitational wave detector. This is due to gravitational wave detectors needing high quantum efficiency photodiodes so that the shot noise of the signal is not significantly enhanced.
\paragraph{}
It is technically challenging to create extended InGaAs photodiodes with high quantum efficiency due to the lattice mismatch between the InP substrate and the InGaAs. While the depletion region of a photodiode can be made larger by running the photodiode with a reverse bias, the dark noise of an extended InGaAs photodiode increases when reverse biased. Therefore, dark noise measurements of a selection of photodiodes (see Figure \ref{fig:pdchapterdarkcurrenttermperaturealldiodes}) were made to investigate suitable operating conditions for them. 
\paragraph{}
To overcome the manufacturing challenges associated with creating extended InGaAs photodiodes, a wafer would need to be commissioned. Increases in quantum efficiency can be obtained by fabricating photodiodes to have anti-reflective coatings; however, the challenge of creating high quantum efficiency photodiodes lies in making it thick enough to absorb $>99\%$ of the incident photons. Additionally, the photodiode must have a low amount of defects if it is to have a quantum efficiency $>99\%$. Reference \cite{Moseley1986} shows results from a photodiode with 95\% quantum efficiency and a cut-off wavelength of 2.4\,\si{\micro\meter}, however due to the exponential dependence of absorption on thickness, it is still a significant challenge to reach $>99\%$ quantum efficiency. Future work may include investigating the growth conditions for extended InGaAs photodiodes as it has been shown that the dark noise of a photodiode made from extended InGaAs can be reduced by orders of magnitudes by optimising this.
\paragraph{}
InSb detectors may also be useful as they have near unity quantum efficiency at short infra-red wavelengths, however their quantum efficiency below 2\,\si{\micro\metre}  can exceed unity due to multi-electron processes \cite{Beattie1962}. An InSb device was tested, however its characteristics at 2\,\si{\micro\meter} are still unclear. To investigate these, the device's window would need removing and a 2\,\si{\micro\meter} laser would be needed. 
\paragraph{}
Alternative photodiode materials could be used to detect 2\,\si{\micro\meter} light. There have been suggestions that HgCdTe could be used to make a photodiode with $>99\%$ quantum efficiency at 2\,\si{\micro\meter} as HgCdTe does not suffer from strain related problems, however the linearity and dark noise of such HgCdTe photodiodes is unknown.
