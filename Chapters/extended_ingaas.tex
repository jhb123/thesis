\section{Introduction}
The current generation of gravitational wave detectors will be limited by the thermal noise due to the Brownian motion of the molecules that make up the mirror coatings. Since these thermally driven fluctuations are a limiting source of noise \cite{InstrumentWhitePaper2018}, proposed future gravitational wave detectors, such as the Einstein Telescope \cite{Punturo2010} and LIGO Cosmic Explorer \cite{Abbott2017e}, will use cryogenically cooled mirrors.
\paragraph{}
However, cryogenically cooled mirror coatings will not be compatible with the current laser wavelength, which is 1\,\si{\micro\metre}, and so future gravitational wave detectors may use either a 1.5\,\si{\micro\metre} or 2\,\si{\micro\metre} wavelength laser. 2\,\si{\micro\metre} is of interest as there have been promising designs for low noise mirror coatings that are optimised for this wavelength.
\paragraph{}
As InGaAs is a widely used semiconductor for detecting 1-1.6\si{\micro\metre} light, suitable InGaAs photodiodes were available for detecting the signal-carrying light in the current generation of gravitational wave detectors. These photodiodes are low noise, linear, can operate at room temperature, can detect tens of milliwatts of light, and have >99\% quantum efficiency.
\paragraph{}
As the current InGaAs photodiodes are not sensitive to 2\,\si{\micro\metre} light, `extended' InGaAs photodiodes may be used instead. These diodes are extended in the sense that their cut-off wavelengths are above 1.6\,\si{\micro\metre}. To achieve this longer cut-off wavelength, extended InGaAs contains a higher percentage of indium compared to conventional InGaAs photodiodes; however, this extra indium comes at the expense of the structural integrity of the lattice. This can cause the quality of the photodiode to be degraded. Previous experiments have been limited by the quantum efficiency of the extended InGaAs photodiode that was used \cite{Mansell2018a,Yap2019}. To obtain low enough electronic noise in these experiments, the authors of \cite{Mansell2018a} used the photodiodes without a bias -- this came at the expense of the photodiodes quantum efficiency.
\paragraph{}
Alternative photodiode materials that could be used include HgCdTe and InSb. HgCdTe photodiodes with quantum efficiencies >99\% are in the design stages of being made, however there are still manufacturing challenges to overcome. InSb detectors have high quantum efficiency\cite{Beattie1962}, however this material would only be suitable for wavelengths above 2.6\,\si{\micro\metre}.
\paragraph{}
In this chapter, the relationship between quantum efficiency, reverse bias and noise in off-the-shelf extended InGaAs photodiodes is explored. A selection of extended InGaAs photodiodes were tested with the aim of understanding general trends in the behaviour of extended InGaAs photodiodes. It was found that to get a high quantum efficiency and linearity, the photodiodes must be operated with a bias. However, if the photodiodes were used with a high bias, their dark noise could exceed the shot noise level of the detected light. The dark noise could be reduced significantly by cooling the diodes and so future detectors may feature cooled read-out photodiodes. Future work may include investigating the growth conditions for extended InGaAs photodiodes as it has been shown that the dark noise of a diode made from extended InGaAs can be reduced by orders of magnitudes by optimising this. However, at the time of writing (2020) and to the authors knowledge, commercially available InGaAs photodiodes for detecting 2\,\si{\micro\metre} light that are suitable for use as the read-out photodiode in a gravitational wave detector do not exist. 
\section{What is an Extended InGaAs Photodiode}
\label{sec:pd_chapter_diode_intro}
A photodiode is a square law detector and so it produces a current that is proportional to the power of the light it is detecting. Usually, they are of the order 1\,mm in diameter. They are two-terminal devices, with an anode and a cathode. Photodiode are often follow a p-doped,inrinsic ,n-doped (PIN) structure. PIN photodiodes are used in applications where there is milliwatts of light power.
\paragraph{}
The basic physics of a PIN photodiode can be qualitatively understood by considering a simpler PN diode. A pn junction  consists of two types of semiconductor: p-dopded and n-doped. In the P-dopded region, holes (the majority charge carriers) are free to move whereas in the n-dopded region electrons (the majority charge carriers) are free to move.
\paragraph{}
At the interface between the p-doped and n-doped region, majority carriers from each side diffuse across it. Then, the minority charge carrier combines with a majority charge carrier, and so no net current will flow out of the device. When in equilibrium, there are no free carriers in the p and n regions, and so these regions become charged. Due to the diffusion of majority charge carriers across the interface, there is an electric field between the p-doped and n-doped region. The region of the device containing no free carriers is called the depletion region.
\paragraph{}
When a photon is incident on a pn junction, it will free an electron-hole pair from lattice if the photon has sufficient energy. If this is photon is absorbed within the depletion region of the diode, then due to the electric field, the electron and hole will be separated. These electrons/holes will generate a current in the circuit connected to the diode. However, if a photon is absorbed outside the depletion region, then the electron-hole pair will not be separated and will recombine. These photons will not contribute to the current in the circuit. The quantum efficiency of a photodiode is the proportion of photons that are converted into photocurrent. The relationship between quantum efficiency and photocurrent is
\begin{equation}
I_p = \eta\frac{q\lambda_{\rm{EM}}}{hc}P.
\end{equation}
\paragraph{}
To widen the depletion region, an external voltage can be used. When a \emph{reverse bias} -- a positive voltage across the cathode and anode -- is applied to the photodiode, there is a larger electric field difference at the interface between the p-doped and n-doped region. The increases the volume of the photodiode in which absorbed photons will generate photocurrent.
\paragraph{}
To further increase the width of the depletion region, a layer of intrinsic semiconductor can be used to separate the p and n-doped region. An intrinsic semiconductor has no charge as it has an equal number of excited electrons and holes. An electric field will be present in a larger volume of material and so more photons will be converted into electrons which leave the photodiode diode.
\paragraph{}
InGaAs is a widely used semiconductor for making photodiodes. Typically, the indium content is around 53\%, sometimes this is made clear by writing $\rm{In_{0.53}Ga_{0.47}As}$, so that the InGaAs can be lattice matched to InP subtrate on which the photodiode is grown on. At this level of indium doping, the InGaAs photodiode will be suffer from few defects and so the diodes created with it will be of high quality. Typically they are sensitive up to wavelengths around 1.6\,\si{\micro\metre}.
\paragraph{}
Longer wavelength sensitivity can be achieved by increasing the amount of indium doping\cite{Martinelli1988}. These photodiodes are often called `extended' and can be sensitive for wavelengths up to 2.6\,\si{\micro\metre}. One of the main issues faced when creating an extended InGaAs photodiode is the lattice mismatch between the InGaAs and the InP substrate on which its grown \cite{Gendry1992} as lattice mismatches introduce defects.
\paragraph{}
Defects caused by the strain between the InGaAs and InP layers of the photodiode generate excess noise, dark current and reduce the quantum efficiency of the photodiode \cite{Joshi1993}. The diode can be made thinner to reduce this strain, although this reduces the quantum efficiency. The critical thickness, that is the thickness above which strain degrades the crystal, for extended InGaAs is of the order 0.1\,nm \cite{Gendry1992} whereas the absorption length (of the order of $10^4\si{\centi\metre^{-1}}$ \cite{Alam2007}) requires the photodiode to be several micron thick for it to have a high quantum efficiency that is required for the detection of gravitational waves (see Section \ref{sec:pd_chapter_grav_wave_ifo_requirments}). Another design challenge arises because InGaAs is not a good conductor of heat so a thicker amount of InGaAs can lead to lower power handling before the diode is thermally destroyed\cite{Li2004}. 
\paragraph{}
These defects can manifest as abrupt changes in the crystal structure known as misfit dislocations (e.g.~\cite{Uchida1993} \cite{Jurczak2017}). Defects produce trap states and these states cause the generation and recombination of charge carriers \cite{Zimmermann2003}, thus giving rise to 1/f noise \cite{Hooge1981}. Defects effectively increase the doping level of absorbing layer of the photodiode which leads to a decrease in quantum efficiency, so in order to obtain high quantum efficiency the defect level must be low \cite{DextquotesingleHondt}. For indium doping levels other than 53\%, buffer layers of InGaAs between the absorbing layer and the substrate can be used to mitigate the lattice mismatch \cite{Zimmermann2003}.
\paragraph{}
InGaAs that is optimised for 2\,\si{\micro\metre} has a refractive index, $n$, of approximately $3.6$ \cite{Alam2007}. Assuming a single quarter-wave layer was to be applied, the film would need a refractive index that was $n_f = \sqrt{3.6}$ and be 600\,\si{\nano\meter} thick. The idea of using this anti reflective coating as a passivation layer is presented in \cite{L_pez_2007}.
\paragraph{}
The dark noise and dark current of a photodiode has several contributors. In low bias regimes, the dark current is driven by the diffusion of minority carriers \cite{DextquotesingleHondt,Forrest1981}. However, the thermal generation-recombination of electron/hole pairs drives the dark noise at low biases \cite{Hooge1981}. Therefore, the dark current does not predict the dark noise, however as both of them increase with higher levels of defects, a diode with a high dark current will have a high dark noise. When the bias is large enough, electrons can tunnel across the the junction leading to a great increase in dark current.
\paragraph{}
The noise of the current made by the photodiode can be modelled as
\begin{equation}
i_{\rm{rms}} = \sqrt{ 2qi + \frac{4k_BT}{R_{\rm{PD}}} + \left(\frac{i_t}{f^{\beta}}\right)^2 }.
\end{equation}
The first term under the square-root, $2qi$, is the shot noise associated with the DC current $i$. The second term, $ \frac{4k_BT}{R_{\rm{PD}}}$ is the Johnson-Nyquist noise due to the shunt resistance of the photodiode, $R_{\rm{PD}}$; this term can become significant when the shunt resistance is low as it is in some extended InGaAs photodiodes. The final term is is Hooge's law of noise in semiconductors \cite{Hooge1981}; $i_t$ is the characteristic noise level and $\beta$ is usually $0.5\pm0.1$.
\section{What is Needed from the Read-out Photodiode in a Gravitational Wave detector}
\label{sec:pd_chapter_grav_wave_ifo_requirments}
The photodiodes used in the readout of a gravitational wave interferometer should have a quantum efficiency of more than 99\%. Losses within the interferometer decrease its SNR as they diminish the level to which the signal light is squeezed and so high quantum efficiency is required to detect the baseline level of $\sim$10\,dB of squeezed light that future gravitational wave detectors will use. Usually, if a bigger signal is required, the gain can be increased by using a larger resistor in the feedback path of the transimpedance amplifier or by using an avalanche photodiode, however neither of these methods would work in a gravitational wave detector as both would boost the noise and the signal in proportion with each other.
\paragraph{}
Current gravitational wave detectors operate in the 1-10\,mW range at the output \cite{Prijatelj2012} to ensure that the junk light from other modes is a small portion of the total signal. As  $q\lambda_{\rm{EM}}/hc$ is of the order unity, the photocurrent will be assumed to be $\sim$10\,mA and so any new photodiode should be able to produce $\sim$10\,mA of photocurrent without saturating. If heterodyne readout was to be used, then the DC light power could be reduced.
\paragraph{}
Photodiodes for gravitational wave detectors should have low $1/f$ dark noise so that the interferometers are not limited in their detection bands by electronic noise. Third generation ground based gravitational wave detectors will be quantum noise limited down to about 10\,Hz \cite{Harry2010}, so the $1/f$ noise must be below the shot noise of the photo-current above this frequency. For a 10\,mA photocurrent, the shot noise is $6\times10^{-11}\,\si{\ampere_{\rm{rms}}/\sqrt{\hertz}}$. Below 10\,Hz other radidly rising sources of noise will limit the sensitivity of the Einstein Telescope \cite{Punturo2010} and Cosmic Explorer \cite{Hall2020}
\paragraph{}
The $1/f$ noise could be avoided by using heterodyne detection, however a conventional heterodyne detection scheme would increase the shot noise level of the interferometer. Heterodyne detection can decrease the SNR of the interferometer if the mixing of the modulation and demodulation waveforms was not time independent \cite{Meers1991}. Although an optimal choice of modulation-demodulation waveform can recover the SNR by increasing the signal, the benefits of using squeezed light would be lost because the shot noise would be enhanced, compared to homodyne readout, by vacuum noise at frequencies that contain no signal entering into the measurement. However, it has been proposed that the increase in noise associated with heterodyne readout could be avoided by the use of a broadband squeezer \cite{Zhang2020a}.
\section{Off-the-shelf Extended InGaAs Photodiodes}
The quantum efficiency, saturation limit, dark current and dark noise was characterised in a selection of commercially available photodiodes. The photodiodes that were tested and their properties according to their datasheets\footnote{Datasheets often quote the noise equivalent power (NEP) of a photodiode. The NEP is the noise spectral density of the dark current generated by the detector divided by the responsitvity of the photodiode. For the Hamamatsu g12183 series diodes, the responsitivity is $\sim1\,\si{\ampere/\watt}$ and the NEP is $\sim10^{-12}\si{\watt/\sqrt{\hertz}}$, so the noise, as claimed by the datasheet, is $\sim10^{-12}\si{\ampere/\sqrt{\hertz}}$. This would seem to be sufficiently low to be shot noise limited down to $\sim1\si{\micro\watt}$. However, this number does not capture the behaviour of the photodiode at illumination levels of several milliwatts where a bias is required to ensure the diode does not saturate. As the noise of these diodes is not flat (see Appendix \ref{chapter:pd_appendix_pd_noise}), NEP should be specified as a function of frequency. The noise and responsitivity are both functions of reverse bias and so the NEP is a function of bias. Another quantity related the NEP is the specific detectivity; this incorporates the area and bandwidth of the photodiode with the NEP.} are listed in table \ref{tab:pd_chapter_photodiodes_tested}. According to each of the diodes datasheets, none of them have a quantum efficiency of $>99$\%; these photodiodes have quantum efficiencies around 80\%. However, the quantum efficiency of a diode can be improved by increasing the bias voltage (see Section \ref{}). Increasing the bias increases the dark current and dark noise of the photodiode and so for each diode a maximum reverse bias voltage was found. The benchmark used was the photodiode's dark noise at 10\,Hz; the maximum useful bias was determined by the voltage where the dark noise was equal to the shot noise of a 10\,mA current. The bias voltage also affects the saturation limit of the photodiode. If a diode was found to have a low saturation threshold ($\sim1\,\si{\milli\ampere}$) then it would not be fit for use in a gravitational wave detector.

\section{Reverse Bias and the Quantum Efficiency of an Extended InGaAs Photodiode}
\label{sec:pd_chapter_reverse_bias_qe}
To demonstrate the effect that reverse bias has on the quantum efficiency of a diode, the Thorlabs FD10D was used. The FD10D was exposed to a variety of beams so that saturation effects would be visible. Two styles of measurements were used: One where the reverse bias and the response of the diode were measured at DC, and a lock-in style measurement where the quantity $\frac{\Delta \eta}{\Delta V_B}$ was measured. The quantum efficiency should be independent of frequency if the diode is operating in a non-saturating regime, and so the two styles of measurements should yield the same outcome. To prove that the increase in signal is a result of increasing quantum efficiency an not some other mechanism, for instance an avalanche effect, the noise of the photocurrent was compared to the level expected for shot noise at a bias voltage the yielded an acceptable level of dark noise (see Section \ref{} for details.).
\subsection{Method}
To measure the photocurrent produced by the photodiode, a transimpedance amplifier that would produce shot noise limited signals for 10\,mA of photocurrent was used. Measurements of shot noise are needed to ensure that the photodiode's quantum efficiency increase is not due to an avalanche process. This circuit is shown in Appendix \ref{sec:circuits_appendix_variable_bias_ad797}. The photodiode circuit allowed for the reverse bias of the photodiode to be altered. 
\paragraph{}
A 1550\,nm wavelength laser (Pure Photonics PPCL300) was used to illuminate the photodiode (FD10D). The beam was split by a 50:50 beam splitter so that the light could be amplitude stabilised. The in-loop diode was a high performance InGaAs photodiode (C3061GH), and the out-of-loop diode was the diode under test. The laser was amplitude stabilised by actuating on the laser diode's current. Shot noise limited measurements could be made above 1\,kHz. A half waveplate and polarising beam splitter were used to control the power level of the light incident on the FD10D, and a lens was used to set the size of the beam.

\paragraph{}
The FD10D was exposed to a variety of different beams to investigate the possibility that current density related phenomena could affect the results. This was done by changing the beam size and beam power. As the FD10D is 1\,mm, the maximum beam width would be around $330\,\si{\micro\metre}$ in order for less than 1\% clipping losses. The powers used were in the milliwatt range as these types of beam powers are the ones that a gravitational wave detector would operated with. The methods in this section can only be used to measure relative increases in quantum efficiency, measurements of absolute quantum efficiency require \textcolor{red}{find ref \ref{}} additional apparatus.
\paragraph{}
To measure the effect that changing the reverse bias voltage has on the quantum efficiency of a photodiode, two approaches were used. By measuring the increase in photocurrent at DC it is straight forward to determined the increase in quantum efficiency. Alternatively, one can perform a lock-in style measurement. If the quantum efficiency depends on the reverse bias of the photodiode, then modulating the reverse bias, $V_B$ at a frequency $f$ will result in $\eta$ modulating at $f$, and so the measurement will be of $\Delta\eta/\Delta V_B$. The input modulation $\Delta V_{\rm{in}} = \Delta V_B$ and the output modulation $\Delta V_{\rm{out}}\cos\theta = \Delta \eta V_{\rm{TIA}}(V_B)$, where $V_{\rm{TIA}}(V_B)$ is the DC signal from the photodiode at a reverse bias $V_B$ and $\theta$ is the phase between the input and output modulation.
\paragraph{}
As 0.6\,V reverse bias was the highest bias that gave an acceptable level of dark noise (see Figure \ref{fig:pdchapterfd10ddarknoise}), the noise of the photocurrent was measured at a region where the noise of the laser was limited by shot noise. This was done to ensure that the increase in photocurrent was due to a real increase in quantum efficiency and not due to another mechanism e.g.~an avalanche process. The expected level of noise, $n$, of the photocurrent, in units of $\rm{A_{rms}/\sqrt{Hz}}$, is
\begin{equation}
n = \sqrt{2qI_{\rm{OOL}} + 2qI_{\rm{IL}} + n_{\rm{IL\,dark}}^2 +   n_{\rm{OOL\,dark}}^2 + n_{\rm{loop}}^2 + n^2_{\rm{analyser}}}
\label{eq:pd_chapter_noise_budget}
\end{equation}
where $q$ is the charge of the electron, $I_{\rm{OOL/IL}}$ is the current through the out-of-loop and in-loop photodiodes, $n_{\rm{OOL/IL\,dark}}$ is the dark noise of the out-of-loop diodes and in-loop photodiode, $n_{\rm{loop}}$ is the electronic noise of the loop and $n^2_{\rm{analyser}}$ is the noise of the analyser. By tracking the height of an intensity modulation in the light for a series of powers up to 16\,mW, the linearity of the diode could be investigated.
\paragraph{}
At this bias, the quantum efficiency was calibrated using two other power meters. The power incident onto the photodiode was measured and the power reflected by the window at small angles was measured.
\subsection{Results}
The effect of increasing the reverse bias on the quantum efficiency of the FD10D is shown in Figure \ref{fig:pdchapterfd10dqeasfnofbias}. There may be a saturation related effect at the 1\% level, beams with a lower intensity experienced a greater increase in $\eta$ than beams with a higher intensity, however the quantum efficiency may not be uniform over the diode -- near the edges $\eta$ is greater. The difference between the DC and AC response could only be described by a non-linear process. These effects are small, and Figure \ref{fig:pdchapterfd10dqeasfnofbias} gives a clear incentive to increase the bias voltage.

\section{Dark Current and Dark Noise in Extended InGaAs Photodiodes}
The dark current and dark noise of extended InGaAs photodiodes has been a limiting factor in previous quantum optics experiments \cite{Mansell2018a,Yap2019}. The dark current of these photodiodes depends on their reverse bias. The larger the reverse bias, the higher the dark current. The dark current of extended InGaAs photodiodes is high because of the relatively large amounts of dislocations due to lattice mismatch between the InGaAs and the substrate that it is grown on.
\paragraph{}
There is a balance to strike between the quantum efficiency and dark noise. It is desirable to have as large a reverse bias as possible as this increases the quantum efficiency and saturation limit of the photodiode (see section \ref{} or figure \ref{}). The dark noise of the photodiode should be below the shot noise level of the light that will be detected. As a bench mark, the dark noise of the photodiodes at \textcolor{red}{10\,Hz} is compared to the dark noise of 10\,mA of photocurrent. 
\paragraph{}
To measure the dark noise produced by the extended InGaAs photodiodes, a transimpedance amplifier with high gain was used. The photodiodes were placed in a light tight box while the measurement was being made.
\subsection{Room Temperature}
The dark noise as a function of bias was measured at room temperature ($\sim20\si{\degreeCelsius}$) for the photodiodes listed in Table \ref{tab:pd_chapter_photodiodes_tested} . The results for the FD10D are shown in Figure \ref{fig:pdchapterfd10ddarknoise}. The dark noise for the rest of the diodes can be found in Appendix \ref{sec:pd_appendix_bias_dark_noise}. Not all diodes had a simple noise spectrum; however, usually, the spectrum would fall as $1/f^{0.5}$. 
\paragraph{}

\section{Excess Noise in the IG24x500S4i}
The experiment explained in Section \ref{label} showed that the noise of the photocurrent IG24x500S4i was higher that predicted by equation \ref{eq:pd_chapter_noise_budget} by a factor of $\sim2$. The diode was not saturating during this measurement as the 1111\,Hz peak has the right relative intensity.
\paragraph{}
To rule out bias noise causing this, a noise projection measurement was made. The transfer function between the bias at the anode of the photoiode and the output of the transimpedance amplifier was measured while 1\,mA of photocurrent was being generated by the diode. A calibration modulation was applied to the bias voltage, and the noise at the anode of the diode and the noise at the output of the transimpedance amplifier were simultaneously measured. Using this method, 42.5\,dB of bias voltage noise was removed from the measurement; however, this did not remove the excess broadband noise. This is shown in figure \ref{fig:pdchapternoiseprojection}.

\paragraph{}
The ideality factor of the IG24 was investigated. The equation of a diode is 
\begin{equation}
I = I_p + I_s \left(\exp\left(-\frac{qV_b}{nk_BT}\right) - 1 \right),
\label{eq:pd_chapter_ideality_factor}
\end{equation}
where $I_p$ is the photocurrent,$I_s$ is the saturation current of the diode, $q$ is the charge of the electron, $V_b$ is the bias of the diode, $n$ is the ideality factor, $k_B$ is Boltzmann's constant and $T$ is the temperature of the diode. If $n$ differs significantly from $1$, then it indicates that additional behaviour that was not described in Section \ref{sec:pd_chapter_diode_intro} occurring within the diode. The current flowing through the diode as a function of forward bias was measured and is shown in figure \ref{fig:pdchapterforwardbiasiv}. The SciPy python function \verb|scipy.optimize.curve_fit| was used to do an unweighted fit of the data to Equation \ref{eq:pd_chapter_ideality_factor}. The diode was found to have $n\simeq1$. To verify Equation \ref{eq:pd_chapter_ideality_factor}, light was shone onto the diode. This is shown in figure \ref{fig:pdchapterforwardbiasmodelwithlight}.

\section{Discussion}
None of the photodiodes tested in this Chapter would meet the requirements described in Section \ref{sec:pd_chapter_grav_wave_ifo_requirments} as the quantum efficiency is specified in all of their datasheets to be below 99\%. Communication with several commercial manufacturers of extended InGaAs photodiodes suggest that there is no off-the-shelf photodiode capable of meeting the requirements set out in Section \ref{}; these results support and give insights into the best possible operating conditions for an off-the-shelf extended InGaAs photodiodes.
\paragraph{}
The results cover seven different models of extended InGaAs photodiode and so it is possible that another photodiode manufacturer (e.g~Teledyne Judson) have better performing photodiodes than the ones tested in this Chapter. Additionally, as a relatively small number of photodiodes have been tested, with only two copies of most photodiodes available for testing, batch-level statistics cannot be obtained from the results. For instance, FD10D reported on in this chapter has a lower noise than the G12183-010k reported on in this chapter; however, this does not tell one if FD10Ds are generally better than G12183-010ks. 
\paragraph{}
Section \ref{sec:pd_chapter_reverse_bias_qe} shows the effect that biasing an extended InGaAs photodiode can have on its quantum efficiency. The quantum efficiency of the FD10D was increased by $15\%$ when its bias was set to its maximum value. This increase in quantum efficiency occurs because the electron-hole pair created spend less within the depletion region, thus they have a smaller chance of recombining with another hole/electron. This motivates using increasing the bias of a photodiode as the quantum efficiency can be greatly increased.
\paragraph{}
The high amount of dark noise associated with the bias that was found in the Laser Components range of diodes (see section \ref{}) meant that they had to be operated with low biases if they were to be able to produce shot noise limited photocurrents above 10\,Hz. This resulted in them having a low saturation level, see Figure \ref{fig:pdchapterlasercomponentsratio}. This is another reason why biasing a photodiode may be necessary. To avoid this saturation limit, one could split the beam and use an array of diodes to detect the light. However, better performing photodiodes could be used instead; the FD10D showed no signs of saturation, see Figure \ref{fig:pdchapterfd10dacdc}, when operating with a bias that fulfilled the noise criteria. 
\paragraph{}
Usually, applying a reverse bias is done to increase the bandwidth of a photodiode, however a few hundred picofarads of capacitance is manageable amount to design a transimpedance amplifier around  for a DC readout scheme such as that used by LIGO. For instance, the photodiode circuit used in section \ref{sec:pd_chapter_reverse_bias_qe} (see Appendix \ref{sec:circuits_appendix_variable_bias_ad797}) was quiet enough to be limited by the shot noise of a 10\,mA photocurrent.
\paragraph{}
While the dark current does not depend strongly on the reverse bias when the bias is below the tunnelling threshold, the dark noise of every photodiode increased as its reverse bias increased. Although photodiodes with higher dark current tended to have higher dark noise, the dark noise could not be predicted from the dark current. For instance, the FD10D had a larger dark current but lower noise than the IG24x500S4i. The dark current tended to be greater in larger photodiodes, this is due to larger diodes being more likely to contain defects. However, the Laser Components photodiodes break this rule as they were all of low quality. The Hamamatsu photodiodes with shorter cut-off wavelengths (less indium), had lower dark current and dark noise compared to the photodiode with longer cut-off wavelengths.
\paragraph{}
The dark noise can vary considerably amongst a batch of photodiodes, for example there was nearly a factor of 100 between the dark noise of the two Hamamatsu G12182-010k tested (see Figure \ref{fig:pdchapterg12182-010knoisecompare}). Whether this is due to their manufacturing or how they were handled prior to purchase in unclear, however this result shows that it may be worth buying a batch of Extended InGaAs diodes and selecting the best ones from it.
\paragraph{}
The dark noise of a photodiode can be significantly reduced by cooling it. The dark current and dark noise of a photodiode increases exponentially with temperature. Photodiodes can be purchased with thermoelectric coolers but integration of such a photodiode into a vacuum system was not investigated and could pose an engineering challenge. 
\section{Conclusion}
Extended InGaAs photodiodes are widely available detectors that can be used to detect $2\,\si{\micro\metre}$ light; however, off-the-shelf detectors do not fulfil the requirements of a photodiode that can be used in the readout of a gravitational wave detector. This is due to gravitational wave detectors needing high quantum efficiency photodiodes so that the shot noise of the signal is not significantly enhanced.
\paragraph{}
It is technically challenging to create Extended InGaAs photodiodes with high quantum efficiency due to the lattice mismatch between the InP substrate and the InGaAs. While the depletion region of a photodiode can be made larger by running the photodiode with a reverse bias, the dark noise of an Extended InGaAs photodiode increases when reverse biased so dark noise measurements of a selection of photodiodes were made to investigate suitable operating conditions for them. 
\paragraph{}
To overcome the manufacturing challenges associated with creating extended InGaAs photodiodes, a dedicated wafer would need to be commissioned. Increases in quantum efficiency can be obtained by fabricating photodiodes to have antireflective coatings however challenges associated with creating high quantum efficiency photodiodes lies in making is thick enough to absorb 99\% of the incident photons. Reference \cite{Moseley1986} shows results from a photodiode with 95\% quantum efficiency and a cut-off wavelength of 2.4\,\si{\micro\meter}, however due to the exponential dependence of absorption on thickness, it is still a significant challenge to reach 99\% quantum efficiency.
\paragraph{}
InSb detectors may also be useful as they have near unity quantum efficiency at short infra-red wavelengths, however their quantum efficiency below 2\,\si{\micro\metre}  can exceed unity due to multi-electron processes \cite{Beattie1962}. An InSb device was tested however due its characteristics at 2\,\si{\micro\meter} are still unclear, to investigate these the device's window would need removing and a 2\,\si{\micro\meter} laser would needed. 
\paragraph{}
Alternative photodiode materials could be used to detect 2\,\si{\micro\meter} light. There have suggestions that HgCdTe could be used to make a photodiode with 99\,\% quantum efficiency at 2\,\si{\micro\meter} as HgCdTe does not suffer from strain related probelms, however the linearity and dark noise of such HgCdTe photodiodes is unknown.
