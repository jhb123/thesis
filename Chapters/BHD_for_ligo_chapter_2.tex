
Fundamentally, the optical layout in HAM6 must combine the signal beam and the LO beam and sense the combined beams to form a balanced homodyne detector (BHD). The implementation of BHD for LIGO A+ requires a complete redesign of the HAM6 layout. This chapter is comprised of three sections. Section \ref{sec:BHD_for_ligo_layout_requirements} is a description of the functionality and design constraints that all candidate HAM6 layouts must satisfy. In Section \ref{sec:bhd_for_ligo_chosen_layout}, the optical properties of the proposed HAM6 layout are examined. Section \ref{sec:BHD_for_ligo_WS_visulations} is an analysis of how mode mismatches between the interferometer and the OMC can be corrected by the Active Wavefront Control (AWC). Section \ref{sec:BHD_for_ligo_conclusion} is the conclusion for this chapter. This work was undertaken by a team made up of myself, Ken Strain, Russell Jones, Stephen Webster, Mark Barton and Eric Oelker. 
\section{The Constraints and Requirements for the Optical Layout Within HAM6}
\label{sec:BHD_for_ligo_layout_requirements}
This section will discuss the functionality required from the optical layout within HAM6 and describe the practical constraints imposed by the existing hardware and infrastructure. HAM6 must contain an optical layout that combines the LO and signal beams as well as providing steering and mode-matching between the interferometer and the OMC. There are a few properties of the HAM6 layout that cannot be changed such as the entry point of beams and size of the optical table. The main design challenge was achieving close to \ang{45} of Gouy phase between the optics for mode-matching, as this requires a lot of space relative to the size of the optical table. .
\subsection{Constraints}
\paragraph{}
As the constraints give a starting point for all designs, they will be discussed first\footnote{It could be argued that requirements are a constraint in a design. Here, the constraints are geometric properties which we have no freedom in, such as the input beam shape, the OMC geometry and the layout of the rest of the rest of the interferometer. A requirement, such as the desired minimum mode-matching, is a property which does not have a set `physical form'. For instance, while Gouy phase does correspond to a physical distance, not all 45\si{\degree} Gouy phase spaces are the same physical size.}. These include: where beams enter the HAM6 chamber, the size of the nominal mode entering HAM6 and the size of the OMC waist. The size of the HAM optical table does restrict the design, however table extensions can be made depending on where they would be needed. 
\subsubsection{Beam Entry Points}
%\paragraph{}
The entry point of the signal and LO beams in principle can be anywhere in HAM6; however, in reality they are fixed due to the location of HAM6 with respect to the interferometer; the location of the septum windows between HAM5 and HAM6; and due to the many other components of the interferometer that the signal and LO beams need to avoid. The LO beam is steered with BHDM1 onto BHDBS2, and the signal beam is steered with OM0 onto BHDBS2. These components are shown in Figure \ref{fig:bhdforligochaptersketchoflodelivery}. This is required to provide the degrees of freedom to co-align the signal and LO beams. There is a freedom in choice for the locations of OM0 and BHDBS2, but there is no choice in the entry points into HAM6 for the signal and LO beams.
%\paragraph{}
\subsubsection{Input and Target Beam Parameters}
The nominal beam parameter at the SRM is $q_{\rm{SRM}} = -3.55 + 1.09i\,\rm{m} $~\cite{AS_port_beam_parameter}, so the design of the HAM6 mode-matching optics is based on this number. As discussed in Chapter \ref{chapter:simulation_finesse}, the SRM beam parameter could differ from its nominal value. Chapter \ref{chapter:simulation_finesse} describes how geometry errors in the SRC cause the arm cavity and the SRC to be mismatched. This means that the radius of curvature and beam size at the SRM will not correspond to the eigenmode of the SRM, and the beam parameter at the SRM that should be considered corresponds to the arm cavity mode. This set of likely modes, shown in Figure \ref{fig:sim_chapter_beam_parameter_at_SRM}, was taken into consideration when choosing a layout for HAM6 since different candidate layouts may have been more or less robust to beam parameter errors at the SRM.  
\paragraph{}
The current OMC design~\cite{Arai2013}, with trivial modifications, will be used to remove junk light from the signal beam, so there is no choice in target waist size. The nominal OMC waist size is \SI{485}{\micro\metre}. The beam enters the OMC through a flat mirror, and the waist is located 140\,mm from this mirror. There is a negligible amount of astigmatism inherent in the design of the OMC. Mode-matching optics therefore have to match the nominal SRM and OMC modes.
%\paragraph{}
\subsubsection{Equal Path Lengths after BHDBS2}
It was decided that the two beams after BHDBS2 would have the same path length to reduce the cost of the HAM6 optics. This may also improve the common-mode noise rejection ratio as asymmetries may enhance the effects of noise couplings. To achieve high levels of common mode noise rejection, both paths should be as similar as possible. Only one path needs to have imperfections for the common mode rejection ratio to be degraded. For instance, it may be a bad design choice to have a path with a mirror whose only purpose is to fold the layout so that a slightly better readout platform can be used. However, it should be noted that the amount of common mode rejection required is related to the classical noise of the laser and its shot noise limit. Since the LO in LIGO A+ will be derived from the PRC it will already be stable \textcolor{red}{(shot noise?)}, so little common mode noise rejection will be needed. \textcolor{red}{ In addition to this, the layout will be in a clean environment with a low amount of residual gas; thus reducing mechanisms whereby imbalances due to path length difference can generate noise (may remove?)}. Therefore, the trade-off between mirror number and path length symmetry versus readout platform preference is not necessarily easy to evaluate.   
\subsection{Requirements}
The HAM6 layout is required to provide steering and mode-matching for the signal-LO beam into the OMC. The allowable mode-mismatch is $2\%$. The losses due to components in HAM6 is summarised in Table \ref{tab:HAM6_losses}~\cite{A+HAM6_design_requirements}. After BHDBS2, the layout must have a pair of mirrors separated by 90\si{\degree} and another pair separated by 45\si{\degree} of Gouy phase to provide orthogonal control over the beam pointing and mode-matching. %The 45\si{\degree} Gouy phase separation requirement makes the design of HAM6 a considerable task. 
\subsubsection{Mode Mathcing Losses}
The goal for mode-matching between the arm mode and the OMC mode is $>98\%$. Active mirrors, called Suspended Active Matching Stages (SAMS), will be used to correct for mode-mismatch; they will provide $\pm100$\,mD of optical power tuning range~\cite{SAMS_DesignRequirementsDocument}. Astigmatism degrades the mode-matching, and non-zero angles of incidence (AOI) of beams onto curved optics cause the reflected beam to become astigmatic (for a comprehensive description of astigmatism see ~\cite{Kochkina:2013bba}). This using a large AOI to increase the separation between optics in HAM6.
\paragraph{}
The role of AWC in HAM6 is to correct for thermal effects in other parts of the interferometer~\cite{BHD_ISC_requirements} such that the mode-matching between the arm mode and the OMC is maximised. Additionally, the AWC in HAM6 will correct for geometry errors in the layout after the SRM. Thermal changes in the interferometers mode may be corrected for by the Thermal Compensation System (TCS)~\cite{Lawrence2002}. In practice, operators and commissioners use each tunable aspect of the interferometer to improve its sensitivity to gravitational waves. For instance, at LLO it has been found that TCS can be used to decrease laser intensity noise coupling to DARM~\cite{Blair_email_2020}. Therefore, TCS may not be able to correct thermally-driven mode errors. The AWC in HAM6 may also be used for correcting geometry errors in the SRC. The optical layout and AWC functionality in HAM6 were designed with this flexibility in mind. 
\subsubsection{Gouy Phase Considerations}
While in principle only two curved mirrors in each path to the OMC would be required to mode-match the beam from the interferometer to the OMC, even a single mirror may be able to do that, a lens in the OFI will be used to provide further Gouy phase separation between the two active mode-matching mirrors in each path after BHDBS2. An active lens was considered here, but concerns of OFI assembly heating~\cite{A+_Output_Faraday_Isolator_Final_Design} and the unavailability of flint glass meant that this idea was discarded. As well as this, the range of optical powers for the SAMS is large enough that it would be an over-design to implement an active lens in the OFI.
\paragraph{}
To provide orthogonal mode-matching control, the two SAMS should be separated by \ang{45}. This Gouy phase separation requirement gives us a method of generating candidate designs, as this can be interpreted as needing the mode-matching mirrors to be as far apart as possible from each other on the HAM table. As the Rayleigh range of the beams is on the order of one meter, and most Gouy phase is accumulated within one Rayleigh range either side of a beam waist, a beam will have to travel a significant distance across the table before it acquires sufficient Gouy phase.
\paragraph{}
Additionally, to provide orthogonal alignment control for each beam path after BHDBS2, two of the mirrors in each of the said beam paths are required to be separated by \ang{90} of Gouy phase. For all designs, the two mirrors for alignment were always immediately after BHDBS2 and immediately before the OMC. It was found that placing the first mirror close to BHDBS2 often provided good layouts. 
\subsubsection{Readout Platform Suspension and Auxillary Components}
%\paragraph{}
The OMCs are expected to be the largest contributor of back-scatter from HAM6 to the interferometer~\cite{BHD_ISC_requirements,Arai2013}. By having the OMCs after the signal and LO beams are combined and by having the OFI protect the interferometer, the requirement on the motion of the OMCs is reduced compared to if they were before BHDBS2. Nevertheless, a single stage suspended platform is required. This is known as the readout platform suspension (RPS). Both OMCs will need to be on this platform, meaning all designs will need to have the OMCs fairly close to each other. Too large an RPS is mechanically infeasible as it will reduce space for other auxiliary components.
\paragraph{}
Additional sensors in HAM6 are required for controlling various degrees of freedom within the layout. Some of these degrees of freedom will be monitored using beams which are picked off from the main beam path, so additional mirrors will be required to steer these beams around the chamber. A set of smaller optics will be installed to provide diagnostic beams which can be imaged with CCDs. A shutter will be required to protect the hardware in HAM6 from being damaged by the large amounts of light that can potentially be transmitted through the SRM. Appendix \ref{app:bhd_for_ligo_sensing_and_control} describes how the longitudinal and pointing degrees of freedom associated with BHD may be sensed and controlled \textcolor{red}{May remove the appendix and refer to stephens document, although it is nice to have all this information in the same book}.
\subsection{Summary}
In summary, the main constraints for the design of HAM6 are where the beams enter the chamber; the size of the optical bench; the input mode and the OMC design. There must be low amounts of loss; therefore, layouts with large AOI onto curved mirrors should be avoided. The optical layout within HAM6 constitutes a nontrivial design task as it is desirable to have orthogonal control over the mode-matching and beam direction. This requires the optical axis to be of a similar length as the maximum spacing permitted by a HAM table. In addition to this, the HAM6 layout needs to leave space for all the other sensors and beams used for control. This means that all designs will use nearly all of the table space.

\label{sec:bhd_for_ligo_chosen_layout}
The design that is being taken forward at the time of writing is shown in Figure \ref{fig:bhdforligochapterlayout5f}. Optically, there are many layouts which could have satisfied the requirements and constraints laid out in Section \ref{sec:BHD_for_ligo_layout_requirements} . The choice of layout for HAM6 was ultimately determined by mechanical factors such as table space, number of suspensions and preference of RPS design. The optical properties of this layout are summarised in Table \ref{tab:layout_parameters}. The mirrors OMA1, OMB1, OMA2, OMB2 etc.~will be referred to as OMx1 and OMx2 as, usually, it is not important to make a distinction between the A and B paths.
\paragraph{}
The beam size and Gouy phase as a function of position along the optical axis is shown in Figure \ref{fig:bhdforligochapterbeamshape}. Significant points along the optical axis are also shown in this figure. The size of the beam at the final mode-matching optic matches the size of the target mode, as the size of the beam cannot be changed at a mirrors surface. The beam is always less than 1.2\,mm within HAM6, an important fact to remember when considering how close structures can be to the beam path.
\paragraph{}
The astigmatism loss was calculated to be $<0.05\%$; for detail in this type of calculation, see Appendix \ref{app:mode_matching_algorithm_derivation}. This astigmatism loss is plotted as a function of angle in Figure \ref{fig:bhdforligochapterastigmatism}; above \ang{10} the loss becomes significant. In reality the IFO and OMC will also have a small amount astigmatism so this calculation will not be entirely correct, however this effect is negligible compared to other losses. Losses of $<0.05\%$ are considered unimportant, especially when compared to the other expected losses, some of which are expected to be at the many percent level, in the readout of the interferometer.
\paragraph{}
The focal lengths of the OFI lens, OMx1 and OMx2 where optimised so that there was as close to \ang{45} Gouy phase separation between OMx1 and OMx2 as possible. This is shown in Figure \ref{fig:bhdforligochapterlenschoice}. With the layout shown in Figure \ref{fig:bhdforligochapterlayout5f}, it is possible to achieve \ang{43} Gouy phase separation between OMx1 and OMx2. Other layouts could provide exactly \ang{45} Gouy phase separation; however, this layout offers many practical benefits, mainly in relation to the RPS, over the other considered designs that outweigh an additional \ang{2} of Gouy phase separation between OMx1 and OMx2. This layout also provides good Gouy phase separation for alignment control, with there being \ang{88} between OMxS and OMx2.
\paragraph{}
In conclusion, the proposed HAM6 layout fulfils the criteria set in Section \ref{sec:BHD_for_ligo_layout_requirements}. It is shown in Figure \ref{fig:bhdforligochapterlayout5f}. Near optimal mode-matching and alignment control can be achieved with this layout. A summary of the properties of the layout can be found in Table \ref{tab:layout_parameters}. \section{Visitations of the Robustness of the A+ HAM6 Layout to Mode Mismatch}
\label{sec:BHD_for_ligo_WS_visulations}
It is expected that there will be some mismatch between the beam from the interferometer and the OMC due to geometry errors within the interferometer. Additionally, the thermal state of the interferometer may affect the arm mode beam parameter at the SRM. To visualise the effectiveness of the mode-matching control provided by the layout's active optics, it is useful to consider a 2D space representing Gaussian beams. The region of this space that can be mode-matched by the layout's mode-matching optics and a distribution of modes that those optics may have to correct for can be compared to gain insight into the effectiveness of the layout. It was found that the layout's active mode-matching optics will cover the vast majority of expected input beams.
\paragraph{}
The mode-matching between two laser beam modes, or a laser mode and a cavity mode, describes how similar those two modes are. In general, a combination of misalignment and differing beam shape can be considered to be a mode-mismatch. However, mode-matching typically refers to the overlap of the two modes if they were co-aligned, thus it is a measure of how similar in shape the two modes are. 
\subsection{A Description of Mode-Matching in Terms of HG Modes}
Mode-matching is usually, to first approximation, thought of as a difference in the beam's waist size and the beam's waist location. First order mode-mismatch can be modelled as light scattering from the beam's, or cavity's, fundamental mode into its $\rm{HG}_{20} + \rm{HG}_{02}$ mode. If a cavity is designed such that the fundamental mode will be resonant while the $\rm{HG}_{20} + \rm{HG}_{02}$ mode will be reflected then the effect of mode-mismatch between the cavity and a signal carrying beam is to decrease the signal light that is transmitted by the cavity; since, some of the signal carrying light will be scattered into the reflected $\rm{HG}_{20} + \rm{HG}_{02}$ mode. Sensing the $\rm{HG}_{20} + \rm{HG}_{02}$ mode therefore gives a measure of the mode-matching between two lasers.
\paragraph{}
A change in waist size is equivalent to an in-phase coupling between the fundamental mode and the $\rm{HG}_{20} + \rm{HG}_{02}$, whereas a change in waist location is a coupling to the $\rm{HG}_{20} + \rm{HG}_{02}$ which is \ang{90} out of phase with the fundamental mode~\cite{Morrison1994}. As the $\rm{HG}_{20} + \rm{HG}_{02}$ acquires $2\phi$ compared to the fundamental mode, where $\phi$ is the Gouy phase acquired by the fundamental mode, spacing beam curvature actuators at \ang{45} will give an orthogonal basis for control over mode-matching. This means that the beam size and beam waist, or two combinations of them that are orthogonal, can be independently controlled.
\subsection{Calculating Mode-Matching Using The Gaussian Beam Parameter}
Two perfectly mode-matched beams will have identical beam parameters at the same point along the optical axis. The overlap integral between two fields, $E_1$ and $E_2$,
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{M} = \frac{\left|\int_{A} E_1^*E_2 dA \right|}{\int_{A} \left|E_1 \right|^2 dA \int_{A} \left|E_2 \right|^2 dA},
\label{eq:bhd_for_ligo_mode_matching_general}
\end{equation}
can be used to determine the amount of mode-matching, $\mathcal{M}$, between two astigmatic modes. Solving this integral gives
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{M} =\frac{\sqrt{\left(q_{x1}-q_{x1}^*\right)\left(q_{y1}-q_{y1}^*\right)\left(q_{x2}-q_{x2}^*\right)\left(q_{y2}-q_{y2}^*\right)}}{\left|q_{x2} - q_{x1}^*\right|\left|q_{y2} - q_{y1}^*\right|},
\label{eq:bhd_for_ligo_mm_loss}
\end{equation}
where $q_{1,2}$ is the beam parameter of the electric field $E_{1,2}$. This is derived in Appendix \ref{app:mode_matching_algorithm_derivation}. The solution to equation (\ref{eq:bhd_for_ligo_mm_loss}) is always $0<\mathcal{M} \leq 1$. Alternatively, mode-matching may be computed in terms of the amplitude of the higher order modes relative to the fundamental mode~\cite{Bayer-Helms1984}. The mode-matching can be calculated at any point along the optical axis of the layout as mode-matching must be preserved throughout it; however, it makes sense to calculate the mode-matching in a physically significant plane such as the input plane, a plane containing a curved optic or the plane containing the waist of the target beam.
\subsection{WS Space Visualisations}
A Gaussian beam can be described by its beam size, $w$, and defocus, $S$~\cite{Perreca2020}. A WS space plots a Gaussian beam in terms of these two parameters. The effect of focussing elements on Gaussian beams can be clearly seen in this representation of them. Because a curved mirror can only change the curvature of a beam, at the mirrors surface the beams before and after reflection must have the same size. The process of a Gaussian beam, described by $q_1(S_1,w_1)$, being reflected from a mirror with power $S_{\rm{m}}$ to produce a new Gaussian beam, $q_2$, can be written as
\begin{equation}
\frac{1}{q_2} = S_{\rm{m}} + \frac{1}{q_1} = (S_{\rm{m}}+S_1) - i\frac{\lambda}{\pi w_1^2}.
\label{eq:BHD_for_ligo_2_1_over_q}
\end{equation}
From equation (\ref{eq:BHD_for_ligo_2_1_over_q}) it can be seen that a mode moves in the $S$ direction when it reflects from a curved mirror. This representation of Gaussian beams has the advantage of showing the beam in terms of intuitive quantities. The downside to this representation is as the beam propagates through the layout, the path that is traced through the WS space by the mode is fairly complex. 
\paragraph{}
There are two other ways that a beam parameter could be graphically represented. Since $q$ is a complex number, it could be represented in terms of its real and imaginary components. This makes seeing the effect of propagation straight forward, but the effect of a focussing element is harder to visualise. Alternatively, plotting $1/q$ in terms of $\lambda/\pi w^2$ and $S$ produces plots where the effect of focussing elements and propagation are easy to visualise~\cite[p.680]{lasers1986}. Curved mirrors and lenses result in a change in defocus, and propagated modes follow circular paths. As these figures are being used to visualise mode-matching and the effect of actuators on the beam size (rather than $\lambda/\pi w^2$) and defocus, the WS representation was chosen.
\paragraph{}
To illustrate how actuators move a mode in WS space, consider two lenses with variable power separated by \ang{45} of Gouy phase. As the two lenses are orthogonal, the waist size and waist location of the mode exiting this layout will be orthogonally controllable. In a WS space the orthogonality of two actuators can be seen by the angle between the two vectors that represent each actuation. If the two lenses are orthogonal then the actuation vectors will be orthogonal. Figure \ref{fig:bhdforligochapterwssketch} is a sketch of the WS space representation for a simple optical layout consisting of two active lenses separated by \ang{45} Gouy phase.
\paragraph{}
The mode-matching requirement between the interferometer and the OMC for LIGO A+ is $>98\%$. It is useful to plot contours of the mode overlap in the WS space between a mode and a target mode. Note that these contours can only tell you about the overlap between a mode and the target mode; the mode-matching between two arbitrary modes can not be deduced from these contours~\cite{Perreca2020}. For example two modes may be mismatched by the same amount from the target in orthogonal directions in WS space. They cannot be $100\%$ mode-matched as these two modes are different from each other.
\subsection{Visualising how Active Mode-Matching Optics Expand the Area of WS Space Representing Acceptable Input Modes}
To visualise the effectiveness of the actuation provided by the active elements of the HAM6 layout, the region of the WS space at the SRM from which the target mode-matching space can be achieved was computed. To do this, first the ring of modes in WS space that is $98\%$ mode-matched to the OMC after reflection from the second active mirror, OMx2, was considered. If OMx2 had a static radius of curvature then the effect of OMx2 would be to move the ring vertically in the WS plane. However, the ring can also be stretched in the $S$ direction as OMx2's radius of curvature can be changed. This can be seen, crudely, by applying $S_{\rm{OMx2}}\pm\Delta S$ to the original $>98\%$ mode-matching ring to produce two new rings separated by $2\Delta S $. 
\paragraph{}
To generate the region from which the target region can be reached by OMx2, the target region should be cut by a line which intersects the ring at the extrema of the beam size. To maximise the amount of WS space covered by the actuator, the upper target region should have $+\Delta S$ applied to it, whereas for the lower region the actuation should be $-\Delta S$. This results in a region of WS space which is larger than the initial target region. All modes in the new region can be at least $98\%$ mode-matched to the OMC. The new region was then propagated to the first mirror, OMx1, where the same procedure can be applied to further expand the region. \paragraph{}
The modes from the SRM that can be matched to the target is shown in Figure \ref{fig:bhdforligochapterlayout5fmodessrmomcmode matchingsuccess}. To visualise how successful the layout is at handling the likely modes from the interferometer (this set of modes was investigated in Chapter \ref{chapter:simulation_finesse}), the interferometer modes were compared to the region of WS space containing modes which can be corrected such that they meet the $>98\%$ mode-matching requirement. It was found that almost all likely modes will be covered by the layout, as the modes near the edge of the interferometer mode continuum are more unlikely to occur than the ones near the centre. Its worth noting that the in Figure \ref{fig:bhdforligochapterlayout5fmodessrmomcmode matchingsuccess}, modes at the yellow end of the spectrum may not be physical as they correspond to an unstable SRC.
\paragraph{}
For all candidate layouts analysed there was no major difference in the amount of modes that could be mode-matched.  In principle, the requirement for having \ang{45} of Gouy Phase separation between OMx1 and OMx2 does not maximise the amount of \emph{likely} interferometer modes that can be mode-matched. It is be possible to shape the region of WS space covered by the layout to maximise the probability that the arm mode will be at least 98\% mode-matched to the OMC by the layout. For example, if it was found that arm modes were most likely to be characterised by an error in beam waist size at the OMC, the active optics should be arranged so that they can actuate predominantly on beam size at the OMC. However, choosing a layout which favours flexibility in one degree of freedom over the other may lead to unforeseen beam parameter errors being uncorrectable.
\paragraph{}
The shape of the pink region in Figure \ref{fig:bhdforligochapterlayout5fmodessrmomcmode matchingsuccess} is nominal; when the HAM6 layout is constructed the pink regions shape will be altered by geometric errors in OMx1 and OMx2. Nevertheless, Figure \ref{fig:bhdforligochapterlayout5fmodessrmomcmode matchingsuccess} is encouraging since it shows that the actuation provided by OMx1 and OMx2 is highly tolerant to mode-matching errors generated by errors in the SRC.
\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:BHD_for_ligo_conclusion}
The functionality required from the BHD layout was discussed, and a proposed layout that satisfies these requirements is shown in Figure \ref{fig:bhdforligochapterlayout5f}. The optical properties of this layout were investigated. By selection of the right combination of mode-matching optics, near orthogonal control of the signal-LO mode-matching and beam alignment to the OMC was achieved.
\paragraph{}
The effect that the AWC had on the region of WS space that contains modes which satisfy the $>98\%$ mode-matching requirement space was investigated. WS diagrams were used to represent how robust the layout is to initial mode-mismatch. Figure \ref{fig:bhdforligochapterlayout5fmodessrmomcmode matchingsuccess} shows that the AWC provides a huge amount of mode-matching range. This means that the layout can tolerate large changes in the beam parameter at the SRM.
