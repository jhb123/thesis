\section{Introduction}
On the $14^{\rm{th}}$ of September 2015, the detection of a gravitational wave emanating from two coalescing black holes signalled the beginning of a new era for astronomy~\cite{Abbott2016}. The information carried by a gravitational wave is undisturbed by the matter between the observer and the astrophysical body that is emitting them, thus it gives astronomers valuable information which cannot be obtained from the electromagnetic radiation emitted by that body. Gravitational wave astronomy also allows for direct observations of objects that do not emit electromagnetic radiation, e.g.~black holes. Therefore, it is important that large numbers of gravitational wave signals are measured to develop our understanding of the Universe.%, so it is imperative that gravitational wave detectors are made more sensitive. 
\par
However, gravitational waves are incredibly weak and the detection of them is a science in and of itself. This chapter will outline how a gravitational wave interferometer, such as LIGO~\cite{Aasi2015}, can be used to detect these signals and the fundamental noise sources these detectors face. While there are other important sources of noise, the suppression of quantum noise is crucial to achieving the sensitivity required to detect gravitational waves; therefore, quantum noise is the focus of this chapter.
\section{The Astrophysical Origins of Gravitational Waves}
\label{sec:detector_chapter_gw_origins}
Gravitational waves produced by inspiralling pairs of massive objects, such as \glspl{bbh} (e.g.~\cite{Abbott2016,Abbott2017b,Abbott2020b,Abbott2020c}), \glspl{bns}~\cite{Abbott2017a,Abbott2020d}, and a source which is either a \gls{bbh} or a binary consisting of a black hole and a neutron star (NSBH)~\cite{Abbott2020a}, have been detected. Every confirmed gravitational wave signal to date (November 2020) is summarised in~\cite{Abbott2020}. The waveform of the gravitational radiation emitted from these systems is known as a chirp because the frequency of the gravitational wave signal increases as the two objects get closer to merging~\cite{Abbott2016c}. The cut-off frequency is determined by the radius of the objects that will merge; for the signals which have been measured, the cut-off frequencies were between 100\,Hz and 10\,kHz. A typical gravitational wave strain amplitude, $h$, of these types of systems, all of which have a total mass of $10\rm{M_\odot}\,-\,100\rm{M_\odot}$ and are between 100\,Mpc to 10\,GPc away from Earth, is $h\sim\!10^{-21}-10^{-23}$. 
\par
Einstein's general theory of relativity accurately describes the gravitational waves measured with the LIGO and Virgo interferometers~\cite{Collaboration2020}. Gravitational waves are emitted from systems such as two inspiralling black holes because the mass distribution of the system is not spherically or rotationally symmetric (see e.g.~\cite{charlesmisner2017} or~\cite{Schutz2009}). As the coupling between mass and the curvature of space-time is weak, gravitational waves are only observable from systems that have large masses moving at relativistic velocities.
\par
In addition to binary systems, other astrophysical phenomena will emit gravitational waves. Asymmetries in pulsars~\cite{Abbott2019,Abbott2019b} and magnetars~\cite{Abbott2019a} will cause them to emit a gravitational wave with a frequency that evolves so slowly that it is essentially monochromatic. When the core of a star collapses as it goes supernova, a burst of gravitational radiation may be emitted~\cite{Sathyaprakash2009,Abdikamalov2020}. Burst signals may also be generated immediately after a \gls{bns} merges~\cite{Zhang2020}. Weaker sources that future gravitational wave interferometers may detect include: a stochastic background level of gravitational waves due to the merging of compact binaries from all over the universe\cite{Abbott2019c}, dark matter~\cite{Garcia-Bellido2017,Bird2016}, and sources that existed during the early universe~\cite{Regimbau2017,Cui2019}. %cosmic strings may give rise to a signal similar to the CMB.
\par
To improve statistical models of the systems that emit gravitational waves, the number of detections needs to be increased. To detect continuous wave signals, the \gls{snr} of $h$ improves with the square-root of the total observing time of the detector~\cite{Jaranowski1998}. With longer observation times, it is more likely that an exotic event will be measured. For these reasons, gravitational wave detectors are operated for year long time-scales with minimal interruption. These are known as observation runs. Between observation runs, the detectors undergo hardware upgrades. \section{Measuring Gravitational Waves with an Interferometer}
Gravitational waves are propagating transverse perturbations in the curvature of space-time, and can be split into a basis set containing two polarisations, plus and cross (e.g.~\cite{charlesmisner2017} or~\cite{Schutz2009}). The effect these waves have, in the local Lorentz frame, on free test masses on Earth is to move them by a small amount \footnote{In the local Lorentz frame of the beam splitter, for any feasible Earth based detector, the effect of a gravitational wave on the wavelength of the light is negligible compared to its effect on the positions of the test masses due to the strong equivalence principle: all non-gravitational laws of physics which can be expressed in a special relativistic form have the same form in a local Lorentz frame (e.g.~\cite{charlesmisner2017}). For a gravitational wave with a frequency of $100\,\rm{Hz}$ and a wavelength $\lambda_{\rm{GW}} = 3,000\,\rm{m}$, in the local Lorentz frame, the metric has the form $g_{\alpha\beta} = \eta_{\alpha\beta} + \mathcal{O}(h)$, where $\eta_{\alpha\beta}$ is the Minkowski space-time metric and $\mathcal{O}(h)$ is a perturbation of the order $\big(\frac{2\pi L}{\lambda_{\rm{GW}}}\big)^2h$. The Minkowski space-time metric is flat, so in the local Lorentz frame the metric is, essentially, flat and the gravitational waves cause the test masses to move by $\Delta L = hL/2$. Maxwell's equations are modified by a term that is of the order $\frac{\delta \lambda_{\rm{EM}}}{\lambda_{\rm{EM}}} \sim\! \big(\frac{2\pi L}{\lambda_{\rm{GW}}}\big)^2h$, and so if $\big(\frac{2\pi L}{\lambda_{\rm{GW}}}\big)$ is small, as it would be for an Earth based interferometer, then the effect of the gravitational wave on the light is much smaller than the effect of the gravitational wave on the test masses.}. 
% this only works because the space time interval for light is 0. 
% footnote https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=JwLZ_1joqC8
\par
To detect plus-polarised gravitational waves, the end mirrors of a Michelson interferometer can be used as the test masses. These mirrors are referred to as \glspl{etm}. A suspended mass will behave in the same way as a free mass when it is driven above its resonant frequency, so the \glspl{etm} need to be suspended~\cite{Aston2012}. A sketch of a gravitational wave interacting with a Michelson interferometer is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:detectorchaptersimplelayout}. The amount the test masses move, $\Delta L$, when they are at a distance of $L$ away from the origin of the local Lorentz frame due to an incoming plus-polarised gravitational wave of strain $h$ is 
\begin{equation}
\Delta L = \frac{1}{2}Lh.
\end{equation}
Interferometers are sensitive to arm length changes that are on the order of one wavelength, $\lambda_{\rm{EM}}$, of the light they are operated with, as they measure the light's phase. As a plus-polarised gravitational wave will move the test masses in anti-phase, the difference between the phase of light when it recombines at the beam splitter after reflecting from the test masses, $\Delta \phi$, is
\begin{equation}
\Delta \phi = \frac{4\pi}{\lambda_{\rm{EM}}} Lh.
\label{eq:detector_chapter_simple_michleson}
\end{equation}
$\Delta \phi$ is known as the \emph{differential phase}, and differential effects, such as differential test mass motion or light being back scattered into just one arm, result in the light acquiring differential phase. The wavelength current interferometers operate with, $\lambda_{\rm{EM}} = 1064\,\si{\nano\meter}$, was selected because test masses with low noise and low optical absorption and mirror coatings with low loss and low thermal noise can be fabricated for this wavelength~\cite{Harry2006}.
\par
The gravitational wave strain is converted into intensity fluctuations of light at the \gls{as} port due to the two light fields from the arms interfering at the beam splitter (see Figure~\ref{fig:detectorchaptersimplelayout}). When on the \emph{dark fringe}, the interferometer is configured so that there is a small, static offset difference in the arm lengths corresponding to a phase $\phi_0$. This results in a small amount of light ($\sim\!10\,\rm{mW}$) at the \gls{as} port for the signal light to beat with, and so the variation in the intensity of the light at the \gls{as} port depends linearly on the gravitational wave strain. This technique is known as DC readout~\cite{Hild2009,Fricke2012}. At the beam splitter, the electric field, $E_{\rm{AS}}$, emerging from the \gls{as} port due to the interference of the beams returning from the \glspl{etm}, $E_{\rm{y}}$ and $E_{\rm{x}}$, due to a differential phase, $\Delta\phi = \phi_0 + \frac{4\pi}{\lambda_{\rm{EM}}} Lh$, is
\begin{align}
E_{\rm{AS}} &= \frac{E_{\rm{y}}}{\sqrt{2}} - \frac{E_{\rm{x}}}{\sqrt{2}}, \\
& \propto \sqrt{\frac{I_0}{2}} \left( \frac{e^{ i\Delta\phi/2 }}{\sqrt{2}} - \frac{e^{ -i\Delta\phi/2 }}{\sqrt{2}} \right),
\end{align}
where $I_0$ is the power of the beam injected into the beam splitter by the laser. The intensity of the beam at the \gls{as} port, $I_{\rm{AS}}$, is 
\begin{align}
I_{\rm{AS}} & \propto E_{\rm{AS}}^*E_{\rm{AS}},\\
&= I_0 \sin^2\left(\frac{\Delta\phi}{2}\right),\\
% &\sim\!eq I_0\phi_0^2 + 2I_0\phi_0\Delta\phi,\\
 &\sim\!eq \frac{I_0\phi_0^2}{4} +\frac{I_0\phi_0}{2}\frac{4\pi}{\lambda_{\rm{EM}}} Lh.
 \label{eq:dector_chapter_AS_port_light}
\end{align}
\par
In the absence of a gravitational wave signal, the DC light at the \gls{as} port is related to the static offset by $\phi_0 = 2\sqrt{ \frac{\overline{I_{\rm{AS}}}}{I_0} }$, where $\overline{I_{\rm{AS}}}$ is the average of $I_{\rm{AS}}$. As we are interested in a fluctuating signal, the DC offset from Equation (\ref{eq:dector_chapter_AS_port_light}) can be removed, allowing for the signal at the \gls{as} port to be written as
\begin{equation}
I_{\rm{AS}} = \sqrt{I_0 \overline{I_{\rm{AS}}}}\frac{4\pi}{\lambda_{\rm{EM}}} Lh.
\label{eq:dector_chapter_AS_port_light_no_DC}
\end{equation}
Therefore, it is in principle possible to measure the differential motion of the \glspl{etm} due to a gravitational wave's strain by placing a photodiode in the beam emerging from the \gls{as} port of a Michelson interferometer.

\subsection{Using Fabry-Perot Cavities in the Arms of a Michelson Interferometer to Enhance its Sensitivity}
For a Michelson interferometer, the arm length which maximises the fluctuating part of $\Delta \phi$ is 1/4 of the wavelength of a gravitational wave. Gravitational wave signals from \glspl{bbh} and \glspl{bns} have frequencies of $\sim\!100\,\si{\hertz}$; therefore, the wavelength of the gravitational wave will be of the order $\sim\!1000\,\si{\kilo\metre}$. It is not possible to construct a Michelson interferometer with $\sim\!1000\,\si{\kilo\metre}$ arms on Earth.
\par
The sites that were available to construct the LIGO detectors allow for 4\,km arms. To reach the optimal arm length on a 4\,km site, the number of times, $B$, that the light bounces off of the \glspl{etm} needs to be increased. Numerically, $B$ should be~$\sim\!250$. This means that Equation (\ref{eq:detector_chapter_simple_michleson}) becomes
\begin{equation}
\Delta \phi = \frac{4\pi}{\lambda_{\rm{EM}}}BLh.
\label{eq:detector_chapter_michleson_with_bounces}
\end{equation}
\par
Fabry-Perot cavities are used to increase the effective number of times light bounces off the \glspl{etm}\footnote{Heriott delay lines were considered as a means of achieving more bounces off the \glspl{etm}, however due to difficulty in manufacturing mirrors which do not scatter light, delay line interferometers fell out of favour. In principle, both delay lines and Fabry-Perot cavities would perform equally well due to the Mizuno limit~\cite{Mizuno1995}.}. Cavities are formed by placing an \gls{itm} in each arm near the beam splitter. When a photon enters a cavity, it remains in the cavity for an average number of bounces that is determined by the reflectivity of the mirrors. Interferometers measure the difference in phase of the electromagnetic fields in the arms, $\Delta \phi_{\rm{EM}} = \frac{d \phi_{\rm{EM}}}{d \phi_{\rm{ETM}}} \Delta \phi_{\rm{ETM}}$, so when a Fabry-Perot cavity is near or on resonance, the Fabry-Perot cavity enhances the detectors sensitivity as the slope $\frac{d \phi_{\rm{EM}}}{d \phi_{\rm{ETM}}}$ is much greater for a Fabry-Perot cavity than it is for a mirror. This is illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:detectorchapterfabryvsmirror}. The exact way this happens is dependent on the frequency of the gravitational wave and the reflectivity of the mirrors, with the arm cavity acting as a low pass filter (e.g~ see\cite[Chapter 3]{Mizuno1995},~\cite[Appendix D]{Heinzel1999},~\cite{Meers1989} or~\cite{Bond2016}).

	\caption{Comparison between the phase of the light reflected from a mirror versus the phase of light reflected from a cavity as the position of the mirror/end mirror is changed. The orange line shows the extra round trip phase acquired by light in the arm if the \gls{etm} is moved one wavelength (\ang{360}) and the blue line shows the same thing for an Fabry-Perot cavity. The phase of the light reflected from the cavity rapidly changes as it moves through the resonance. The interferometers sensitivity is proportional to the slope $\frac{d \phi_{\rm{EM}}}{d \phi_{\rm{ETM}}}$, and so a Fabry-Perot cavity can increase the sensitivity of the interferometer.}

\section{Sources of Noise in Gravitational Wave Interferometers.}
As the strain induced by a gravitational wave causes a minuscule change in the phase accumulated by light in the arm cavities, suppressing the noise below this signal is the main obstacle faced when trying to measure a gravitational wave. From Equation (\ref{eq:detector_chapter_michleson_with_bounces}), for a gravitational wave strain of $h_{\rm{rms}} \sim\! 10^{-23}$ interacting with LIGO, the beams interfering at the beam splitter will have a difference in phase of $\sim\!10^{-11}\,\si{\radian}$. 
\subsection{Laser Power and Shot Noise}
\label{sec:detector_chapter_shot_noise}
The shot noise associated with detecting a coherent state, such as that created by the laser, can limit the sensitivity of the interferometer. To reduce the level of shot noise, the energy, $E$, of the laser needs to be high. The uncertainty in energy, $\Delta E$, that is associated with a measurement of the time between two events, $\Delta T$, is determined by the time-energy uncertainty principle, $\Delta E \Delta T\geq \hbar/2$. The uncertainty in the phase, $\Delta \phi$, of a light beam with angular frequency $\omega_{EM}$ is related to the measurement time by $\Delta \phi = \omega_{EM} \Delta T$~\cite{Braginsky2000}. The phase is averaged over a time corresponding to half the gravitational wave's period, so $\Delta T = \frac{1}{2f_{\rm{GW}}}$. The energy used in the measurement is $E = I_0\frac{1}{2f_{\rm{GW}}} = \hbar\omega_{EM}N$, where $I_0$ is the power of the laser and $N$ is the number of photons detected in the averaging time. As the laser is in a coherent state (e.g.~\cite{Caves1981,Danilishin2012}), the uncertainty in detecting $N$ photons is $\Delta N = \sqrt{N}$; therefore, the uncertainty in the energy used in the measurement is $\Delta E = \hbar\omega_{EM}\sqrt{N}$. Using the expressions for $\Delta E$ and $\Delta \phi$, the uncertainty principle may be used to obtain
\begin{equation}
N \geq \frac{1}{4 (\Delta \phi)^2}.
\label{eq:phase_photon_number_relation}
\end{equation}
%\begin{equation}
%	h \geq \sqrt{\frac{2\hbar \lambda_{\rm{EM}}}{\pi c}\frac{\Delta f}{\mathcal{E}}}
%\end{equation}
\par
The amplitude spectrum of the intensity at the \gls{as} port, $I_{\rm{AS rms}}$, measured by the photodiode over a time corresponding to a bandwidth of $\Delta f$ will depend on the amount of light at the dark port,
\begin{equation}
I_{\rm{AS rms}} = \sqrt{2\overline{I_{\rm{AS}}}\hbar\omega_{EM}\Delta f}.
\end{equation}
The conversion from the power of the light at the \gls{as} port to gravitational wave strain, $h/I_{\rm{AS}}$, can be inferred from Equation (\ref{eq:dector_chapter_AS_port_light_no_DC}),
\begin{equation}
\frac{h}{I_{\rm{AS}}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{I_0\overline{I_{\rm{AS}}}}} \frac{\lambda_{\rm{EM}}}{4\pi LB},
\label{eq:detector_chapter_AS_port_TF}
\end{equation}
and so the amplitude spectrum of the strain corresponding to the shot noise of the light at the \gls{as} port is 
\begin{equation}
%I_{\rm{AS rms}} = \sqrt{\frac{\hbar\omega_{EM}}{2I_0} \Delta f} \frac{\lambda_{\rm{EM}}}{4\pi LB}.
I_{\rm{AS rms}} = \sqrt{\frac{\hbar c \lambda_{\rm{EM}}\Delta f}{4\pi I_0L^2B^2} }.
\label{eq:detector_chapter_shot_noise_ifo}
\end{equation}
\par
Equation~(\ref{eq:detector_chapter_shot_noise_ifo}) assumed the frequency response of the interferometer to a gravitational wave is flat, however the arm cavities have a low pass filtering effect on the signal, and so the shot noise will rise as $f$ above the characteristic frequency of the arm cavity. This can be seen in the spectral densities of the LIGO detectors' noise (Figure~\ref{fig:detectorchapterdesignsensitivitycurves}).

\par
To make the photon shot noise be a factor of 10 below the expected gravitational wave signal, Equation~(\ref{eq:detector_chapter_michleson_with_bounces}) and Equation~(\ref{eq:phase_photon_number_relation}) show that, for a LIGO-like interferometer, the required number of photons to measure a gravitational wave with a strain amplitude of $h_{\rm{rms}} = 10^{-23}$ is $\sim\!10^{22}$. For a gravitational wave signal with a frequency of 100\,Hz, the laser power incident on the beam splitter would need to be $\sim\!1\,\si{\mega\watt}$. 
\subsection{Using Power Recycling to Reduce Shot Noise}
Single frequency continuous wave lasers with a power of 1\,MW do not exist, so to increase the laser power that is incident upon the beam splitter, a \gls{prc} can be used~\cite{Meers1988,Strain1991,Schnier1997}. When the interferometer is on the dark fringe, most of the light from the laser gets reflected by it. Thus, the interferometer is, effectively, a mirror. Drever~\cite{Drever1983} proposed that a mirror, known as the \gls{prm}, could be placed between the laser and the interferometer to form a cavity. A sketch of this is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:detectorchaptersimplelayout}. The total power impinging on the beam splitter would be increased by making wavelength-scale position changes, i.e.~\emph{tuning}, of the position of the \gls{prm} such that the light reflected from the interferometer and the light from the laser add in phase, i.e.~the reflected light power would be `recycled'. Current laser sources that can be operated for year-long timescales are capable of producing of the order $100\si{\watt}$, see Chapter~\ref{chapter:TnT_lab_work}, and so the power recycling factor needs to be $\sim\!10^4$. This technique has been implemented at LIGO~\cite{Arain2008} and other large-scale interferometers~\cite{Acernese2014,Aso2013}.
\subsection{Using Signal Recycling to Shape Quantum Noise}
\label{sec:detector_chapter_signal_recycling}
An interferometer's bandwidth depends on the amount of time that the signal-carrying light spends in the arms. Meers suggested that the signal light could be recycled in a similar fashion to how power is recycled~\cite{Meers1988}. The detection bandwidth can be altered by introducing a mirror, known as the \gls{srm}, at the output of the interferometer between the photodiode and the beam splitter. A coupled cavity, known as the \gls{src}, will be formed by the \gls{srm}, \gls{itm} and \gls{etm}. A sketch of this is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:detectorchaptersimplelayout}. The finesse of the arm cavities as seen by the light emerging at the \gls{as} port depends on the tuning of the \gls{srm}. There are two common modes of operation: \gls{rse}~\cite{Heinzel1996,Mizuno1993} and \emph{signal recycling}. Alternatively, the \gls{srm} can be tuned so that the interferometer is optimised for a narrow band of frequencies, e.g.~\cite{Ganapathy2020}. 
\par
When in \gls{rse} mode, the \gls{srm} is tuned so that the \gls{src} is anti-resonant, thus broadening the interferometer's sensitivity `bucket' at the expense of losing peak sensitivity. Originally, \gls{rse} was motivated by needing to reduce the thermal load on the beam splitter by increasing the finesse of the arm cavities~\cite{Mizuno1993}. However, by increasing the finesse of the arm cavity, the bandwidth of the interferometer is reduced. To retain the desired arm cavity finesse from a sensitivity point of view, the \gls{srm} can be made more highly reflective and tuned so that the finesse of the arm cavity experienced by the signal is lowered.
\par
In signal recycling mode, the \gls{srm} is tuned so that the \gls{src} is resonant. This deepens the sensitivity bucket at the expense of bandwidth. The trade-off between bandwidth and peak sensitivity comes from the conservation of energy. From an energy argument, the sensitivity of a detector to gravitational waves is determined by the detector's bandwidth and the energy within this bandwidth; so for the same amount of power at the beam splitter, this can be thought of as a fundamental limit to the interferometer's sensitivity. This is known as the Mizuno limit~\cite{Mizuno1995}.
\subsection{Reducing Shot Noise by Removing Light in Higher Order Modes with a Mode Cleaner Cavity.}
\label{sec:detector_chapter_OMC}
All light that is detected by the output photodiode contributes to the total shot noise of the measurement; therefore, only light which carries signal should be incident on the photodiode to enhance the \gls{snr} of the measurement. In an ideal interferometer, the light emerging at the \gls{as} port will be a beam that is defined by the mode that is resonant within the arm cavity. To minimise diffraction loss, and due to the reduction in purity of higher order modes due to mode degeneracy and imperfect mirrors~\cite{Sorazu2013}, the light in the arm cavity will be in the \emph{fundamental Gaussian mode}. However, due to optical imperfections, e.g.~astigmatism, and the \gls{rf} side-bands used to control the interferometer, the light emerging from the \gls{as} port contains light in higher order modes. This undesired light is referred to as \emph{junk light}. A mathematical description of Gaussian beams can be found in Appendix~\ref{chapter:Light_and_Gaussian_Modes}.
\par
To remove junk light, mode cleaners are used (e.g.~\cite{OMC_ligo,Prijatelj2012}). A mode cleaner is a high finesse cavity that strips away light in higher order modes and the control sidebands. The LIGO \gls{omc}~\cite{OMC_ligo} is a monolithic fused silica bow-tie cavity that has a round-trip of $\sim\!1\,\rm{m}$. As higher order modes acquire a different amount of phase as they propagate compared to the fundamental Gaussian mode, an optical cavity can be designed such that when the fundamental mode resonates, the higher order modes do not. These higher order modes will be suppressed by a factor proportional to the cavity's finesse. However, it is possible for some higher order modes to not be suppressed if their round trip Gouy phase is nearly the same as that of the fundamental mode; thus, the cavity is designed so that only higher order modes which carry an insignificant amount of light will do this. The control sidebands are attenuated if they are outwith the cavity's linewidth. The beam transmitted by the mode cleaner will be predominantly in the fundamental mode, and so the \gls{snr} of the signal measured by the photodiode will be maximised. 
\subsection{Radiation Pressure Noise and the Standard Quantum Limit}
Radiation pressure noise is a quantum effect that arises due to the uncertainty in the momentum that light imparts to the test masses when it is reflected from them~\cite{Caves1981}. These quantum fluctuations will give rise to a differential force on the \glspl{etm}, meaning that this noise couples as $1/f^2$ to the measurement of the positions of the \glspl{etm}. A fluctuating force, $F$, arises due to the beam's shot noise. If the power of the beam is $I$, the amplitude spectral density of this force is $F = 2\sqrt{2\hbar \omega_{EM}I}/c$, where $c$ is the speed of light. Thus, the amplitude spectral density of a mirror's position due to radiation pressure is
\begin{equation}
%\Delta x_{\rm{RPN\,Free\,mass}} = \frac{\sqrt{\hbar\omega_{EM}P}}{2\sqrt{2}mc\pi^2f^2}.
\Delta x_{\rm{RPN\,mirror}} = \frac{\sqrt{\hbar \omega_{EM}I}}{\sqrt{2}mc\pi^2f^2}.
\end{equation}
To reduce the effect of radiation pressure noise, the LIGO test masses are 40\,kg. The effect of radiation pressure noise on the LIGO detectors' sensitivity can be seen in Figure~\ref{fig:detectorchapterdesignsensitivitycurves}.
\par
As the relative shot noise decreases while radiation pressure noise increases as the laser power is increased, a balance can be struck between the types of noise. For a laser which produces a coherent state, i.e.~a classical laser, the \gls{sql}~\cite{Caves1981,Kimble2001,Braginsky1996} sets an optimal laser power for a given \gls{etm} mass which minimises the total quantum noise. In a Michelson interferometer, the \gls{sql} sets a bound that follows a $1/f$ slope. For a given power, the interferometer's quantum noise will reach this limit at only one frequency.
\par
The quantum noise of an interferometer with an \gls{srm} that is tuned to either signal recycling mode or \gls{rse} mode will also reach the \gls{sql} at one frequency; however, for an arbitrary detuning of the \gls{srm}, the quantum noise of the interferometer will drop below the \gls{sql} at some frequencies due to non-linear optical effects~\cite{Buonanno2001}.
\subsection{Coating Thermal Noise and Other Noise Sources}
There are many classical sources of noise to overcome in a gravitational wave interferometer. The most notable of these is the noise associated with the Brownian motion of atoms in the amorphous mirror coating layers of the test masses~\cite{Flaminio2010}. Current detectors are designed around the expected noise level that can be achieved with these coatings. This will be one of the limiting sources of noise in the A+ detector, as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:detectorchapterdesignsensitivitycurves}. Other noise sources include seismic noise from the motion of the Earth, Newtonian noise from pressure waves in the Earth's crust, residual gas pressure noise that alters the refractive index of the arms, and the noise associated with the interferometer's electronics (e.g.~\cite{Weiss1972,Buikema2020}).
\section{Reducing Shot Noise with Non-Classical States of Light}
\label{sec:detector_chapter_two_photon_formalism}
The two-photon formalism developed by Caves and Schumaker~\cite{Caves1985,Schumaker1985} can be used to describe how non-classical states of light can improve the sensitivity of an interferometer. In this framework, light is analogous to a quantum simple harmonic oscillator. A simple harmonic oscillator can be described by two orthogonal components, such as the momentum and position quadratures; for light, a convenient basis to use are the \emph{amplitude} and \emph{phase} quadratures. The amplitude and phase quadratures, $ \mathbf{\hat{X}}$ and $\mathbf{\hat{Y}}$ respectively, defined in terms of annihilation and creation operators of the electromagnetic field, $\hat{a}$ and $\hat{a}^\dagger$, are
\begin{align}
\mathbf{\hat{X}}(t) &= \frac{1}{2}\left(\hat{a}(t) + \hat{a}^\dagger(t)\right),\\
\mathbf{\hat{Y}}(t) &= \frac{1}{2i}\left(\hat{a}(t) - \hat{a}^\dagger(t)\right).
\end{align}
From this formalism, an uncertainty relation between the phase and amplitude quadratures can be derived:
\begin{equation}
\Delta\mathbf{\hat{X}}\Delta\mathbf{\hat{Y}}\geq \frac{1}{2}.
\end{equation}
If the uncertainty in one quadrature is reduced, the uncertainty in the other is increased. Such a light field is \emph{squeezed}. The phase of the squeezed field which has the minimum uncertainty is defined as the squeezing angle.
\par
As the gravitational wave signals will be in the phase quadrature of the light, it would be advantageous to, by the uncertainty principle, move noise to the undetected amplitude quadrature from the detected phase quadrature; this can be achieved by using squeezed light. Squeezed light is created with an optical parametric amplifier; this is a device which generates correlated photons by converting single higher energy photons into two lower energy photons. This squeezed vacuum light is injected into the interferometer from the \gls{as} port so that the observed noise within the interferometer is squeezed. This technique has been implemented at \gls{llo}~\cite{Tse2019} and GEO600~\cite{Lough2020}.
\par
However, fluctuations in the amplitude quadrature of the light perturb the position of the test masses; thus, they couple to the signal at the \gls{as} port. During LIGO's third observation run, the squeezing angle had to be tuned to prevent radiation pressure noise from limiting the \gls{bns} range of the detector~\cite{Tse2019}. 
\par
To infer the quantum state of a light field, a technique known as \gls{bhd} can be used to observe one quadrature of the light field~\cite{Walls1983}. By combining the signal light field that is to be measured with a much stronger \gls{lo} light field on a beam splitter, the quantum state of the signal light field is encoded in the combined beams' intensity. The relative phase between the signal and local oscillator determines the quadrature of the quantum state that will be observed. Chapter~\ref{sec:BHD_for_ligo_what_is_bhd} explores this in more detail. 
\section{Interferometry Techniques to Beat Quantum \\Limits}
\label{sec:detector_chapter_beating_IFO_quantum_limit}
Squeezed light and an effect known as ponderomotive squeezing~\cite{Kimble2001}, can be used to surpass the \gls{sql} at some frequencies. Ponderomotive squeezing arises due to the natural correlations between the phase and amplitude quadratures of the light created by the interferometer. A demonstration of the \gls{sql} being surpassed at the LIGO Livingston detector can be found in~\cite{Yu2020}. Although~\cite{Yu2020} shows the \gls{sql} was beaten at some frequencies, the noise at other frequencies was increased relative to if no squeezing was used. The LIGO detectors are operated for broadband gravitational wave sensitivity, but sub-\gls{sql} interferometer configurations could be used if detecting certain astrophysical sources with merging frequencies around the frequency at which the \gls{sql} is beaten was of interest. 
\par
To avoid the increase in radiation pressure noise that is experienced when squeezed light is used, a frequency dependent squeezer can be utilised~\cite{Kimble2001}. The light's squeezing angle can be rotated as a function of frequency. This is done by reflecting the light from a high finesse detuned \emph{filter cavity}. At frequencies within the linewidth of the cavity, the light reflected from the filter cavity will experience a phase shift and so the squeezing angle will be rotated; in contrast, at frequencies outside the cavity's linewidth, the light incident upon the cavity is reflected without experiencing any phase shift and so the squeezing angle is not rotated. At low frequencies, the amplitude quadrature can be squeezed and thus the interferometer's radiation pressure noise can be decreased. At high frequency, the phase quadrature can be squeezed and thus the interferometer's shot noise can be decreased. A frequency dependent squeezed light source that is suitable for LIGO has been demonstrated~\cite{Komori2020} and frequency dependent squeezing will be part of the A+ detector. 
\par
Variational readout is a technique where quantum noise due to radiation pressure is removed from the measurement by using a frequency dependent homodyne angle~\cite{Kimble2001}. This is realised in a similar way to frequency dependent squeezing; the \gls{as} beam is coupled to a filter cavity and thus the detected quadrature of the beam emerging from this cavity is frequency dependent. This beam is then measured on photodiodes using a conventional \gls{bhd} scheme. Variational readout schemes are sensitive to small amounts of loss within the filter cavity, and so this technology is not mature enough to be implemented into current gravitational wave detectors.
\par
To beat the bandwidth limit, energy has to be injected into the system. Using white-light cavities as a means of increasing a detectors bandwidth have been studied~\cite{Bentley2019,Miao2015,Li_unpublished}. However, these interferometer configurations have yet to be experimentally demonstrated.
\par
There are several sub-\gls{sql} schemes which avoid the quantum noise problems related to making a position measurement of the test masses. If a momentum measurement of the test masses could be made, then a random imparting of `position' would not affect future measurements of the momentum~\cite{Braginsky547}. This is a type of quantum non-demolition measurement. As a speed measurement is being made, the displacement noise would rise as $1/f$ instead of $1/f^2$; this type of measurement can be made with a speed meter interferometer~\cite{Purdue2002,Zhang2018,Huttner2020}.
\section{The Effect of Loss Mechanisms Within the Interferometer on Measuring Quantum States of Light}
\label{sec:detector_chapter_losses}
Loss in the optical path from the arm cavities to the readout photodiodes results in less signal as well as more noise. The decrease in signal is straight forward to understand: if there are fewer photons to measure, then the signal will be smaller. For coherent light, the signal is proportional to the power and the noise is proportional to $\sqrt{\rm{power}}$, so the \gls{snr} is proportional to $1/\sqrt{\rm{power}}$; thus, loss results in a decrease in the \gls{snr}. 
\par
As gravitational wave detectors implement squeezing to decrease the quantum noise in the detection quadrature, it is important to preserve the quantum state of the signal field by having minimal loss. Real optical components have physical attributes that result in incident light power being wasted. These mechanisms are known as losses, and examples of these include absorption and scattering. A lossy optical component can be modelled as an ideal one downstream of a beam splitter whose splitting ratio represents the loss (e.g.~\cite[Section 2]{Danilishin2012}). The incoming light enters a beam splitter; some light is directed to the ideal component and some gets lost. In the remaining input to the beam splitter, vacuum fluctuations can enter. Some of these fluctuations will make it to the ideal component, and so the state of the light that is detected is contaminated with extra vacuum fluctuations compared to if there was no loss. The factor by which the shot noise increases , $\epsilon$, due to a simple loss mechanism, such as a non-ideal photodiode, is

\section{Photodiodes}
\label{sec:detector_chapter_photodiodes}
Photodiodes are used to sense the signal-containing light from the interferometer via the \gls{omc} (see Section~\ref{sec:detector_chapter_OMC})\footnote{In this thesis, unless specified, I will be talking about the `DC' photodiodes used to sense the gravitational wave signal. There are other types of photodiodes used in gravitational wave detectors, e.g.~\gls{rf} photodiodes; these are not discussed.}. A photodiode is a square-law detector that produces a current that is proportional to the power of the light incident on the detector. The photodiode's \emph{quantum efficiency} is the fraction of photons that are absorbed by the photodiode producing a photocurrent; a quantum efficiency less than unity is a source of loss. Photodiodes are usually operated with a \emph{reverse bias} -- cathode held at a positive voltage relative to the anode -- to widen their depletion region, thus improving their quantum efficiency and bandwidth (see Section~\ref{sec:pd_chapter_diode_intro} for more detail).
\par
Due to its bandgap, InGaAs is a widely used semiconductor for detecting 1-1.6\si{\micro\metre} light, and suitable InGaAs photodiodes were available for the current generation of gravitational wave detectors. The requirements for the photodiodes used in a gravitational wave detector are discussed in Section~\ref{sec:pd_chapter_grav_wave_ifo_requirments}. Usually, off-the-shelf InGaAs photodiodes are of the order 1\,mm in diameter to keep their junction capacitance low enough for high-speed operation\footnote{High-speed operation ($>100\,\rm{kHz}$) is not needed for a photodiode that is used to sense the light which contains the gravitational wave signal.} and to keep their dark current low. Photodiodes often follow a \gls{pin} semiconductor structure (see Section~\ref{sec:pd_chapter_diode_intro}) as this design can yield photodiodes with high quantum efficiency and fast response times. InGaAs \gls{pin} photodiodes can often detect up to 100\,mW of light power, and they can be operated at room temperature. 
\par
Since future gravitational wave detectors may need to operate with 2\,\si{\micro\metre} light (see Section~\ref{sec:detector_chapter_3G}), the current photodiodes may be replaced with devices capable of sensing light with a wavelength of 2\,\si{\micro\metre}. `Extended' InGaAs photodiodes may be used to do this. Extended InGaAs is an industry term for InGaAs photodiodes with cut-off wavelengths that are above 1.6\,\si{\micro\metre}. Based on conversations with four photodiode manufactures and distributors~\cite{Gasparian2018,Hornby2018,Miron2018,Kalinina2018}, it is unlikely that commercially available extended InGaAs photodiodes for detecting 2\,\si{\micro\metre} light that are suitable for a gravitational wave detector exist. As discussed in Chapter~\ref{chapter:2um_photoiode_chapter}, it is difficult to produce defect-free extended InGaAs, and defects in extended InGaAs photodiodes can cause the noise and quantum efficiency of the photodiode to be degraded. It may be noted that previous experiments in squeezing at 2\,\si{\micro\metre} have been limited by the properties of the extended InGaAs photodiode that was used~\cite{Mansell2018a,Yap2019}. In~\cite{Mansell2018a}, the photodiode was not reverse biased to minimise noise from the photodiode which would have limited the experiment; this came at the expense of the photodiode's quantum efficiency being degraded, and the total observed squeezing was limited by the photodiode's quantum efficiency. The characterisation of some extended InGaAs photodiodes is described in Chapter~\ref{chapter:2um_photoiode_chapter}.

\section{The Future of Ground-based Gravitational Wave Detectors}
\label{sec:detector_chapter_3G}
The next generation of ground-based gravitational wave detectors include the Einstein Telescope~\cite{Punturo2010,Hild2011}, LIGO Voyager~\cite{Adhikari2020} and LIGO Cosmic Explorer~\cite{Abbott2017e,Reitze2019,Hall2020}. LIGO Voyager will be a cryogenic interferometer installed in the current LIGO infrastructure and will be a factor of $\sim\!2$ more sensitive than LIGO A+. The Einstein Telescope and Cosmic Explorer interferometers will be ten times larger than the current generation of interferometers and will be able to: test the no-hair theorem, test alternative theories of gravity, test models of string theory, measure objects with large redshifts to learn about the formation of cosmological structures, measure closer to the big bang than is possible with electromagnetic telescopes, detect cosmic strings, probe the physics of neutron stars and measure what happens during a supernova. As well as this, the third generation of gravitational wave detectors may measure signals from exotic sources which have not yet been theorised.
\par
The current generation of gravitational wave detectors are limited by the thermal noise around 100\,Hz, see Figure~\ref{fig:detectorchapterdesignsensitivitycurves}, due to the Brownian motion of the molecules that make up the mirror coatings~\cite{InstrumentWhitePaper2019}. Since these thermally driven fluctuations are a limiting source of noise, proposed future gravitational wave detectors may use cryogenically cooled mirrors.
\par
However, the material that the test masses are currently made from, fused silica, has undesirable noise properties when it is cooled\cite{Schroeter2007} and so the future test masses may be made of crystalline silicon; crystalline silicon is opaque at 1\,\si{\micro\metre}\cite{Green1995} and since light will be required to transmit through them, this forces a change from a 1\,\si{\micro\metre} laser. Additionally, cryogenically cooled mirror coatings may not be compatible with the current laser wavelength~\cite{Martin2008,Martin2010,Martin2014}. Therefore, future gravitational wave detectors may use either a 1.5\,\si{\micro\metre} or 2\,\si{\micro\metre} wavelength laser\cite{Reitze2019,Hall2020}. 2\,\si{\micro\metre} is of interest as there have been promising designs for low noise mirror coatings that are optimised for this wavelength~\cite{Craig2019}.
%\par
%As the limiting sources of noise in current gravitational wave detectors is the coating thermal noise, research into cryogenic mirror coatings is underway~\cite{Craig2019}. Fused silica is opaque at these frequencies and its noise characteristics do not improve when cooled, so silicon test masses are being developed~\cite{Adhikari2020}.

Laser technology for 2\,\si{\micro\meter} light is still in its infancy, and so there is lots of uncertainty in the practicality of using 2\,\si{\micro\meter} as the operating wavelength of a gravitational wave detector; for instance, low noise, high quantum efficiency photodiodes that can detect 2\,\si{\micro\metre} light have never been fabricated. \section{Conclusion}
Further detections of gravitational waves will lead to a better understanding of the Universe. To reach the design sensitivity of the LIGO detectors, among other upgrades, the input laser power needs increasing. To further lower the LIGO detectors noise floor, the A+ upgrade will utilise frequency dependent squeezed light. For this to be successfully implemented, the quantum state of the light field must be accurately detected with a balanced homodyne readout and losses in the readout must be minimal. 
\par
As there is potential to reduce the noise floor that will limit the A+ detector by using cryogenic mirror coatings that are compatible with 2\,\si{\micro\meter} light, future detectors may use a 2\,\si{\micro\meter} laser; this leads to the question: is laser technology at this wavelength sufficiently mature to enable quantum limited gravitational wave detectors? \gls{bhd} and photodiodes with high quantum efficiency and low dark noise will be required for any future interferometer.
