\section{Sources of GW}
\textcolor{red}{order = physics of GW generation -> detected sources -> undetected sources. Try to emphasise the need to improve current detectors to detect the the undetected sources?}
Gravitational waves are emitted from mass quadrupole systems \footnote{Due to the conservation of mass/energy, there is no mass monopole radiation. Due to the conservation of momentum and angular momentum, there is no dipole radiation. The next term that can generate radiation is the quadrupole moment $h_{ij} = \frac{1}{R}\left(\frac{2G}{c^4}\ddot{I}_{ij}\right) $.}. As the coupling between mass and the curvature of space-time is weak, gravitational waves are only observable in systems that have large masses involved.
\paragraph{}
To date, gravitational waves emanating BBH, BNS, and, possibly, from a binary consisting of a black hole and a neutron star (NSBH) \cite{Abbott2020,Abbott2020a} have been detected. The waveform of graviatational radiation emitted from these systems is known as a chirp signal. These signals increase in frequency as the two objects get closer to merging. Due to the geometry of these systems, the frequency of the gravitational waves are in the audioband with the cut-off frequency, determined by the radius of the objects that will merge, between 100\,Hz-10\,kHz. A typical gravitational wave strain amplitude of these types of systems, all of which have a total mass of $10\rm{M_\odot}\,-\,100\rm{M_\odot}$ and are of the order 100\,Mpc to 10\,GPc away from Earth, is $h\sim10^{-21}$. 
\paragraph{}
Other sources of gravitational waves include pulsars and magnetars. Asymmetries in these systems will cause them to emit a gravitational wave with a constant frequency. Burst signals will be emitted by supernovae. There will be a stocastic background of gravitational waves, similar to the the cosmic microwave background radiation, as well as a background level of gravitational waves due to the merging of compact binaries. Dark matter and Cosmic strings may give rise to gravitational waves. However, the gravitational waves from these systems are yet to be directly detected.

\section{Measuring Gravitational Waves with an Interferometer}
\paragraph{general relativity (BIG L shaped IFO)}
Einstein's general theory of relativity accurately describes the gravitational waves measured with the LIGO and Virgo gravitational wave detectors \cite{Collaboration2020}. Gravitational waves are propagating longitudinal perturbations in the curvature of space-time, and can be split into a basis set containing two polarisations, plus and cross (reference gravitation).\footnote{In the local Lorentz frame of the beam splitter, for any feasible Earth based detector, the effect of a gravitational wave on the wavelength of the light is negligible compared to its effect on the positions of the test masses. This is due to the strong equivalence principle. This states that `All physical laws that are expressible as a tensor equation in special relativity have the exact same form in a local lorentz frame of a curved space time'. In the local Lorentz frame of a gravitational wave detector, the metric has the form $g_{\alpha\beta} = \eta_{\alpha\beta} + \mathcal{O}(h)$, where $\eta_{\alpha\beta}$ is the minkowski space-time metric and $\mathcal{O}(h)$ is a perturbation of the order $\frac{2\pi L}{\lambda_{\rm{EM}}}^2h$. The minkowski space-time metric is flat, so in the local Lorentz frame the metric is, essentially, flat and the gravitational waves cause the test masses to move by $\Delta L = \frac{1}{2}hL$. Maxwell's equations, due to the strong equivalence principle, are modified by a term that is of the $\frac{\delta \lambda_{\rm{EM}}}{\lambda_{\rm{EM}}} \sim \frac{2\pi L}{\lambda_{\rm{EM}}}^2h$, and so if $\frac{2\pi L}{\lambda_{\rm{EM}}}$ is sufficiently small, as it would be for an Earth based interferometer, then the effect of the gravitational wave on the light is much smaller than the effect of the gravitational wave on the test masses. Alternatively, in the traceless-transverse gauge, the position of the test masses is fixed and the gravitational wave changes the wavelength of the light.} 
\paragraph{}
The effect these waves have, in the local Lorentz frame, on test masses on Earth is to move them by a small amount. To detect plus polarised gravitational waves, the end mirrors of a Michelson interferometer can be used as the test masses. In a Michelson interferometer, light is split with a beam splitter and directed towards mirrors. These mirrors reflect the light back to the beam splitter where it recombines. When the mirrors at the end of a Michelson interferometer are moved differentially by a plus polarised graviational wave. This causes a phase modulation in the light in the arms, and the intensity of the light at the \emph{anti-symmetric} port is modulated at the frequency corresponding to the frequency of the phase modulation. By placing a photo detector in this beam, it is possible to measure the differential signal generated by the gravitational wave.
\subsection{Required Arm Length of the Michelson Interferometer}
The optimal arm length of the interferometer is determined by 1/4 of the wavelength of a gravitational wave. For BBH and BNS, the gravitational wave signal will be at 100\,Hz, and so the wavelength of the gravitational wave will be of the order 3000\,km. It is not possible to construct such an interferometer on Earth; the sites that were available to construct the current generation of gravitational wave detectors allow for 4\,km arms. Note that if the arm where to be made longer, the sensitivity of the detector would decrease as the phase difference accumulated in the arms would no longer result in maximum constructive interference at the antisymmetric port.
\paragraph{}
To reach the reach the optimal arm length on a 4\,km site, the number of times that the light bounces off the test mass, $B$, needs to be increased. Numerically, $B$ should be $\sim250$. Delay lines and Fabret-Perot cavities within the arms of the interferometer were investigated. Ultimately, due to technical considerations in the geometry of the mirrors required to implement a delay line interferometer, Fabret-Perot cavities were selected as a means of increasing the amount of bounces of the light off of the test masses. This means that $B$ is a statistical quantity, and the average number of bounces will be $B$. \textcolor{red}{more detail/less rotating hand theorem?}

The challenge faced when detecting gravitational waves is a result of the gravitational wave strain a causing minuscule change in the phase accumulated by light in the arm cavities. The phase shift, $\Delta \phi_{\rm{EM}}$, acquired by the laser beam of wavelength $\lambda_{\rm{EM}}$ when a gravitational wave with strain $h$ and with optimal wavelength for the arm length interacts with the detector is 
\begin{equation}
\Delta \phi_{\rm{EM}} = \frac{4\pi LB}{\lambda_{\rm{EM}}}h.
\end{equation}
The choice of $\lambda_{\rm{EM}} = 1064\,\si{\nano\meter}$ as the wavelength which the detector operates with is determined by the low loss and low thermal noise coatings and test masses that can be fabricated for 1064\,nm light. Numerically, for a gravitational wave of strain $10^{-23}$ interacting with LIGO, the change in phase shift that the light experiences is $\sim10^{-11}$. 
\paragraph{}
The required energy of the laser beam $E$ to measure the tiny phase shift that the light experiences due to the gravitational wave strain is determined by the time-energy uncertainty principle $\Delta E \Delta T \geq \hbar/2$. The uncertainty in time is related to the uncertainty in phase by $\Delta \phi = \omega_{EM} \Delta T$. The energy used to measure this time is determined by half of a the gravitational waves period (if the zero crossings of the EM wave are being used). This time $T$ corresponds to $1/2f$, where $f$ is the frequency of the gravitational wave. The power of the laser is $I_0 = \hbar\omega_{EM}N$, where $N$ is the number of photons detected in the time interval $T$, and so the energy used to measure the time is $E = I_0 T = I_0 \frac{1}{2f} = \hbar\omega_{EM}N$. As the laser is in a coherent state, the uncertainty in the number of photons detected in time $T$ is $\Delta N = \sqrt{N}$. This is known as shot noise. The uncertainty principle may now be written as $\Delta E = \hbar\omega_{EM}\Delta N$ which can be expressed as
\begin{equation}
N \geq \frac{1}{4 (\Delta \phi)^2}.
\end{equation}
To beat the photon shot noise by a factor of 10, the required number of photons to measure a gravitational wave with a strain amplitude of $10^{-23}$ is $\sim10^{22}$. For a gravitational wave with a frequency of 100\,Hz, the laser power required entering the arm cavity would be $\sim1\si{\mega\watt}$. No such laser exists.
\paragraph{}
To increase the laser power that is injected into the beam splitter, a power recycling cavity can be used. Gravitational wave detectors are operated on the \emph{dark fringe} meaning that there is a low amount of power at the antisymmetric port and most of the injected laser power gets reflected from the beam splitter towards the laser. This means that the beam splitter acts as a mirror. Drever \cite{bibid} proposed that a mirror, called the power recycling mirror (PRM), could be placed in the between the laser and the beam splitter and this would form a cavity. By tuning the position of the PRM such that the the light reflected from the interferometer and the light from the laser add in phase, the power would be `recycled' and the total power impinging on the beam splitter would be increased. Current laser sources that can be operated for year-long time scales are capable of producing of the order $100\si{\watt}$, and so the power recycling factor needs to be $\sim10^4$. This technique has been successfully implemented by many large scale interferometers. \textcolor{red}{Does this need expanding, or just some references to how PRC is implemented e.g. matching transmission to losses.}
