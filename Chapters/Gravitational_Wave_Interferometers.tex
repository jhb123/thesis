\paragraph{general relativity (L shaped IFO)}
Einstein's general theory of relativity accurately describes the gravitational waves measured with the LIGO and Virgo gravitational wave detectors \cite{Collaboration2020}. Gravitational waves are propagating longitudinal perturbations in the curvature of space-time, and can be split into a basis set containing two polarisations, plus and cross (reference gravitation). 
\paragraph{}
The effect these waves have, in the local lorentz frame, on test masses on Earth is to move them by a small amount. To detect plus polarised gravitational waves, the end mirrors of a michelson interferometer can be used as the test masses. In the local Lorentz frame of the beam splitter, for any feasible Earth based detector, the effect of a gravitational wave on the wavelength of the light is negligible compared to its effect on the positions of the test masses. \footnote{Alternatively, in the traceless-transverse gauge, the position of the test masses is fixed and the gravitational wave changes the wavelength of the light.}\footnote{This is due to the strong equivalence principle. This states that `All physical laws that are expressible as a tensor equation in special relativity have the exact same form in a local lorentz frame of a curved space time'. In the local Lorentz frame of a gravitational wave detector, the metric has the form $g_{\alpha\beta} = \eta_{\alpha\beta} + \mathcal{0}(h)$, where $\eta_{\alpha\beta}$ is the minkowski space-time metric and $\mathcal{0}(h)$ is a perturbation of the order $\frac{2\pi L}{\lambda_{\rm{EM}}}^2h$. The minkowski space-time metric is flat, so in the local lorentz frame the metric is, essentially, flat and the gravitational waves cause the test masses to move by $\Delta L = \frac{1}{2}hL$. Maxwell's equations, due to the strong equivalence principle, are modified by a term that is of the $\frac{\delta \lambda_{\rm{EM}}}{\lambda_{\rm{EM}}} \sim \frac{2\pi L}{\lambda_{\rm{EM}}}^2h$, and so if $\frac{2\pi L}{\lambda_{\rm{EM}}}$ is sufficiently small, as it would be for an Earth based interferometer, then the effect of the gravitational wave on the light is much smaller than the effect of the gravitational wave on the test masses.
